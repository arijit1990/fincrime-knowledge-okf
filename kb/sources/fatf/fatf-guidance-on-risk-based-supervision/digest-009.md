## 7.5.4. Ireland: Central Banks’s engagement with law enforcement and other agencies/supervisors

- **descriptive / method**: For its ML/TF Risk Assessment the Central Bank of Ireland meets An Garda Síochána (which houses the FIU), the Revenue Commissioners, the Director of Public Prosecutions and the Criminal Assets Bureau, and researches public information such as agency annual reports, crime statistics, predicate offences and seized assets. (p. 83)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Director of Public Prosecutions and the Criminal Assets Bureau (CAB)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: This engagement identified threats associated with sectors such as banking, money remitters and bureaux de change, which were incorporated into the sectoral ML/TF risk assessments. (p. 83)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "banking, money remitters and bureaux de change were identified and incorporated"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The Central Bank of Ireland incorporates information from the National Risk Assessment and Supranational Risk Assessment into its ML/TF Risk Assessment as needed to keep them aligned. (p. 83)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "National Risk Assessment (NRA) or Supranational Risk Assessment (SNRA) process is considered and incorporated"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: In assessing sector threats the Central Bank of Ireland also uses STR-related information from An Garda Síochána and Revenue. (p. 83)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "information available from an Garda Siochana and from Revenue in relation to Suspicious Transaction Reports"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The Central Bank of Ireland participates in the national Anti-Money Laundering Steering Committee (AMLSC), a regular information sharing forum for Irish departments, agencies and competent authorities, and incorporates relevant threat and vulnerability updates into its ML/TF Risk Assessment. ## 7.5.6. United States of America: Consistent messaging by banking supervisors, including on lower risk sectors/entities

- **descriptive / case example**: US banking supervisors issued multiple statements on risk-based AML/CFT examinations, including at lower risk entities; the joint statement sets out how examination plans and procedures are tailored to each bank's risk profile. (p. 84)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "banking supervisors tailor examination plans and procedures based on the risk profile of each bank"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: US common practices for assessing bank risk profile include monitoring changes in business model, complexity and risk profile using public information, and tailoring information requests to these. (p. 84)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "tailoring requests for information to the institution’s business model, complexity, and risk profile"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: US supervisors leverage the bank's AML/CFT risk assessment, independent testing or audits, prior examination conclusions and off-site monitoring or request-letter information to determine risk profile and next examination scope. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "independent testing or audits, analyses and conclusions from previous examinations"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: US supervisors contact banks between examinations or before finalising scope, consider the bank's ability to identify, measure, monitor and control risks, and follow up between examinations on remediation. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "considering the bank’s ability to identify, measure, monitor and control risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: US banking supervisors generally allocate more resources to higher-risk and fewer to lower-risk areas, which FATF says promotes financial inclusion by tailoring attention to entity risk profiles. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This approach promotes financial inclusion by allowing supervisors to tailor supervisory attention"</sub>

## 7.6. Special considerations for the MVTS sector

- **descriptive / context**: FATF describes the MVTS sector as diverse: some providers specialise in specific areas with few outlets in one or two jurisdictions, others are global with dense agent networks across many corridors; both often share the same agents (grocery stores, internet cafés, bureaux de change). (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These two broad categories of MVTS providers often use the same agents"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF notes MVTS are a powerful enabler of financial inclusion in many developing countries, while in many jurisdictions all or part of the sector is considered exposed to significant ML/TF risk. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "MVTS are a powerful enabler of financial inclusion in many developing countries"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: FATF says MVTS risks should be frequently (re)assessed and carefully monitored at both sectoral and entity level for an accurate understanding of threats and vulnerabilities. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These risks need to be frequently (re)assessed and carefully monitored"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: FATF says supervisors need to ensure a risk-based approach that mitigates against financial exclusion or unauthorised MVTS activity, which would increase ML/TF risk. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "mitigate against financial exclusion or unauthorised MVTS activities"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The Dutch central bank DNB analyses all money transfers made in the Netherlands each quarter using network analysis to detect potentially unusual patterns and trigger on-site inspections, supervising around a thousand locations this way. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "analyses all money transfers made in the Netherlands each quarter"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In Spain payment institutions must send monthly statistics broken down by country and agent, enabling SEPBLAC's strategic analysis of money remittance to select risk-based supervisory targets and improve operational analysis for competent authorities. (p. 86)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "payment institutions are required to send monthly statistical information broken-down by country and agent"</sub>

## 7.6.1. Australia: Developing an MVTS corridor risk assessment

- **descriptive / definition**: FATF describes 'de-risking' or 'de-banking' as financial institutions terminating or restricting relationships with remittance providers and withdrawing correspondent relationships based on perceived high ML/TF risk, affecting cost and availability of remittances. (p. 86)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the termination or restriction of business relationships with remittance providers by financial institutions"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: AUSTRAC and DFAT, in close consultation with industry, conducted an ML/TF risk assessment of remittance corridors from Australia to Pacific Island countries via remittance providers. (p. 86)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "remittance corridors from Australia to Pacific Island countries36 through remittance providers"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The Pacific corridor assessment used four intelligence inputs (AUSTRAC transaction reports and holdings, law enforcement intelligence, interviews with remitters, banks and experts, and a remitter survey) and examined five areas: criminal threat, customer profile, transaction profile, remittance purpose and detection/mitigation controls. (p. 86)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Five key areas were examined: criminal threat profile, customer profile, transaction profile"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The assessment found ML/TF risks of non-bank remittances from Australia to the 14 South Pacific Island nations were low. (p. 86)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "non-bank remittances from Australia to the 14 South Pacific Island nations were low"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The low-risk finding spurred initiatives to cut costs and widen access: simplified CDD, more industry guidance, and development of a "know-your-customer utility" for Pacific remitters to verify customer identity without raising costs. (p. 87)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "simplified customer due diligence procedures, developing further industry-specific guidance"</sub>

## 7.6.2. France: measures to assess risks in the MVTS sector

- **descriptive / case example**: France's ACPR applies its general FI risk methodology to MVTS but collects specific MVTS data; since 2015 a dedicated team handles MVTS off-site controls, collecting economic and compliance data to tailor strategy and launch inspections. (p. 87)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Since 2015, a dedicated team in the ACPR is assigned to MVTS off-site controls"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: ACPR adapted practices for MVTS risks, notably agent use, including a specific tool for MVTS providers present in France only through agents and stronger FIU co-operation on MVTS. (p. 87)[^original]

## 7.6.3. Malaysia: Outreach with the private sector to address potential de-risking

- **descriptive / case example**: Malaysia addresses de-risking of money services businesses through ongoing engagement among entities, government agencies and banks so stakeholders understand the regulatory framework, oversight and the sector's NRA risk assessment. (p. 87)[^original]

## 8.1.1. Brazil

- **descriptive / method**: COAF's risk model uses an impact-probability matrix to set risk and priority ratings, applied to registered entities and to unregistered persons conducting regulated activities, which determines the supervisory tools applied. (p. 88)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "COAF’s risk model uses a matrix that plots variables of impact and probability"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Technology-based entity risk assessment lets COAF reach a broader range of entities including lower risk ones, balancing enhanced and simplified measures by matrix risk level. (p. 88)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "balance enhanced and simplified measures depending on the risk level shown by the matrix"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: COAF's AVEC (Electronic Compliance Assessment) is a fully automated platform assessing compliance of groups or whole sectors simultaneously via the standard communication channel with registered persons; its results feed the risk and priorities matrix. (p. 88)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The AVEC is an electronic inspection instrument that assesses the degree of compliance of groups or whole sectors"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: COAF's APO (Preliminary Objective Assessment) is an IT-based individual entity check of some obligations, focused on lower-risk situations and needing some supervisor involvement. (p. 88)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is used to verify the compliance of certain natural or legal persons with some of their obligations"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: COAF's APA (Comprehensive Preliminary Assessment) is for higher complexity and risk, requiring documents beyond database checks to allow deep analysis of compliance gaps. (p. 88)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The APA, in turn, is the inspection procedure for higher complexity and risk situations"</sub>

## 8.1.2. Malaysia: Shift from DNFBP Sectoral Analysis to Entity Analysis

- **descriptive / case example**: Bank Negara Malaysia is the main AML/CFT supervisor for DNFBP sectors and, given their large size, applies risk-based supervision across entity selection, activities and actions. (p. 88)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Bank Negara Malaysia – BNM) is the main AML/CFT supervisory authority for DNFBP sectors"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Malaysia shifted DNFBP risk analysis from a sectoral to an entity basis: initially it targeted NRA higher risk sectors (e.g. DPMS risk based on supply chain vulnerabilities such as luxury segments and large retail chains), later adopting more granular entity data for more accurate selection. (p. 89)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Malaysia shifted its risk analysis from a “sectoral basis” to an “entity basis”"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: BNM's DNFBP supervisory actions range from comprehensive review inspections and abridged review inspections to off-site tools including an annual Data and Compliance Report (DCR) collecting inherent risk information. (p. 89)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "comprehensive review inspection, abridged review inspection or off-site supervisory tools"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: BNM's DCR requests customer data (natural/legal persons, legal arrangements, PEP status, high risk jurisdiction customers), products (cash based, nominee, easily transferable), delivery channel, geography, business information (size, turnover, revenues, activities) and sector-specific factors such as TCSP nominee exposure. (p. 89)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Products (e.g. cash based, nominee product, easily transferable products)"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: In BNM inspections each component is rated against baseline parameters; inherent risk and control ratings determine net (residual) risk, which decides whether supervisory or enforcement action follows depending on severity. (p. 89)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "will determine the net risk (residual risk) of an entity inspected"</sub>

## 8.2.1. United Kingdom: The supervisor of DNFBP supervisors (OPBAS) experience

- **descriptive / context**: FATF notes FIs have historically been more tightly regulated for AML/CFT than DNFBPs, driving investment such as transaction monitoring technology, while DNFBP supervisor oversight has been lower in places but is changing through OPBAS's work. (p. 89)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial Institutions have historically been more tightly regulated for AML/CFT than the DNFBP sectors"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: OPBAS oversees SRBs supervising sub-sectors including tax advisory, audit, insolvency, conveyancing and trust company formation, and roles such as accountants, bookkeepers, solicitors, barristers and notaries across the UK. (p. 90)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "tax advisory, audit, insolvency, conveyancing and trust company formation"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Evolving risks in UK professional sectors include sham litigation and planting Organised Crime Gang members into firms through weak staff screening. (p. 90)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "planting Organised Crime Gang members into a firm due to weak staff screening processes"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: When OPBAS began in 2018 it found some supervisors and firms did not view AML as a core function, and some DNFBP supervisors' systems lacked sophistication and treated AML as a tick box exercise. (p. 90)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "some viewing AML as a tick box exercise"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: OPBAS found advocacy and regulatory functions had been separated in legal sector supervisors but not always clearly in accountancy supervisors; without clear demarcation and robust governance, protecting member interests and revenue can conflict with robust regulatory action. (p. 90)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supervisors have separated advocacy from regulatory functions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: FATF reports DNFBP supervisors, with OPBAS support and challenge, continue taking positive steps though progress remains to be made. (p. 90)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "continue to take positive steps in developing their ability to deliver effective AML/CFT supervision"</sub>

## Remote review of files to enable off-site AML/CFT supervision of SRBs who supervise DNFPBs

- **permissive / method**: OPBAS combines on-site and off-site work and can request remote, secure electronic delivery of a DNFBP supervisor's files to identify AML/CFT risk concerns or non-compliance indicators. (p. 90)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "OPBAS can request from a DNFBP supervisor a remote review of relevant files"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Negative file review outcomes can trigger further OPBAS investigation and alter a DNFBP supervisor's risk rating if shortcomings are proven; OPBAS also analyses yearly data provided to the UK government, cross-referenced with other reports, for forward-looking risk profiling. (p. 90)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "OPBAS also analyses yearly data provided to the UK government"</sub>

## Supervising a large number of entities and with limited risk information

- **descriptive / case example**: Singapore's ACD under the Ministry of Law was established in 2019 to supervise precious stone and precious metal dealers (PSMDs); approximately 1 900 PSMDs are registered. (p. 90)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Approximately 1 900 PSMDs are currently registered with ACD"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Facing limited risk information, ACD surveyed all PSMDs in February 2020 (73% responded) and in December 2020 imposed semi-annual reporting to improve data quality and timeliness. (p. 90)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "only 73% of PSMDs responded to the survey"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: ACD obtained STRs and intelligence on PSMDs from law enforcement and combined them with registration probity checks and environmental scanning in its supervisory risk model; the entity risk assessment was completed in April 2020. (p. 90)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "which was completed in April 2020"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: ACD subjects higher risk PSMDs to more frequent and intense inspections on top of off-site monitoring, and periodically recalibrates ratings using inspection outcomes, surveillance, off-site monitoring and financial intelligence. (p. 90)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "ACD would review and re-calibrate the risk rating of PSMDs on a periodic basis"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: ACD participated in AML/CFT capacity building to learn best practice and regional ML/TF typologies given its nascent regime. (p. 90)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "learn regulatory best practices and understand regional ML/TF typologies"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: ACD engaged a third-party professional firm to conduct compliance reviews on PSMDs rated medium-high risk with no identified risk factors, with a quality monitoring process, freeing ACD to focus on higher risk dealers. (p. 90)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "engaging a third party professional firm to conduct compliance reviews on PSMDs"</sub>

## Working with private sector

- **descriptive / case example**: ACD works with industry associations representing jewellery retailers, watch dealers, diamond dealers and bullion traders to raise ML/TF awareness, co-develop educational materials and broadcast guidance, and publishes fact sheets and outreach to inform targeted risk-based supervision. (p. 91)[^original]

## 8.2.3. United Kingdom (Gambling Commission) – monitoring low-risk entities

- **descriptive / case example**: The UK Gambling Commission monitors lower risk entities on a three-year full assessment cycle; to be lower risk a business must have demonstrated regulatory compliance, and during the cycle it is assessed through thematic and targeted compliance activity. (p. 91)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "uses a three-year full assessment cycle to monitor lower risk entities"</sub>

## 8.3.1. United Kingdom - Office for Professional Body Anti-Money Laundering Supervision (OPBAS)

- **descriptive / case example**: In 2019 OPBAS and the National Economic Crime Centre set up two Intelligence Sharing Expert Working Groups (ISEWGs), loosely based on JMLIT, pioneering public-private intelligence sharing for the legal and accountancy professions. (p. 91)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "two new Intelligence Sharing Expert Working Groups (ISEWGs) with the National Economic Crime Centre"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Each sector's ISEWG includes DNFBP AML supervisors, law enforcement via the NECC, HMRC, the FCA and OPBAS, operates under published Terms of Reference, and has strategic and tactical functions. (p. 91)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The ISEWGs have two distinct functions: strategic and tactical"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: In ISEWG strategic sessions members discuss high-level and emerging threats, share anonymised real case examples with mitigations, and a volunteer drafting group redrafts JMLIT alerts to be sector-relevant. (p. 91)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "receive JMLIT alerts and redraft them to make them relevant to their sector"</sub>

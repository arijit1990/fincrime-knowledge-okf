## 2.2.3. Assessing residual risks

- **descriptive / definition**: Residual risks are ML/TF risks remaining after AML/CFT systems and controls are applied to inherent risks. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "are ML/TF risks that remain after _AML/CFT systems and controls_ are applied to address inherent risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: An entity with weak controls may not be high risk if inherent risk is low (though weak controls may be exploited over time, changing inherent exposure), and an entity with high inherent risk may not be high risk if strong controls lower residual risk. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an entity with weak AML/CFT controls may not be high-risk if the inherent risks arising from its businesses are low"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Residual risk assessment should not be purely quantitative based only on numerical scores; supervisors should be able to reflect significant concerns about an entity's potential systemic ML/TF impact. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should not be a purely quantitative approach based solely on numerical risk scores"</sub>
- **expected / limitation**: Supervisors should acknowledge that inherent risks can never be entirely mitigated, so residual risk always remains to be managed by entities in line with their risk appetite. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "no matter how robust AML/CFT controls are, inherent risks cannot be entirely mitigated"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Supervisory risk models usually consider both inherent and residual risk: high inherent risk generally signals need for closer attention, while residual risk may shape intensity/scope and be used to prioritise between entities. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisory risk models usually consider both inherent and residual risks."</sub>
- **permissive / method**: In setting tolerable residual risk, supervisors can consider impact on the jurisdiction if residual risk is high, unintended consequences of over-applying mitigation (e.g. higher ML/TF risk from financial exclusion), and entities' ability to manage residual risk through governance, training and competence. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the possible unintended consequences of over-applying mitigation measures"</sub>

## 2.3. What information does a supervisor need to identify and understand the risks?

- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors' ML/TF risk understanding should rest on analysis of all relevant qualitative and quantitative information, including prudential and conduct records, surveys, off-site reporting, past supervisory records, AML/CFT returns, FIU and LEA feedback on usefulness of entity outputs, and open sources. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should be formed based on the analysis of all relevant qualitative and quantitative information"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors may consider entities' own risk assessments but should always maintain an independent view rather than relying unduly on them. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supervisors should always maintain an independent view instead of unduly relying on the entity’s own risk assessments"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should protect privacy interests, but privacy should not unduly impede information sharing to combat ML, TF and other illicit finance; data access directly affects assessment granularity and timeliness. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "privacy should not serve as an undue impediment to sharing to combat ML, TF, and other illicit financial activities"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: R.2 requires AML/CFT authorities, including supervisors, and data protection and privacy authorities to co-operate and coordinate so AML/CFT requirements are compatible with data protection and privacy rules. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to ensure the compatibility of AML/CFT requirements with Data Protection and Privacy rules"</sub>

## Box 2.3. Sources of information for risk identification and understanding

- **descriptive / data or feature**: Box 2.3 sources include the NRA, findings of past entity-level or thematic supervision, and input from domestic and international supervisors such as prudential findings on corporate governance. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Findings of past supervisory activity (either entity-level or horizontal/thematic reviews)"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Entity-sourced information includes its risk assessment and appetite, data returns and questionnaire responses such as annual compliance reports on AML/CFT controls (a legal requirement in some jurisdictions, e.g. Australia), routine financial and operational off-site data including prudential data, PPP input, and independent testing/audit results. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "annual compliance reports16 that consist of questions relating to the implementation of AML/CFT systems"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: FIU feedback on entities' STRs, covering timeliness and quality, under- or over-reporting relative to peers and responsiveness to FIU requests, should be analysed against total operations in the sector and its concentration level. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "under or over-reporting compared to peers and their responsiveness to the FIU’s request for information"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The FIU may identify cases where an STR should have been filed but was not, an indicator of internal control effectiveness; recurring STR typologies may suggest specific risk exposures or mitigation deficiencies. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identify situations where a suspicious transaction report (STR) should have been filed but was not"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Regular supervisor-FIU exchanges on the governance, functioning and risk culture of entities' AML/CFT teams are a source; the FIU may also share information before inspections or after events like whistle-blower reports. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Regular exchanges between the supervisor and the FIU on their assessment on the governance"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Other competent authorities (police, prosecutors, intelligence, tax, customs, anti-corruption and TFS agencies) provide typologies, sector risk perceptions and views on usefulness of FI AML programme outputs. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "police, prosecutors, intelligence agencies, tax, customs, anticorruption authorities"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: France's ACPR has a dedicated division producing press reviews for off-site supervision teams on request, on specific issues (e.g. tax havens) or specific FIs (e.g. litigation abroad, negative shareholder information); common third-party sources include Transparency International and OCCRP. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is a dedicated division in charge of press reviews"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Further sources: whistle-blower matters and complaints, data on financially excluded populations, and input from international bodies (FATF and FSRB reports, ESAs Risk Factor Guidelines). (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Findings from matters reported by whistle-blowers and complaints."</sub>

## 2.4. How do supervisors keep their risk understanding updated?

- **descriptive / context**: Effective supervision depends on timely identification and prioritisation of areas and institutions; supervisors typically update risk assessments on a fixed cycle and upon trigger events, with opportunities to move to more dynamic assessment using available data. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "review and update their risk assessments according to a fixed cycle and in response to trigger events"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should set out the frequency and triggers for updating sectoral and entity risk assessments in their SRA methodology. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Set out the frequency and triggers for updates to sectoral and entity risk assessments"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should identify and assess emerging risks and trends in their population and revise assessments on an ongoing basis against law enforcement observations on emerging threats and typologies; sources include FIUs, LEAs, inspection teams, other supervisors and FATF/FSRB typology papers. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Identifying and assessing emerging risks and trends17 within their supervised population"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should maintain regular dialogue and information sharing with public and private sectors to understand the latest trends and risks. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Regular dialogue and information sharing with the public and private sector to understand latest trends and risks"</sub>

## 3. Risk-based approach to supervision

- **expected / expectation**: Supervisory authorities should develop and implement risk-based, graduated supervisory strategies with a clear nexus to jurisdiction- or sector-specific ML/TF risks, so risk determines the nature, frequency, intensity and focus of supervision across higher and lower risk entities. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisory authorities should develop and implement supervisory strategies that are risk-based and graduated"</sub>

## 3.1. What is a supervisory strategy?

- **descriptive / definition**: A supervisory strategy sets clear AML/CFT supervision objectives and explains how identified risks and emerging risks will be addressed and how tools will be applied. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A supervisory strategy sets clear objectives for AML/CFT supervision"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The strategy should not focus only on the highest risks but set adequate coverage, including monitoring, of all entities and sectors, including lower risk ones. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The strategy should not only focus on the highest risk entities or sectors"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The strategy and risk assessment output are used to plan supervisory activity, commonly 12 or 24 month inspection plans; the strategy should cover how each risk category is addressed, including non-inspection tools, and the information, support and guidance to be given to entities, and be revised as needed. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "commonly including 12 or 24 month supervision or inspection plans"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should refer to relevant supervisory principles, including the Basel Committee Core Principles for Effective Supervision, and communicate regularly with prudential and other supervisors so concerns are incorporated and respective programmes (inspections, desk-based reviews) are mutually known. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Basel Committee on Banking Supervision’s Core Principles for Effective Supervision"</sub>

## 3.2. How can supervisory strategies address the risks identified?

- **expected / expectation**: Strategies should apply supervisory tools on a graduated basis across entities, with nature, frequency, intensity and focus set by ML/TF risk level. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "application of the supervisory tools on a graduated basis across the spectrum of supervised entities/sectors"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The strategy should explain the rationale for each tool relative to risk ratings (purpose, outcome and regularity); supervisors should judge whether activities contribute to outcomes (risk identification/mitigation) rather than counting interventions. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "rather just the form or quantity of those interventions"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Supervisors should consider bespoke strategies for the highest-risk entities beyond the activity level for others, and thematic reviews across entities in response to risk-trigger events or priority risk areas. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisors should consider developing additional tailored/bespoke strategies for engaging with entities presenting the highest ML/TF risk"</sub>
- **expected / limitation**: Supervisors should augment fixed cycle-based approaches with timely interventions, recognising the limitations of relying solely on cycle inspections set using point-in-time risk assessments given fast-evolving ML/TF risks. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "recognise the limitations of relying solely on cyclebased supervisory inspections"</sub>

## Box 3.1. The use of thematic assessments to address risks across a range of entities

- **descriptive / method**: Thematic inspections, on-site, off-site or both, holistically assess industry awareness and mitigation of NRA-identified risks, prioritising entities with heightened exposure to the theme, which may include entities with otherwise lower overall risk profiles. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could include entities that might otherwise have a lower overall ML/TF risk profile"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: MAS has conducted targeted thematic inspections of FIs on combating proliferation financing, transaction monitoring and detecting abuse of legal persons, and published guidance papers on findings and good practices. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "combating proliferation financing, transaction monitoring, and detecting the abuse of legal persons"</sub>

## 3.3. How can supervisors adjust their approach to vary the nature, frequency, intensity and focus of supervision?

- **expected / method**: Four principles guide tool selection: outcome-focused (clear objectives), risk appropriateness (tool type and intensity matched to entity risk and strategy), efficiency (most efficient means given resources), and dynamism and responsiveness (agile response to emerging risks). (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisors should keep in mind the following four principles in deciding the tools to adopt for supervision."</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Footnote 19: efficiency measures include shorter targeted inspections or meetings, fewer administrative elements, smaller inspection teams for greater coverage, and outsourcing some activities. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "using smaller teams to carry out inspections to gain greater coverage"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Ways to adjust supervision by risk: change the off-site/on-site ratio; focus on higher risk products or specific AML/CFT processes (customer identification, risk assessment, monitoring, reporting) and which interviews and premises (HQ vs branches); adjust frequency and duration; set scope, coverage and depth of transaction testing; and allocate suitably skilled resources. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "adjusting the ratio between off‐site and on‐site supervision"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: Supervisors may combine tools calibrated to objectives: thematic inspections for systemic material risks, and targeted inspections or monitoring tools for entity-specific concerns depending on assessed impact. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "thematic inspections could be carried out to better address material risk concerns that are assessed to be of a systemic nature"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The most intensive tools comprehensively test entity controls; higher-risk entities should face more frequent and intense scrutiny than lower-risk ones. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Entities associated with higher ML/TF risks should be subject to more frequent and more intense scrutiny"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In the US, many of the largest financial institutions have resident inspection staff conducting continuous AML examinations of their components. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "many of the largest financial institutions have resident inspection staff that conduct continuous AML inspections"</sub>

## Box 3.2. Planning inspections and associated resources in line with the supervisory strategy

- **expected / expectation**: Inspection plans should list the entities to be inspected in a period (including follow-ups), the type and scope by risk, the focus of each inspection where relevant, and required resources and timelines. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the supervisory resources required for each inspection or review, as well as a timeline"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Inspection plans should reflect the approach to entities at different risk levels, leave flexibility for unplanned risk-triggered inspections, be documented and amended when entity risk changes or new risks arise, and be governed by an internal policy on approval, progress review, change approval and publication (e.g. inspections per risk rating); adapted from EBA and IMF guidance. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "leave sufficient flexibility to accommodate or address unplanned inspections triggered by risk events"</sub>

## 3.4. How can supervisors use a combination of off-site and on-site tools to strengthen their risk-based approach?

- **descriptive / context**: Supervisory tools used in combination can be mutually reinforcing in strengthening supervisory effectiveness. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These tools when used in combination could have mutually reinforcing effects"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Off-site monitoring keeps supervisors current on the risk landscape, inherent risk profiles and control weaknesses, guiding on-site review focus; effective off-site monitoring analyses data for an entire sector rather than snapshots, e.g. risk surveillance including STR/CTR data to detect emerging risks and control issues. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "risk surveillance (a supervisor’s monitoring of relevant data and information including STR/CTR information where available)"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Where off-site monitoring reveals material risk concerns, supervisors may trigger an immediate for-cause inspection, which should take precedence over routine inspections. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "such for-cause inspections should take precedence over any routine inspections"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: On-site inspections allow more thorough review through sampling tests, validate the entity's risk profile, and through interactions with board, management and staff inform assessment of risk culture; an off-site pre-engagement may revalidate the entity's risk assessment beforehand. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "would help inform supervisors’ assessments of the entities’ risk culture"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Sample testing can be done off-site using entity data and SupTech; where not possible, on-site live testing can be used, e.g. selecting customers randomly or by risk during a CDD walkthrough and asking staff to produce risk assessments and CDD documentation. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "select customers (random selection/based on level of risk etc.) and in a “live” assessment"</sub>
- **expected / context**: As technology improves and entities keep data electronically, boundaries between off-site and on-site work blur and SupTech becomes necessary; supervisors should consider adapting their frameworks weighing pros and cons of approaches. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the boundaries between off-site and on-site interventions are increasingly blurred"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Footnote 20: relied-upon risk assessments and independent audits should consider and test all risk areas, including products, services, customers, delivery channels and geographic locations. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The relied-upon risk assessments and independent audits should properly consider and test all risk areas"</sub>

## 3.5. How should supervisors treat lower risk sectors and entities?

- **expected / expectation**: Most supervisory resources should go to higher risk areas, but strategies should also set the approach for lower risk areas; sound sectoral assessment does not necessarily require assessing each entity. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "most supervisory resources should be dedicated to the higher ML/TF risk areas"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: Lower risk entities still receive supervisory attention commensurate with their risk, e.g. less frequent cycles, sample testing or reactive interventions; supervisors are not expected to cover all lower risk entities in a fixed inspection cycle, especially in large populations. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisory authorities are not expected to cover all lower ML/TF risk entities under a fixed inspection cycle"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: Monitoring of lower risk entities may involve limited on-site tools and centre on detecting material risk events or escalations in risk profiles to enable effective intervention. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "centre it on the detection of any material risk events or escalations in risk profiles"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should regularly test their assumptions about risk and control adequacy and be able to act reactively on intelligence from returns, other supervisors, media, whistle-blowers, law enforcement or the FIU/STRs. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisory authorities should regularly test their understanding and assumptions of the level of ML/TF risk"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Education and outreach should extend to lower risk sectors so they apply proportionate measures and report ML/TF risks; supervisors can support financial inclusion, e.g. by reducing requirements on lower risk entities that do not mitigate enough risk to justify the effort. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "reducing requirements on lower risk entities that do not mitigate risk sufficiently to justify the effort they consume"</sub>

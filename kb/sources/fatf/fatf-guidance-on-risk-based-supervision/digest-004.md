## 3.5. How should supervisors treat lower risk sectors and entities?

- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 21: the FATF Financial Inclusion Guidance gives further detail on risk assessment supporting simplified due diligence and justified exemptions. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "risk assessment for the application of simplified due diligence and justified exemptions"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should not create blanket exemptions shielding all low-risk entities or entire low-risk sectors from possible on-site inspection; any entity or sector should potentially face inspection at some point. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisors should, however, not put in place blanket exemptions that exempt all low-risk entities"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: This can be achieved through a minimum number of annual random on-site inspections and/or a policy specifying high-risk circumstances (e.g. presence of certain risk indicators) warranting inspection despite otherwise low risk. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a minimum number of (annual) random on-site inspections"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Supervisors can support financial inclusion by reassuring regulated entities that serve lower risk entities that those entities are adequately supervised. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "financial services to lower risk entities those lower risk entities are adequately supervised"</sub>

## Box 3.3. Supervising lower risk sectors and entities and supporting financial inclusion

- **expected / expectation**: Lower risk does not mean no risk; supervisors should be able to detect significant new risk concerns in lower risk sectors and entities and devote sufficient resources to verifying and monitoring risk understanding there, while allocating more to higher risk sectors. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "lower risk does not mean no risk"</sub>
- **permissive / requirement**: R.1 and INR.1 allow jurisdictions to exempt particular types of regulated entities from some FATF Recommendations where there is proven low risk and the exemption is in strictly limited and justified circumstances. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "if there is a proven low risk and the exemption occurs in strictly limited and justified circumstances"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: In a risk-based regime, CDD, internal controls, compliance function, ongoing monitoring, STR and other reporting requirements should correspond to sector and institution risk. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should also correspond to the risk-level of the sector and the institutions"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Disproportionate obligations, supervisory expectations and lack of guidance can lead to unnecessarily prohibitive CDD in lower risk sectors, raising costs and undermining financial inclusion. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may result in the application of unnecessarily prohibitive CDD and other AML/CFT controls in lower risk sectors"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Excessive AML/CFT obligations may raise overall ML/TF risk by driving users to the unregulated sector or making services to low-income customers such as NPOs unprofitable, shifting transactions to less transparent channels. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "driving potential users to the unregulated sector as a result of their failure to gain access"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: US banking supervisors reminded banks that the charitable sector as a whole should not be seen as uniformly or unacceptably high ML/TF risk, and issued non-binding guidance on factors for assessing NPOs' AML/CFT risk profiles. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should not view the charitable sector as a whole as presenting a uniform or unacceptably high risk"</sub>

## Box 3.4. The role of supervisors in identifying de-risking or in encouraging financial inclusive practices

- **descriptive / context**: Financial exclusion carries serious ML/TF risk because customers may turn to the unregulated cash economy or providers lacking robust controls. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "customers may seek the unregulated cash economy"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Where an institution engages in large-scale, indiscriminate account closures or service denials, or fails to apply simplified due diligence where risks are lower, supervisors should engage it to understand its reasons. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is involved in large-scale and indiscriminate account closure or denial of services"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Per FATF MVTS Guidance (2016), accepting or maintaining relationships and using SDD are ultimately commercial decisions, but supervisors need to scrutinise them to see whether they signal need for clarifications or reform, changing risks or profit concerns, and should give guidance where decisions stem from misunderstanding RBA flexibility. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the decision to introduce simplified due diligence measures or to accept or maintain a business relationship is ultimately a commercial one"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Indiscriminate denial of service to entire customer classes without seriously assessing individual customer risk and mitigation is contrary to FATF advice. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "indiscriminate denials of service to entire classes of customer"</sub>

## 3.6. How can supervisors develop a more robust risk-based approach over time?

- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should keep strategies under regular review, using knowledge gained on entity controls, business model risks and tool effectiveness to enhance sectoral and entity risk understanding and consider emerging risks; institutional memory is key. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Building and maintaining the institutional memory is key to achieve this."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should use experience to continuously refine strategies and methods, and risk assessment and planning should be coordinated with prudential supervision and other relevant departments or supervisors, not done in isolation. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should not be conducted in isolation, but in close co-ordination with prudential supervision"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should ensure sound, consistent assessments and independent decision-making; risk ratings for sectors and entities should be supported by a documented assessment outline, including on-site and off-site findings, and rationale. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supported by a documented outline of the assessment"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: Especially for large complex populations, risk ratings may be peer reviewed by uninvolved AML/CFT staff; this is preferable for every sectoral rating change and, for entities, on an annual sample across all risk ratings rather than all entities. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "subject to peer review/challenge by other staff members within the AML/CFT supervisory unit"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: A supervisory panel of management or specialist staff not involved in direct supervision can provide independent "horizontal" review of findings and recommendations to promote consistent judgement and share broader perspective. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a supervisory panel to provide independent judgement and to promote consistency"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Adequate training at all levels, from front-line supervisors to managers and board members, on interacting with entities and risk-based decision-making is required; visible senior staff engagement signals commitment. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Training is required at all levels, from front-line supervisors to managers and board members."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Supervisory management must articulate its risk tolerance, and recognise that AML/CFT weaknesses in lower risk areas may go undetected under risk-based supervision; responses depend on whether outcomes fall within the risk tolerance. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AML/CFT related weaknesses in areas of lower ML/TF risk may go undetected by supervisors"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Supervisors across sectors and jurisdictions should encourage collegiality and share best practices, e.g. "best practice" visits and inspections helping less mature authorities learn from established supervisors. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should encourage collegiality and share best practices"</sub>

## 3.7. How should remedial actions and available sanctions be applied in risk-based supervision?

- **mandatory / requirement**: R.35 requires jurisdictions to have a range of effective, proportionate and dissuasive criminal, civil or administrative sanctions for natural or legal persons failing AML/CFT requirements. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "R.35 requires jurisdictions to have a range of effective, proportionate and dissuasive sanctions"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should have access to progressively severe remedial actions and sanctions, such as warnings, action letters, orders, agreements, administrative sanctions, penalties and fines, and restrictions or conditions on activities, matched to the level and nature of deficiencies. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "warnings, action letters, orders, agreements, administrative sanctions, penalties and fines"</sub>
- **expected / method**: In choosing remedial actions, supervisors should consider the nature of findings, prioritising deficiencies in higher risk areas identified in national, sectoral or supervisory risk assessments. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "deficiencies in relation to higher risk areas, including those identified in a national, sectoral or supervisory risk assessment"</sub>
- **expected / method**: Supervisors should consider impact or harm: systemic breakdown vs isolated incident or egregious activity (e.g. failing to report large volumes of suspicious activity), how long the gap persisted, and probability of risks materialising given size, nature, geographic reach and business volume. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "whether it is a systemic breakdown, isolated incident or other egregious activity"</sub>
- **expected / method**: Supervisors should consider withdrawing, restricting or suspending licences (or registration) where an entity is found by legal process to have engaged in ML/TF-related crime or severe systematic AML/CFT violations, and prohibiting directors and senior managers. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a severe and systematic violation of AML/CFT measures"</sub>
- **expected / method**: Supervisors should consider publishing supervisory action results and deficiency information so other entities note the consequences of similar failings. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "publishing the results of the supervisory actions and providing information on the relevant entities’ deficiencies"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Remedial actions and sanctions should deter past misconduct, correct control weaknesses and promote a compliance culture across board, senior management, compliance and other staff; supervisors should be able to sanction board members, management, controlling owners and employees proportionately to their responsibility, especially for intentional or serious breaches. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "against board of directors and management, controlling owners and other employees of regulated entities"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should ensure entities' compliance departments have stature, independence, staffing and resources commensurate with the entity's risk profile. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "have sufficient stature, independence, staffing and resources commensurate with the risk profile"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: Confidence in an institution's intent, commitment and capability to remediate may shape choice of formal vs informal tools; with a large deficiency but a satisfactory compliance culture and high remediation capability, supervisors may take a lighter approach. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the supervisors may opt to take a lighter approach in remediation techniques"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Supervisors should apply sanctions transparently, consistently and proportionately, and consider policies on which actions fit which circumstances and methodologies for setting fines and sanctions dissuasive and proportionate to entity size and seriousness of failure. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "methodologies for calculating/determining amount of fines, severity of orders and administrative sanctions"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should avoid a "zero tolerance" or "zero failure" approach and mandatory sanctions where risk impact is immaterial, as this encourages a return to rules-based compliance. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supervisors should avoid taking a “zero tolerance” or “zero failure” approach"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Sanctions may suit lower-risk non-compliance such as repeated, knowing or wilful breaches, but supervisors should consider the totality of mitigation and avoid sanctioning entities for focusing on higher risk areas. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to address repeated, knowing or wilful non-compliance with AML/CFT requirements"</sub>

## 3.8. How should supervisors measure the effectiveness of their risk-based approach?

- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should record, monitor and analyse their own activities, keeping an (ideally digital) repository of engagements with each entity including issues, action plans and risk assessments, and extract MI to measure against key risk indicators and feed aggregates into the NRA. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they have a repository for recording supervisory engagements (ideally in digital form)"</sub>
- **recommended / method**: Supervisors are encouraged to use data to show impact, tracking numbers and seriousness of issues over time, control effectiveness ratings, changes in entity risk management and risk profiles, and sector trends including de-risking and financial exclusion. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisors are encouraged to use data to determine and demonstrate the impact of their supervision."</sub>
- **expected / method**: Supervision data should target resources, tools and outreach, e.g. rising transaction monitoring deficiencies prompting new guidance; success shows as findings moving from significant gaps to refinements, and improved entity risk assessments. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "potential deficiencies in the transaction monitoring capabilities of the regulated entities"</sub>
- **expected / method**: Supervisors should seek FIU feedback on the number, quality and timeliness of STRs from sectors and entities, as improvements can indicate supervisory success. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisors should seek feedback from FIUs as to the number, quality and timeliness of reports"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Effectiveness factors include ML/TF offences using the sector's infrastructure and trends, STR number/quality/timeliness changes, number and severity of breaches including repeats, stakeholder complaints, and evidence of entities going beyond tick-box compliance with proportionate responses across risk levels. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Evidence of entities going beyond a tick-box approach"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Measuring results and output feedback guards against confirmation bias; misalignment with supervisors' risk understanding should prompt reconsideration, and supervisors should revisit risk models using LEA, FIU and international input, sample testing, whistle-blower reports or adverse media. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "can help safeguard against confirmation bias"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Accountability mechanisms should include at least one of: management board oversight; supervisor oversight of SRBs (decentralised model); review by a State Audit Office or similar; or publication of strategy, inspection plans and results. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "review by a State Audit Office or similar governmental body"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: Without impinging on independence, KPIs can be set by the board, State Audit Office or national co-ordination authority; industry surveys can assess supervisor performance; and supervisors and FIU can report STR number and quality by sector. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "industry surveys could be used to periodically assess performance of the supervisor"</sub>

## Box 3.5. UK’s Office for Professional Body Anti-Money Laundering Supervision (OPBAS) measures to test the effectiveness of DNFBP supervisors’ risk-based approach

- **descriptive / case example**: OPBAS supervises SRBs designated as DNFBP supervisors under UK money laundering regulations; at its request a supervisor of a UK NRA high-risk sector deep-dived its 103 highest risk entities on-site, found high non-compliance and poor controls, and allocated resources to ongoing more intensive supervision. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identified their 103 highest risk entities"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: That supervisor will also dip-sample high, medium and low risk entities to test whether its wider supervisory strategy is fit for purpose. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "dip sample visit some entities identified as high, medium and low risk"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Where SRBs both advocate for and supervise members, conflicts of interest must be managed; this was an OPBAS focus, and robust governance helps mitigate it. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "care must be taken to ensure potential conflicts of interest are managed appropriately"</sub>

## 3.9. Domestic co-operation, including between AML/CFT supervision and prudential supervision

- **descriptive / context**: Co-operation among AML/CFT supervisors, other supervisors, FIUs, tax authorities and law enforcement is important for shared risk understanding; coordination with LEAs and the FIU helps assess usefulness of entities' AML/CFT outputs and align risk prioritisation messages. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "can help to assess the effectiveness/usefulness of the outputs of entities’ AML/CFT programs"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Prudential and AML/CFT supervisors should establish effective co-operation regardless of institutional setting; even separate prudential supervisors often handle licensing and monitor controls relevant to AML/CFT (see Basel Committee guidelines Annex 5). (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Prudential and AML/CFT supervisors should establish an effective co-operation mechanism regardless of the institutional setting"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Resource availability can distort attention: a well-funded supervisor of a lower risk sector may over-supervise, while a poorly resourced supervisor of a higher risk sector may under-supervise; NRA processes and coordination should aim for risk-sensitive resource allocation. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a poorly resourced supervisor of a higher risk sector may fail to apply adequate proportion of its resources"</sub>

## 3.10. International co-operation to achieve a risk-based approach to supervision

- **descriptive / context**: Many entities operate cross-border under multiple supervisors; ML/TF risks are often cross-border and control failings can replicate across a group, so risk-based supervision requires international co-operation, covered by Recommendation 40 (INR.40 paragraphs 10-13). (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "systems and control failings in one part of the group can be replicated elsewhere"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: International co-operation enhances risk understanding, e.g. of group attitudes and emerging risks, such as risks of MVTS corridors for financial inclusion, and sharing risk and control assessments strengthens understanding of group risk profiles. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "sharing risks associated with MVTS corridors for financial inclusion purposes"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: International co-operation harnesses synergies via coordinated interventions and shared priorities; supervisors may conduct inquiries for foreign counterparts or let them conduct inquiries in-country, and can jointly assess group-level preventive measures and control and audit functions. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisors may conduct inquiries on behalf of foreign counterparts"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Challenges include lack of common understanding on what to share, legal obstacles, data protection rules inhibiting sharing personal data for fit and proper tests, withholding of ongoing case information for fear of tipping off, and ad hoc rather than ongoing contact. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Data protection and privacy provisions often inhibit sharing of relevant personal information for fit and proper tests."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors of higher risk group entities should actively communicate with other relevant supervisors through official channels, including spontaneous sharing; Basel Committee guidelines address home/host roles and group-wide AML/CFT supervision. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "including spontaneous sharing of information that may be relevant to other supervisors"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In the EU, cooperation occurs in AML/CFT colleges for entities in multiple member states; DNFBP supervisory co-operation is less developed, but DNFBP supervisors participate in the "International Supervisors Forum" with Australia, Canada, New Zealand, the UK and the US. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "which includes supervisors from Australia, Canada, New Zealand, the United Kingdom and the United States"</sub>

## 5.3. New areas of supervisory responsibility – identifying and understanding the risks

- **descriptive / context**: Where supervisors' mandates are expanded to cover activities not previously subject to AML/CFT supervision (paragraph 124), they may lack a good understanding of the sector's risks or of the strength of mitigation measures, and need to consider how to integrate these entities into their risk models. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supervisors may not have a good understanding of the risks in the sector or the strength of mitigation measures"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: As a starting point for new activities, supervisors should focus on the potential inherent ML/TF risk of the sector, build an initial understanding of it, and supplement that through engagement with law enforcement, other supervisors already supervising or licensing/registering such entities, and the entities themselves (for example through an ML/TF questionnaire or meetings during registration or licensing). (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supervisors should focus on the potential level of ML/TF risk in the sector (i.e., inherent risks)"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: To avoid diverting resources from existing higher-risk sectors when taking on new sectors, additional resources may be required or sought, and these resource considerations should be part of planning and rolling out regulation to new sectors. Supervisors can also learn from other jurisdictions already supervising the activities. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "additional resources may be required or sought"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Where many entities enter a market or seek licensing or registration at once (for example VASPs), it may be useful for supervisors to build in flexibility to prioritise incoming requests, for example by prioritising entities carrying out the highest-risk activities for early registration, monitoring key risk indicators, placing more emphasis on ad-hoc onsite and off-site reviews, and engaging regularly with industry bodies. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it may be useful for supervisors to ensure that sufficient flexibility is built into their approach"</sub>

## 5.4. Difficulties in assessing risks at the entity-level

- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 127 notes that some sectors have a large number of mostly smaller institutions, making comprehensive individual risk profiles difficult; for newly established institutions or recently regulated sectors there may be no in-depth knowledge of business models and activities, and the supervisor's own audits or other supervisory results are not yet available. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Some sectors have a large number of (mostly smaller) active institutions"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Supervisors should undertake sectoral risk assessments as a first step; these give an overview of risks from business activities in the sector and insight into individual institutions' profiles, and make it possible to provisionally apply the sectoral risk rating as a default rating to newly established or recently regulated institutions. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Undertake sectoral risk assessments as a first step."</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Depending on the regulatory population, supervisors can develop clusters of entities sharing common characteristics where the ML/TF risks affecting entities in the cluster are very similar. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "develop clusters of entities that share common characteristics"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Supervisors should encourage supervised entities to use the supervisors' sectoral risk assessment as a starting point or model for their own risk assessments over time, and could consider making an application to register conditional upon preparation of a risk assessment (reviewed at the time of application). (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisors could also consider making application to register conditional upon preparation of a risk assessment"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: The larger, more comprehensive and higher-risk an entity's business activities, the greater the granularity that should be applied in assessing its risks; for small entities with very limited business, risk profiles can be based on the sector analysis combined with key financial figures such as turnover, transaction volume and cross-border transactions of the business volume. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the greater degree of granularity in the assessment of risks should be carried out when developing a risk profile"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: To improve entities' risk assessments, supervisors should identify themes and common shortcomings that can be addressed through guidance and feedback, and use several channels to disseminate NRA or supervisory risk assessment outcomes. Jersey produced a video on key ML/TF risks, and other jurisdictions have produced summarised snapshots of risks. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identify themes and common shortcomings that may be addressed through guidance and feedback"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Supervisors should provide clear guidance for institutional risk assessments and consider ready-to-use templates. Where entities lack analytical capacity, templates may collect risk information (for example volumes of certain products or services, number of non-resident clients) that can be the basis for the supervisor's risk assessment. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Consider developing ready to use templates that will guide them in their institutional risk assessments"</sub>

## 5.5. Building risk understanding over time

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 129 acknowledges that developing a supervisory risk assessment methodology for the first time, or updating it to provide more nuanced assessment, can be a daunting task. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to provide more nuanced risk assessment, can be a daunting task"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Supervisors should seek to identify and use quantitative and qualitative data when starting or updating a risk assessment, ideally up-to-date, accurate, relevant and consistent data. Data can be obtained through a questionnaire or data return from entities covering, for example, ML/TF alerts, STR activity and staff training, as well as the entity's financial and economic activity. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisors should seek to identify and use quantitative and qualitative data when starting or updating a risk assessment"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Supervisory risk understanding develops over time through supervisory work, engagement with law enforcement and other supervisors, and participation in AML/CFT fora; this should be incorporated into risk assessments, and supervisors should have processes ensuring regular review and update. Supervisors should seek to undertake entity-level risk assessments when applying supervisory tools, and these should feed into sectoral risk assessments. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these individual risk assessments should feed into the sectoral risk assessments"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: When developing a risk assessment methodology, supervisors should opt for models giving results at various levels (for example by risk category for one or across multiple entities, consolidated views, year-over-year trends), allowing a view of risk across entities of similar size and operations or in the same sector, and allowing reports on changes in risks and control quality between assessment periods. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supervisors should opt for the models that provide results at various levels"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Supervisory authorities should seek to form an initial view of the inherent risk in the sectors they supervise and of the national context, drawing on the NRA, sector experts and engagement with other relevant authorities; the source says this ensures the risk factors assessed are adapted for ML/TF purposes. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "build an initial understanding of the inherent risk in the sectors they supervise"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Supervisory authorities should seek to enhance and strengthen their models for risk understanding by supplementing the qualitative approach with quantitative information. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supplementing the qualitative approach to risk understanding with quantitative information"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Supervisory authorities that routinely collect data from supervised entities as part of their supervisory tools, or that have access to data from other sources, should ensure that relevant data is integrated into the risk assessment process. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should ensure that relevant data is integrated into the risk assessment process"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Supervisors should also consider adapting the data requested via questionnaires or data returns to address the latest risks; case study 7.1.2 is referenced. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should also consider adapting the data requested via questionnaires or data returns to address the latest risks"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: As a risk model becomes more sophisticated it may be adapted to distinguish more finely between entities' relative risks (for example by adding more specific rating categories), and supervisors should periodically review whether their risk rating approach remains adequate and proportionate to the regulatory population. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisors should review periodically their risk rating approach to assess whether it remains adequate and proportionate"</sub>

## 5.6. Engagement with other authorities to supplement the risk assessment

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 131 states that other authorities hold information that should inform supervisory risk assessments: for example suspicious activity reported to FIUs, so supervisors need feedback on reporting and typologies, and prudential or foreign authorities may know of new activities in a regulated entity that can create new AML/CFT risks. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Other authorities hold important information that should inform supervisory risk assessments"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Supervisors should diversify the inputs to their risk assessments by engaging with other AML/CFT or prudential supervisors, the FIU, law enforcement agencies and relevant foreign authorities, facilitated by secondments, liaison officers, joint meetings or guidance. In some jurisdictions the FIU provides regular reports on the quality and quantity of STR filings and specific warnings on internal control weaknesses (see section 3.9 and case studies at 7.5). (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisors should diversify the sources of inputs of their risk assessments by engaging with other stakeholders"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Supervisors should build strong co-operation with prudential or other authorities regulating the supervised sectors. Where one authority supervises both ML/TF and prudential risk of FIs there can be significant synergies (understanding business models, governance and control weaknesses), but information sharing and co-operation remain critical, as when the functions sit in different agencies. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Building strong co-operation with the prudential authorities or other authorities regulating the sectors being supervised"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Strong co-operation with foreign authorities can be achieved through informal and proactive information exchange, international supervisory colleges and official communication channels, participation in supervisors' forums and regular meetings (see section 3.10). (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "establishing international supervisory colleges and official channels for communication"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Public/private co-operation example: the UK has published an Economic Crime Plan setting out public and private sector actions to prevent abuse of the UK for economic crime; inputs and outputs are considered at ministerial and working level so that risks are identified, shared and mitigated across the financial services and DNFBP sectors. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the UK has published its Economic Crime Plan"</sub>

## 5.6

- **expected / expectation**: The methodology and results of a supervisor's risk assessment should be well supported by a clear rationale and an understanding of how risks are identified and weighted, and should be revisited regularly as the risk environment changes. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should be well supported with a clear rationale and understanding"</sub>

## 5.7. Data collection issues

- **descriptive / limitation**: Data collection is an important way for supervisors to identify and monitor risks but can be time consuming and burdensome for entities and supervisors when inefficient; entities may struggle to provide data where their systems are incompatible with the supervisor's, and supervisors may struggle to process large-scale data sets (paragraph 133). (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Data collection is an important way for supervisors to identify and monitor risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Common data collection challenges listed are: lack of relevant historical quantitative data or data not retained in the requested form; information not in digital format or held in multiple databases; high volume; inconsistent definitions and compatibility issues between institutions; need for data cleaning; and cost of collection, validation, storage, processing and dissemination. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "inconsistent definitions may affect the quality of the data collected"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 134: entities may not understand data requirements or may interpret them differently, creating consistency and comparability problems and inaccurate outcomes. A new data request may require supervised entities to adapt their information systems, so advance notice is needed, even though supervisors increasingly use technology that needs data. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "adapt their information system to be able to report adequate and reliable data, so advance notice is needed"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Supervisory agencies should coordinate and share information internally so that information already collected by one department is not requested by another, and consult other authorities such as the FIU that may hold relevant data. In the UK the FCA has an Information Governance Board to ensure uniform data requests are justified, including that the data has not already been collected. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Effective co-ordination and information sharing within the supervisory agency"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Regulated entities should be consulted early in the development of data collection tools. In France, FIs are consulted before the yearly ML/TF questionnaire is issued; presenting new questions and the rationale for changes helps entities understand and answer accurately, lets them raise difficulties such as data availability needing IT developments, and facilitates collection of better data. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Regulated entities should be consulted early in the development of data collection tools"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Supervisors should increase the type of information requested gradually, starting with information already collected, and consider automated collection. They should carefully assess the minimum data needed for an informed ML/TF risk assessment, bearing in mind more information does not necessarily mean a better assessment, and give sufficient prior notice and time for entities to adapt systems and ensure data quality and reliability. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "more information does not necessarily translate into a better risk assessment"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: For significant providers of data, supervisors may liaise with the entities' technology providers so back-end/output supervisory requirements can be built into front-end/input data collection portals. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supervisors may liaise with the entities"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: For sectors with fast-paced technology or market change (for example the VASP sector), authorities could engage with industry bodies or self-regulating bodies to understand the technology and adapt data collection accordingly. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "authorities could engage with industry bodies or self-regulating bodies to understand the technology"</sub>

## 5.8. Special considerations for DNFBP supervisors

- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 136: some sectors, particularly DNFBPs, have very many entities, so supervisors may have little or no data on individual activities; the range of sizes (sole traders to international groups) and diversity of activities make assessing risks across all sub-sectors challenging without highly specialised supervisory resources. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "have a very large number of entities such that understanding ML/TF risks of each entity is difficult"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 137 lists reasons data collection from DNFBP sub-sectors may be difficult: little or no capacity to produce comprehensive reliable data; lack of legal authority to collect data (particularly for self-regulating bodies); difficulty identifying reporting entities, especially in sectors not directly licensed or regulated; and absence of compliance data on individual entities, which makes assessing control effectiveness and residual risk particularly challenging. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a lack of legal authority to collect data"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Supervisors of DNFBP sectors may identify sub-sectors, market segments or clusters and understand their features so that risk profiles can be established at the sub-sector or segment level. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "risk profiles can be established at the subsectorial or segment level"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Supervisors may develop simplified risk assessment templates for less complex, lower-risk entities, collecting information on business and transactions, products and services, and client profiles, from which supervisors can form a broad judgement of risk. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisors may develop simplified risk assessment templates for less complex entities"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Supervisors may coordinate with licensing bodies and sectoral associations to obtain information on sector entities, subject to a legal basis for sharing information; these bodies could help identify entities for supervisory focus based on criteria set by the supervisor. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "subject to a legal basis to share information between the supervisor and these bodies"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Supervisors may introduce and strictly enforce obligations to submit risk and activity information (for example an annual report); such obligations need to be backed by provisions in law together with sanctions for non-submission. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These obligations need to be augmented by provisions in law together with sanctions for nonsubmission"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: For sectors with little data, supervisors may initially adopt a relatively simple risk-based strategy driven by broad indicators of inherent risk, adding complexity as better data becomes available and engagement increases, allowing consideration of control frameworks and residual risk (see sections 5.4 and 6.3). (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supervisors may initially implement a relatively simple risk-based supervisory strategy"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Supervisors may undertake on-sites at a random sample of sectors with data gaps (for example lower-risk sub-sectors not on regular inspection cycles), to assess control frameworks and also to confirm their risk understanding of the sector or the validity of risk information provided. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisors may undertake on-sites of a random sample of sectors where there are data gaps"</sub>

## 5.9. Other guidance

- **expected / recommendation**: Supervisors should have skilled and trusted personnel able to assess and understand risks, including recruitment through fit and proper tests or integrity testing as appropriate; authorities should maintain high professional standards so staff skills and expertise are commensurate with the complexity of the entity's operations and risk profile, and meet integrity standards. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisors should have skilled and trusted personnel who can assess and understand risks"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Authorities should consider balancing staff specialisation in particular sectors or entities over several years, to build knowledge, with rotation or other safeguards for objectivity and sharing of expertise within teams; secondments from industry are a good way to complement knowledge and experience. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "building in rotation or other safeguards to ensure objectivity and sharing of expertise within supervisory teams"</sub>

## 6.1. Sequencing to establish risk-based supervision

- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 139: where supervisors take on new responsibilities or AML/CFT supervision is applied to new sectors, it may be difficult to achieve fully effective risk-based supervision in the short term. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it may be difficult to achieve a fully effective risk-based supervision over the short term"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Supervisors should consider building a step-by-step approach to risk-based supervision into their strategy, illustrated by the process of the Anti-Money Laundering Compliance Unit in the Irish Department of Justice, which supervises several DNFBP sectors. A first step is to develop the legal framework and define the regime's scope (activities or entity types regulated), including thinking about sector-specific powers such as powers to enter premises and remove files. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Develop legal framework and define scope of the regime"</sub>

## Box 6.1. Step-by-step approach to establishing risk-based supervision

- **descriptive / method**: Box 6.1 (Irish Department of Justice) steps: establish a preliminary understanding of the sector, including an estimate of entities in scope and size of operations; establish supervisory authority and staff, considering knowledge and skills gaps and technology needs; and run a staff training programme (who delivers it, who is involved, what is available). (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Establish a preliminary understanding of the sector, including identifying an estimate of the entities in scope"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Box 6.1 steps: develop inspection procedures around legislative obligations and international best practice (for example FATF/EU), and think about inspection frequency (for example more often for high risk) and focus (for example particular cohort challenges). (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Develop inspection procedures around obligations in legislation, international best practice"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Box 6.1 steps: learn about cohorts by identifying inherent risks through the specific threats and vulnerabilities of each sector, reviewing existing information (NRAs, other authorities' assessments, FATF and EU documentation) and identifying missing information. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Learn about your cohorts – identify inherent risks by understanding the specific threats and vulnerabilities in each sector"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Box 6.1 steps: consider the balance between off-site reviews and onsite inspections (it is sometimes difficult to establish residual risks in certain cohorts without an on-site visit), and identify residual risks after applying AML/CFT measures. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Sometimes it is difficult to establish residual risks in certain cohorts without an on-site visit."</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Box 6.1 steps: undertake outreach with the sector before commencing inspections (for example information booklets and templates), consider capacity-building inspections for both entity and supervisor, and share information within the AML supervisor (for example fortnightly internal team meetings on findings, issues, FATF/EU guidance, trends, media and strategies). The source notes that in reality many steps may happen in a different order or in tandem. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Undertake outreach with sector before commencing inspections"</sub>

## 6.2. Insufficient resources or inexperienced staff

- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 141: there may be a lack of, or inadequately trained, staff for proper risk-based supervision; teams may be new, covering new sectors, or be existing regulators newly assigned AML/CFT responsibilities, and supervisory tools and technologies may be lacking. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There may be a lack of, or inadequately trained staff, to conduct a proper risk-based supervision"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Supervisors should ensure senior management support and buy-in, and use risk assessment results to secure extra resources by showing remaining unmitigated risks. Within a larger agency they should consider designating AML/CFT resources; where expertise is lacking they should develop capacity-building strategies and consider appropriate use of other experts, secondments from more experienced authorities, and third parties or consultants as an interim measure (see section 4.3). (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Ensure that there is requisite senior management support and buy in within the supervisory body"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: When designing the supervisory approach and target operating model, supervisors should conduct a detailed training needs analysis and allocate training resources. A supervisor taking on a newly regulated sector is unlikely to have staff with both sector technical knowledge and risk-based supervision experience, or to recruit them easily; tailored training and mixed teams of skilled supervisors and technical experts can address this. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "conduct a detailed training needs analysis and allocate resources for training"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Supervisors should provide AML/CFT training or learning opportunities with adequate budget and staff time, and explore best-practice insight from more established supervisors, for example a resource centre with job aids and templates, access to financial crime training courses or recorded material, and international or regional training or experience exchange. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a resource centre that has job aids, templates, and other tools that can assist less experienced staff"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: When allocating limited supervisory resources according to sector and entity risks, under the outcome-focused approach (Section 3.4) supervisors should focus not only on headcount but also on the capability and training of AML/CFT staff. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supervisors should focus not only on the headcount but also the capability and training"</sub>

## 6.3. Supervising sectors with a large number of entities and limited risk information

- **recommended / recommendation**: Supervisors should identify key players, such as entities with large market share or in higher-risk sub-sectors, and may engage with entities providing AML/CFT compliance services to many entities (for example outsourced transaction monitoring or CDD). It may be possible to use economies of scale by leveraging an inspection of one entity through assumptions about others using the same service provider, subject to individual refinements and differences in use of the product or service. (p. 62)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It may be possible to use economies of scale by leveraging off an inspection to one entity"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Supervisors should identify and engage AML/CFT compliance officers in these entities to raise awareness of risks and regulatory requirements. (p. 62)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Identifying and engaging with AML/CFT compliance officers in these entities"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Supervisors should use communications and guidance to set expectations and give feedback on good and poor practices, through channels such as industry outreach, published outcomes of thematic reviews and details of specific failings in enforcement notices, so that businesses with less direct supervisory engagement can run gap analysis of their systems and controls. (p. 62)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "publishing the outcomes of thematic reviews and detailing specific failings in enforcement notices"</sub>

## 6.4. Poor independent audits of entities

- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 144: many supervisors of financial institutions use FIs' internal and external audits as an important source of information on AML/CFT controls (many smaller DNFBPs lack internal audit functions). Audits of inadequate scope or quality challenge supervisors: they cannot be leveraged to tailor review areas and allocate resources, and poor audit reports and papers hinder understanding of audit coverage and the quality and quantity of transaction testing, limiting the supervisor's ability to risk-focus. (p. 62)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Independent audits with an inadequate scope or of poor quality may present a challenge for the supervisor"</sub>

## 6.3. Supervising sectors with a large number of entities and limited risk information

- **permissive / recommendation**: If adequate information is available, risk rating scales with more ratings (for example high, medium high, medium, medium low and low) may help provide greater distinction of relative risks of entities within and across sectors with many entities than a three-rating scale, which lets supervisors tailor their approach further. (p. 62)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "using risk rating scales that include more risk ratings"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: Sub-sectors or clusters of entities can be grouped by similar, factual inherent risk characteristics, such as conveyancing in London. (p. 62)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "can be grouped together by similar, factual inherent risk characteristics"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: Supervisors can supervise such sub-sectors by picking entities under a risk-based sampling methodology for on-site or off-site supervision. (p. 62)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisors can supervise these sub-sectors by picking entities using criteria under a riskbased sampling methodology"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Where assessment outcomes within a sub-sector are significantly varied, the sub-sector may not be specific enough to be clustered together for supervision purposes; where outcomes are similar, trends can be identified and supervisory strategies can target the entire sub-sector. (p. 62)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Where the outcomes of these assessments are significantly varied, the sub-sector may not be specific enough"</sub>

## 6.4. Poor independent audits of entities

- **expected / recommendation**: Supervisory authorities should assess whether entities have processes to ensure audit scope and depth are appropriate and that audits are performed by competent, qualified and reputable independent auditors, and should satisfy themselves of audit quality, for example by sample checks. Supervisors should confirm the FI's or DNFBP's independent audit plan assesses the effectiveness of AML/CFT controls across and within the entity's or group's operations. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supervisory authorities should assess whether the entities have processes in place to ensure the audit scope and depth is appropriate"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Supervisors should cross-compare findings from supervision activities and independent audits to help detect deficiencies in independent audits and auditors. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Cross-compare findings from supervision activities and independent audit"</sub>

## 6.5. Special considerations for DNFBP supervisors

- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 146 identifies further challenges to risk-based supervision of DNFBPs beyond data collection and risk assessment (section 1.9), including difficulty ensuring an adequate level of DNFBP supervision where risk models and supervisory programmes usually focus on larger FIs such as banks, which is particularly relevant where a single supervisor covers DNFBPs. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Difficulties in ensuring an adequate level of DNFBP supervision"</sub>

## 1.3. Common supervisory frameworks

- **descriptive / context**: Each supervisory model has benefits and challenges regarding resources, expertise, cost efficiency, information availability and national/international co-ordination, and may have intended or unintended effects on effectiveness and resource allocation. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Different models can have intended or unintended consequences on the overall effectiveness and allocation of resources."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Attention given to DNFBP sectors may differ significantly between a jurisdiction with a single AML/CFT supervisor also covering financial sectors and one with a separate DNFBP supervisor. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the level of attention given to DNFBP sectors may vary significantly"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: A comprehensive NRA (or other nationally coordinated ML/TF risk assessment) and meaningful national co-operation are critical so supervisors coordinate and adjust risk assessments and approaches; where functions are split (e.g. FIU, prudential, AML/CFT supervisor) countries must combine information to form an overall risk view. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries need to be able to bring all the relevant information together to reach an overall view of the risks"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Countries should ensure a correlation between sectoral risk and the resources available to the supervisor responsible for that sector. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is a correlation between sectoral risk and resources available to the supervisor responsible for that sector"</sub>

## 1.4. Characteristics of an effective risk-based supervisory framework

- **descriptive / definition**: Under an effective risk-based supervisory framework, the supervisor identifies, assesses and understands ML/TF risks in its sectors and entities and mitigates them on an ongoing basis, using a sound risk assessment system to identify, measure, control and monitor risk and enabling timely intervention on significant risk changes. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "enables the identification, measurement, control and monitoring of ML/TF risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: An effective supervisor understands ML/TF risks at sector and entity level based on assessment of inherent risks and mitigation quality, informed by the national risk assessment. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "based on sound risk assessment of inherent risks and quality of mitigation measures"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: An effective supervisor directs focus to higher or emerging risks while applying risk-based strategies to lower risks without unnecessarily affecting access to and usage of financial services. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "without impacting unnecessarily on access to and usage of financial services"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: An effective supervisor positively influences entity behaviour through targeted guidance and feedback, directing or overseeing remediation, and dissuasive, proportionate enforcement considering risk, context and materiality. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "exercising enforcement powers in a dissuasive and proportionate manner taking risk, context and materiality into account"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: An effective supervisor monitors the evolving risk environment to respond promptly to emerging risks, is adequately resourced with expertise, powers, discretion and tools, and coordinates with the FIU, law enforcement, other supervisors and foreign counterparts, including joint supervisory activities. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Is equipped with the expertise, powers, discretion, and tools needed and adequately resourced"</sub>

## 1.5. Overview of the risk-based supervision process

- **descriptive / method**: The risk-based supervision process has two main components: identifying and understanding risks, and mitigating them. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "(1) identifying and understanding risks, and (2) mitigating those risks"</sub>

## 2. Supervisors’ risk understanding

- **expected / expectation**: To apply risk-based supervision, supervisors should develop, document and update their ML/TF risk understanding via a supervisory risk assessment (SRA). (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisors should develop, document and update their ML/TF risk understanding by undertaking a supervisory risk assessment (SRA)."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The SRA's purpose is to help supervisors plan risk-sensitively by deciding how much attention to give sectors and entities and which risks to prioritise; its scope covers threat, vulnerability and consequence. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The scope of the SRA should cover: threat, vulnerability and consequence"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Following the October 2020 FATF change, supervisors are required to assess supervised entities' PF risk exposure and factor it into risk-based measures. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supervisors are now required to assess how the entities they supervise or monitor are exposed to PF risks"</sub>

## 2.1.1. Sectoral and entity-level risk assessment

- **expected / expectation**: Understanding sector-level inherent risks and common control weaknesses is the starting point for entity-level understanding; supervisors should maintain ongoing risk assessments of sectors and of individual entities and/or groups. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supervisors should establish and maintain ongoing risk assessments of sectors8 and individual entities and/or groups"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: Risk assessment approaches may depend on the supervisory framework and number of sectors and entities; e.g. bank supervisors may assess each bank or group banks with similar size, structure and ML/TF risk exposure, varying supervisory intensity by subgroup. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "group together banks with similar characteristics, including size, structure and ML/TF risk exposure"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: Where justified by risk and materiality, DNFBP supervisors may infer entity-level risk from sector assessments when entity classes can be clearly defined (by activity, business model, customer profile, geographic risk), provided the assessment is nuanced enough per class. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "DNFBP supervisors may determine risks at the entity level bases on risk assessments at the sector level"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Within the TCSP sector, TCSPs acting as formation agents of legal persons may be identified as higher risk than other TCSPs. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "TCSPs that are in the business of acting as a formation agent of legal persons may be identified to be of greater risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Determining a sector's risk requires considering its business models and entities' business and risk profiles (e.g. volume, customer profiles); sub-sector categorisation, such as retail, private and investment banking, can group similar risks. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "sub-sectoral risks may be identified for those providing mainly retail services, private banking or investment banking"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: When deciding on sub-sectoral assessment, supervisors should consider number of entities, nature and variation of activities, business volume, compliance by entity type, and the sector's size relative to others. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the number of entities in a sector, the nature of and variation of business activities"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Risk assessment methodology should consider the jurisdiction's supervisory framework for financial, VASP and DNFBP sectors, since inadequately supervised sectors may add risk to related sectors. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "certain sectors may not be supervised adequately and may therefore introduce additional risks to other related sectors"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: An effective framework requires supervisors to understand their sectors' risk relative to other sectors, otherwise they may over-invest in risks important to them but not to the jurisdiction overall. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they may spend a disproportionate amount of time and effort dealing with a risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Entity-level risk assessments identify standalone risk levels to guide supervisory engagement; inherent risk varies with business type, size, customer profile and exposure to high-risk jurisdictions, and adequate mitigation varies by entity. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "its exposure resulting from doing business with high-risk jurisdictions"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: Entity-level assessments may use transaction and activity data by product, service, customer, channel and geography, compare volumes and activity types with peers to find entities above the sector "average", and analyse operating models, policies, procedures and STRs filed. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "comparing volumes and types of activities against peer entities"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Supervisors often rate the quality of mitigation using ratings weighted and tailored to sectoral and entity-level inherent risks, as not every deficiency is equal. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "not every deficiency is equal"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: A common approach to residual risk rating is an iterative risk matrix with inherent ML/TF risk on one axis and vulnerabilities or quality of AML/CFT mitigation on the other, also considering the probability of ML/TF. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "develop an ongoing and iterative risk matrix with ratings for the inherent ML/TF risks on one axis"</sub>
- **expected / method**: Inherent risk indicators should be tailored to each sector; some apply broadly, others only to particular sectors or sub-sectors. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The risk indicators used to assess inherent risks should be tailored to each sector."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Aggregating entity-level assessments is not the same as a sectoral risk assessment, though it helps identify common risks and reveals deficiencies prompting responses such as new or amended regulations, enhanced measures or supervisory guidance. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Aggregating ML/TF risk assessments of individual entities is not the same as a sectoral risk assessment"</sub>

## 2.1.2. Supervisory risk assessments and the National Risk Assessment

- **descriptive / context**: The interplay between SRAs and the NRA is two-way: supervisors' understanding should feed the NRA, and their risk understanding should be informed by and consistent with the NRA, which brings in other authorities' insights (law enforcement, judicial, customs, FIU, intelligence). (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The interplay between supervisory risk assessment and the NRA process is two-way."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors must develop their own risk understanding; if relying on the NRA for sectoral understanding, they should check it is sufficiently up-to-date and granular for their needs and supplement it as necessary. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supervisors should assess whether the NRA analysis meets their information needs"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: Examples of SRA-NRA interplay: supervisory approach aligned with NRA-identified higher/lower risk activities; revising inherent risk models or control assessments based on NRA risks; ongoing supervision feeding NRA risk identification; and understanding financial inclusion products and exclusion risks to justify exemptions or due diligence levels. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Revision of inherent risk modelling or controls assessment based on identified risks in the NRA."</sub>

## 2.2.1. Assessing inherent risks

- **descriptive / definition**: Inherent risks are ML/TF risks intrinsic to a sector or entity's business before AML/CFT controls, linked to business nature, scale and complexity and to customers, products and services, geographies and delivery channels. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "are ML/TF risks intrinsic to a sector or an entity’s business activities before any AML/CFT controls are applied"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: INR.10 paragraphs 15–17 give examples of higher and lower risk factors, and FATF sectoral RBA guidance and typologies reports can guide inherent risk assessment. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "INR.10 provides some examples of possible higher- and lower-risk factors (see paragraphs 15–17)"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should allocate adequate resources to understand regulated entities' inherent risks, using their own sector knowledge or engaging experts. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisors should allocate adequate resources to ensure a good understanding of the inherent risks"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Under R.1, regulated entities must assess the ML/TF risks facing their businesses; these assessments inform supervisors' view of sector and entity risk by risk category. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "regulated entities must assess the ML/TF risks facing their businesses"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should provide guidance and clarify expectations for entity risk assessments to obtain more organised and informative assessments. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisors should provide guidance and clarify the supervisory expectations for entity risk assessments."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Beyond R.1 risk categories, supervisors should consider other supervisory information, including entity type risks such as the entity's AML/CFT systemic importance to its sector and key financial indicators, applying judgement and contextual factors. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the systemic importance of the entity to the sector in which it operates from the AML/CFT angle"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: An institution aggressively expanding market share or changing its business model may be more risk-tolerant than one with an established lower-risk client base. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An institution that aggressively expands its market share or changes its business model may be more willing to take risks"</sub>
- **permissive / expectation**: For group entities, supervisors may need to consider risks from the wider group, including operational complexity and geographic risks and AML/CFT standards in countries where it operates (see Basel Committee guidelines revised July 2020, paras 63–83, 89 and 90). (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supervisors may also need to consider the risks posed by the other aspects of the group’s business"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Beneficial owners or directors of questionable fitness and propriety may raise concerns about the entity's ability or willingness to implement a sound AML/CFT framework and "tone at the top". (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "beneficial owners or directors of entities whose fitness and propriety are questionable"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: AML/CFT and prudential problems often reinforce each other in troubled institutions: some banks weakened controls to attract illicit funds for liquidity or solvency, and AML/CFT findings causing business loss can create liquidity or solvency problems, especially for small or specialised banks. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "banks have weakened or abandoned their AML/CFT controls in an attempt to attract illicit funds"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Entities serving financially excluded people with adequate mitigation are a relevant factor, since without them risks may shift to the unregulated economy where they are unmitigated. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the risks might be transferred to the unregulated economy where risks are left unmitigated"</sub>
- **permissive / data or feature**: Box 2.1 entity type risk: industry, materiality or market share, operational complexity, business model and strategy (including expansions, mergers and acquisitions), shareholding/beneficial ownership, and key financial indicators such as asset and deposit growth, liquidity and cross-border flows. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "key financial indicators (e.g., asset and deposit growth, liquidity and cross-border flows)"</sub>
- **permissive / data or feature**: Box 2.1 customer risk: demographics, specialised offerings for client groups, natural vs legal persons or legal arrangements, business types served, domestic vs foreign customers, and special categories such as PEPs. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "whether customers are domestic or foreign and whether there are specific categories of customers involved"</sub>
- **permissive / data or feature**: Box 2.1 geographic risk: domestic and international footprint (fund origins/destinations, client location, BO residency), markets served, robustness of foreign AML/CFT frameworks, and contextual factors like corruption, crime or terrorism levels. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "where funds are received from/sent to and where clients are based and residency of beneficial owners"</sub>
- **permissive / data or feature**: Box 2.1 products and services risk: features such as anonymity, transaction volume and speed and contract duration, plus revenues generated from them. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "anonymity, volume and speed of transactions, duration of the contracts"</sub>
- **permissive / data or feature**: Box 2.1 delivery channel risk: ability to reliably identify customers via remote/digital onboarding, delivery solely by post, phone or internet, and use of introducers or intermediaries. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the ability to reliably identify/verify customers through remote or digital onboarding"</sub>
- **permissive / data or feature**: Box 2.1 transactional risk: transaction types and financial flows, and FIU information and analysis on the entity's reporting, which can provide independently verified insight; the list is not comprehensive. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "information and analysis received from the FIU of the transactional reporting from the entity"</sub>

## 2.2.2. Assessing mitigation efforts

- **descriptive / definition**: AML/CFT systems or controls are the measures within an entity to mitigate ML/TF risks. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "are the measures in place within an entity/s to mitigate ML/TF risks"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should look beyond specific controls such as CDD, record keeping and transaction monitoring to assess overall AML/CFT framework effectiveness, including whether corporate governance and compliance culture support sound controls. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "whether the broader corporate governance environment and compliance culture enables sound and effective AML/CFT internal controls"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 13: some jurisdictions assess controls objectively via scoring methodologies, others more subjectively, or with a combination. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "through a scoring methodology while others may do so more subjectively"</sub>
- **expected / method**: Supervisors should proactively monitor controls with a range of tools, such as periodic sector-wide collection of key control information to spot major deficiencies or thematic weaknesses. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "periodic collection of information on the key AML/CFT controls across the sector"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: Data analytics on STRs filed by supervised entities can identify potential control weaknesses at specific entities, supporting targeted scrutiny or broader sector guidance. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the use of data analytics to analyse suspicious transaction reports filed by supervised entities"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should holistically assess entity controls, paying attention to board and management oversight, since boards and management are ultimately responsible for AML/CFT controls. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisors should develop a holistic assessment of the AML/CFT systems or controls within an entity"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Many recent large-scale AML/CFT compliance failures occurred with the will or knowledge of top management, boards and sometimes owners, or from inadequate oversight. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Many of the large-scale AML/CFT compliance failures in recent years occurred either with the will or knowledge of top management"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors must understand the risk appetite of owners, board and management, using board minutes, policies and exchanges with prudential/conduct supervisors, and should meet and assess the competency of senior management, board, owners and non-executive directors. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is important to meet with and assess the competency of senior management, board, owners and non-executive directors"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: Monitoring open source information and risk-appetite indicators such as aggressive expansion, and developing risk indicators, may help identify wilful or reckless defiance of AML/CFT obligations; group-level supervision helps understand group dynamics and risk tolerance. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may assist supervisors in identifying wilful or reckless defiance of AML/CFT obligations"</sub>

## Box 2.2. Assessing entities’ AML/CFT systems and controls

- **expected / expectation**: Box 2.2: to assess controls holistically, supervisors should consider adequacy of board and senior management oversight, qualified staff numbers with authority and resources, policies (and conflicts like turnover-based pay), risk management and compliance functions, internal controls, information systems management, independent internal/external audit, and AML/CFT training. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "conflicts with other policies and procedures, e.g., remuneration based on turnover"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The Box 2.2 list is non-exhaustive and must be adapted to entity size, e.g. small businesses without a board or separate compliance function. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "small businesses who may not have a board or separate compliance function"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: When assessing mitigation of inherent risks, supervisors should consider jurisdiction- and sector-specific risks and the size and characteristics of supervised entities. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supervisors should consider risks specific to their jurisdiction and sectors they oversee"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Singapore's NRA identified trade-based money laundering, abuse of legal persons and corruption as key FI risks; MAS built inherent risk indicators around them to collect FI information and assess controls. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Singapore’s NRA identified trade-based money laundering, abuse of legal persons and corruption"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: German supervisors assess the appropriateness of an institution's transaction monitoring system based on criteria such as business model and transaction volume. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supervisors assess the appropriateness of an institution´s transaction monitoring system"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Assessment of an entity's mitigation should rest on the supervisor's own interactions and knowledge, supplemented by third-party work only where the supervisor is comfortable with its robustness and it does not contradict its own understanding. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisors should only place reliance on such third-party work to the extent that it is comfortable"</sub>
- **permissive / data or feature**: Third-party work may include reports by external auditors, the FIU, foreign supervisors and home supervisors of foreign entities; where legally permitted, supervisors may hire third parties for targeted AML/CFT reviews or audits. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a supervisor might hire a third party to conduct targeted AML/CFT reviews or audits on their behalf"</sub>
- **permissive / data or feature**: Financial-sector AML/CFT supervisors with access to prudential or conduct work may consider governance across the 'three lines of defence', operational controls and data quality; such work is especially useful where it reveals inconsistent views on governance between supervisors. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the quality of governance and oversight across the ‘three lines of defence’"</sub>

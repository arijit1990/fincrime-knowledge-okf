## 6.4. Poor independent audits of entities

- **descriptive / context**: FATF identifies as a DNFBP supervision challenge the difficulty of ensuring supervisors are specialists, or adequately trained, experienced and knowledgeable, given the very diverse activities of supervised entities. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Difficulties in ensuring supervisors are specialists and/or sufficiently trained, experienced and knowledgeable"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF notes that DNFBP supervisors, especially self-regulatory bodies, may lack full legal authority to supervise every entity in the sector. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may not have full legal authority to carry out supervision on all entities within the sector"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: FATF suggests (para 147) intensive outreach and engagement with and through sectoral associations, which need not be the self-regulatory bodies, including providing DNFBP sector-specific typologies. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Intensive outreach and engagement with and via sectoral associations"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: FATF suggests comprehensive training for supervisors on the business models and activities of the different DNFBP sub-sectors. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Comprehensive training for supervisors on the business models and activities of the various DNFBP sub-sectors"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: FATF suggests ensuring random, reactive and event-driven supervisory activity gives enough coverage of DNFBP subsectors that are not in cyclical on-site programmes. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Ensuring that random, reactive and event-driven supervisory activity provides sufficient coverage across DNFBP subsectors"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: FATF suggests defining a supervisory strategy adapted to the sector and to the degree of risk entities present. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Defining a strategy which is adapted to the sector and degree of risk presented by entities"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Per section 5.8, supervisors may begin with a relatively simple risk-based strategy (e.g. driven by broad indicators of inherent subsector risk) and add complexity as better data and more supervisory engagement allow effective consideration of control frameworks and residual risk in individual entities. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supervisors may initially implement a relatively simple risk-based supervisory strategy"</sub>

## 6.6. Role of self-regulatory bodies for DNFPBs

- **permissive / context**: Under the FATF Standards a jurisdiction may assign all or some supervisory tasks to self-regulatory bodies (SRBs) of DNFBPs, except for casinos; FATF cautions this may not be optimal for all jurisdictions. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may decide to assign all or some of supervisory tasks and responsibilities to self-regulatory bodies (SRBs)"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: FATF notes SRBs generally may lack the powers and tools of government supervisors, especially sanctioning power. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "SRBs may lack the power and the tools of government supervisory agencies, particularly the sanctioning power"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: FATF notes some SRBs may face conflict of interest and independence issues, particularly where they depend on membership fee income. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "particularly where SRBs are dependent upon membership fee income"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: FATF notes many SRBs have serious human resource and other capacity constraints, or lack adequate focus, training or experience on AML/CFT. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "many SRBs have serious human resources and other capacity constraints"</sub>

## 149. Strategies to address this challenge:

- **expected / recommendation**: FATF says designation of AML/CFT supervisory authorities should carefully analyse SRB limitations before deciding the SRBs' supervisory role; a jurisdiction may instead give SRBs a complementary role such as market entry controls, awareness raising, training and guidance. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the role of the SRBs can be more complementary in nature"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: FATF says if an SRB is chosen as supervisor, laws and regulations need to be drafted or amended to give it the necessary powers and tools and to deal with conflict of interest situations. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "If an SRB is chosen as a supervisor laws and regulations need to be drafted/amended"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: FATF says a competent authority should exercise some level of oversight or supervision over the AML/CFT work of SRBs. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There should be some level of oversight/supervision by a competent authority over the AML/CFT work of SRBs"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In the UK, OPBAS was established to supervise SRBs designated as DNFBP supervisors under the Money Laundering Regulations, to ensure a consistent AML/CFT supervisory approach across DNFBP sectors and assess whether SRBs meet their legislative obligations. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In the UK, OPBAS was set up as a supervisor of SRBs designated as DNFBP supervisors"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: FATF reports that significant progress has been made under OPBAS though further effectiveness improvements remain; OPBAS is delivering its second phase of supervisory work and expected to publish its third report in 2021. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "OPBAS continues to deliver its second phase of supervisory work and expects to publish its third report in 2021"</sub>

## 6.7. Lack of clarity in the division of supervisory roles and responsibilities

- **descriptive / context**: FATF observes that many jurisdictions lack clarity on division of AML/CFT supervisory responsibilities, especially between the FIU and other supervisors, between prudential and AML/CFT supervisors, or among AML/CFT supervisors covering different aspects of one entity, so the primary responsible agency is unclear. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is not always clear which agency has the primary role and responsibility for AML/CFT supervision"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: FATF suggests that ideally the law should clearly identify the agency with primary AML/CFT supervisory responsibility for a sector, addressing ambiguities and eliminating overlaps and conflicts between AML/CFT laws and sectoral supervision laws as necessary. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Ideally, the law should clearly identify which agency has the primary responsibility of AML/CFT supervision of a sector"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: FATF notes memoranda of understanding can help define authorities' respective roles and principles for collaboration and information sharing; clear division is especially essential where a multinational and/or federal authority supervises domestic or local entities. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "memoranda of understandings can help define the respective roles the authorities"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: FATF suggests setting up mechanisms for co-operation and a consistent approach between agencies, with information flowing freely and in a timely manner. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "ensure that information flows freely and in a timely manner"</sub>

## 6.8. Zero-tolerance or zero-failure approach

- **descriptive / context**: FATF states that a zero-tolerance approach which tolerates no imperfections, especially in lower-risk areas, is counterproductive for an effective AML/CFT system and risk-based supervision, at both supervisor and entity level. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is counterproductive to an effective AML/CFT system and for risk-based supervision"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF notes institutional support for a risk-based approach can be hard to build due to fear of missing compliance failures in lower-risk areas, and the approach requires deep sector knowledge, critical thinking and subjective supervisory judgment. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "fears of missing compliance failures in areas deemed as lower risk"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: Per section 3.7, supervisors may validly take remedial or other action across the risk spectrum, e.g. where failure stems from repeated, knowing or wilful non-compliance with AML/CFT requirements. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "repeated, knowing or wilful non-compliance with AML/CFT requirements"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: FATF warns that at entity level a zero tolerance approach could cause indiscriminate cutting loose of whole classes of customers without serious and comprehensive consideration of individual customers' risk and mitigation measures. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could lead to indiscriminate cutting loose of entire classes of customer"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: FATF says supervisors, especially in early stages of implementing an AML/CFT risk-based approach, should explain the approach to their regulated population and provide guidance on how it should be applied. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supervisors should explain the approach to their regulatory population"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: FATF says supervisors should seek high-level internal support for their supervisory strategies by explaining the rationale and demonstrating the benefits of the approach. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supervisors should seek high-level support for their supervisory strategies by explaining its rationale"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: FATF considers developing supervisory risk statements and frameworks with senior management sign-off an appropriate strategy. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "senior management sign-off of supervisory risk statements and frameworks"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: FATF says supervisors should introduce the RBA gradually and give the sector more flexibility as its expertise and risk assessment capability grow. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisors should introduce the RBA gradually, and give greater flexibility to the sector"</sub>

## 6.9. Integrated vs. Standalone AML/CFT Supervision

- **descriptive / context**: FATF notes some agencies run dedicated AML/CFT supervision programmes and teams while others integrate AML/CFT supervision into general or prudential supervision; both have pros and cons. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "conduct their AML/CFT supervision as an (integrated) part of general or prudential supervision program"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: FATF notes that in integrated supervision, on-site plans may depend heavily on prudential risks, leaving prudentially sound entities with higher ML/TF risks out of inspection plans, contrary to the RBA. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "leaving prudentially sound entities with higher ML/TF risks out of the inspection plan"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: FATF notes that standalone AML/CFT supervision often makes co-ordination and collaboration with prudential supervisors challenging. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "co-ordination and collaboration with the prudential supervisors and other aspects of supervision is often challenging"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: FATF says authorities choosing integrated, standalone or combined approaches should carefully weigh their advantages and disadvantages, and refers to Basel Committee guidance on coordination between AML/CFT and prudential supervision. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "authorities should carefully consider these advantages and disadvantages"</sub>

## 6.8. Zero-tolerance or zero-failure approach

- **expected / expectation**: FATF says supervisors should make clear that it is inappropriate to indiscriminately terminate or restrict business relationships of entire classes of customer, without seriously and comprehensively taking into account the risk level and mitigation measures of individual customers within a sector. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is inappropriate to indiscriminately terminate or restrict business relationships of entire classes of customer"</sub>

## Table 6.1. World Bank comparison of integrated and stand-alone inspections

- **descriptive / finding or result**: World Bank Table 6.1 lists, for integrated AML/CFT inspection by general or prudential supervisors, pros (all supervisors gain AML/CFT experience; smoother co-ordination) and cons (prudential risks determine the inspection plan, supervisors may see AML/CFT as secondary, and specialisation and depth may remain limited). (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisors may tend to see the AML/CFTas a secondary issue compared toprudential risks."</sub>

## 6.9. Integrated vs. Standalone AML/CFT Supervision

- **recommended / recommendation**: When choosing integrated or standalone AML/CFT supervision, or a combination, authorities should carefully consider the advantages and disadvantages of each approach. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "authorities should carefully consider these advantages and disadvantages"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: FATF says standalone AML/CFT teams should seek input from other supervisory areas when forming sector risk assessments and identifying entity-specific risks, e.g. cyber-security or client assets concerns that may indicate vulnerability to exploitation by financial criminals. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Standalone AML/CFT supervision teams should seek input from other supervision areas"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: FATF says AML supervision teams should share offsite and onsite supervisory findings with prudential supervisors, as major AML/CFT issues may cause or indicate critical prudential concerns. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "major AML/CFT issues may lead to or indicate critical prudential concerns"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: FATF says AML/CFT supervision should be driven by ML/TF risks rather than prudential or conduct risks, though the latter may inform understanding of ML/TF risk. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AML/CFT supervision should be driven by ML/TF risks rather than prudential or conduct risks"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: FATF notes jurisdictions with dedicated AML/CFT teams may categorise resources into high risk, medium risk, low risk and responsive supervision plus risk analysis/data collection/horizon scanning, or split by intervention type (on-site and off-site), depending on size, characteristics and risks of the population. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "1) high risk supervision 2) medium risk supervision 3) low risk supervision 4) responsive supervision"</sub>

## 6.10. Risk-based supervision strategies should be up-to-date and dynamic

- **descriptive / context**: FATF notes that with advances in finance and technology risks change faster than before, and outdated assessments can undermine risk-based supervision; per section 2.4 risk assessments should be kept under review so resources target the highest risks. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Outdated assessments can undermine risk-based supervision"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: FATF says supervisory authorities should be fast and agile in understanding risks, where possible using SupTech to monitor risks in real time or continuously, and be flexible to adapt plans promptly to emerging ML/TF risks. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "take the advantage of SupTech in monitoring the risks in real time/on a continuous basis"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF notes on-site inspections are challenging and resource intensive where businesses outside the country operate within the regulatory perimeter (e.g. online services) or entity functions sit in different locations (e.g. groups), and external factors such as the pandemic can prevent them. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "on-site inspections are challenging and resource intensive"</sub>

## 6.11. Logistical challenges in performing on-site inspections

- **recommended / recommendation**: FATF suggests using tools such as video-conferencing to simulate on-site testing while ensuring adequate vigour and spontaneity. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Utilise tools such as video-conferencing to simulate the types of testing that would occur at an on-site inspection"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The UK Gambling Commission supervises online casinos serving the UK using multi-day Microsoft Teams assessments with key individuals, real-time data viewing and system interrogation; materials (risk assessment, policies, procedures, controls) are reviewed beforehand and the customer accounts to be assessed are usually only named during the live assessment. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Microsoft Teams assessments over a number of days with key individuals"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The UK Gambling Commission requires annual assurance statements from its highest impact operators, covering around 90% of the market, and asks entities to complete 'calls for information'. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "annual assurance statements from highest impact operators that cover around 90% of the market"</sub>

## 7.1.1. Belgium

- **descriptive / case example**: In Belgium, the National Bank of Belgium (NBB) is the AML/CFT supervisor for banks, life-insurance undertakings, investment firms, and payment and e-money institutions, and uses three tools centred on an annual AML/CFT questionnaire. (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The NBB makes use of three tools centred on information from an Annual AML/CFT questionnaire"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The NBB's periodic AML/CFT questionnaire captures each entity's ML/TF risk environment: inherent risk, vulnerability, and completeness and effectiveness of mitigating measures. (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the inherent AML/CFT risk it faces, its vulnerability to these risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The NBB developed four subsector questionnaires (banking, securities, insurance, payment) while keeping them consistent and comparable; they are published on the NBB website. (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "four different questionnaires have been developed for each subsector"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The NBB's Automated QLB Response Analysis Tool ("FRA") automatically analyses and scores questionnaire responses, assigning each financial institution a High, Medium High, Medium Low or Low risk profile. (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Automated QLB Response Analysis Tool (“FRA”), which assigns one of the following"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The FRA lets the NBB visualise and compare questionnaire responses across all or groups of institutions, and in future will enable comparisons over time. (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In future, it will also enable comparisons over time"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The FRA also supports ad hoc institution-by-institution analysis, using visualisation to quickly identify main risks and internal procedure shortcomings revealed in the questionnaire's second part on control environment weaknesses. (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "carry out ad hoc analyses on an institution-by-institution basis"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: FRA risk profiles are based solely on each institution's questionnaire responses and are therefore influenced by the quality of those responses. (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These profiles are therefore influenced by the quality of these responses"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The FRA does not incorporate other information such as institutions' overall risk assessment reporting, the AMLCO annual report, internal audit reports, results of prior off-site and on-site supervision, information from other national or foreign supervisors, prudential information, CTIF/CFI information on reporting intensity and quality, legal authorities' information on investigations or prosecutions, and reliable public information. (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "information provided by CTIF/CFI, particularly in relation to the intensity and quality of the reporting"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: FRA risk profiles also exclude subjective elements such as the expertise, transparency or reliability of the AMLCO or managers and overall "supervisory judgement". (p. 71)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "are not taken into account in the risk profiles allocated by FRA"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The NBB built a "Scorecarding" tool into which FRA results are transferred, letting supervisory staff integrate the additional information and modify, refine or correct the automated risk profile where necessary. (p. 71)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the NBB has developed an additional tool called "Scorecarding""</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The NBB's Scorecarding tool was expected to be fully operational in 2020, after IT developments and after FRA profiles from questionnaires submitted by 30 June 2019 are supplemented with external information and staff analysis. (p. 71)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This “Scorecarding” tool should be fully operational in 2020"</sub>

## 7.1.3. Germany (BaFin) – entity-level risk ratings

- **descriptive / case example**: BaFin assigns each supervised entity to a risk class taking into account both the institution's individual abstract risk situation and the quality of its AML/CFT measures and safeguards. (p. 72)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "BaFin introduced a system of risk classification for AML/CFT supervision"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: BaFin assesses the quality of AML/CFT measures by rating and scoring fulfilment of duties in different categories, checking several sector-tailored individual criteria for the overall rating. (p. 72)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the fulfilment of duties from different categories are rated and scored"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: BaFin's scoring uses different multipliers for preventive measure categories, weighting substantial deficiencies much more than average or low ones, so the more a deficiency impairs a safeguard the lower the rating. (p. 72)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "giving substantial deficiencies much more weight for the rating than average or low deficiencies"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: BaFin's rating procedure accounts for missing or unclear findings in annual audit reports, which create uncertainty in evaluating risk management quality. (p. 72)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The rating procedure takes into account lacking and unclear findings in the annual audit reports"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: BaFin combines the ML/TF threat rating and the AML/CFT prevention quality rating in a matrix to allocate each entity a final risk class that determines supervisory intensity and scope. (p. 72)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "have to be combined (matrix) and each entity has to be allocated to a risk class"</sub>

## 7.1.4. Ireland: AML/CFT risk model and AML/CFT Supervisory Strategy

## Risk assessment model and risk analysis

- **descriptive / case example**: The Central Bank of Ireland became AML/CFT competent authority for financial institutions in July 2010 and determined that PRISM prudential risk ratings were not appropriate ML/TF risk indicators because they are based on the impact of an institution's failure on financial stability. (p. 73)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Central Bank became competent authority for AML/CFT supervision of financial institutions in July 2010"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Central Bank of Ireland found an institution can be low risk prudentially yet high ML/TF risk; e.g. money remittance firms may not be high impact prudentially but their jurisdictional reach and services can make them high ML/TF risk. (p. 73)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "while a money remittance firm may not be considered a high impact firm under prudential risk ratings"</sub>

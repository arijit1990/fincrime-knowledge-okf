## 7.1.2. France

- **descriptive / case example**: The Central Bank of Ireland devised a separate ML/TF risk assessment model to assess financial institutions by ML/TF risk and inform its AML/CFT supervisory strategy, setting out inherent risks and overall controls and mitigants per sector. (p. 73)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Central Bank devised a separate risk assessment model that would assess financial institutions based on their ML/TF risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The Central Bank of Ireland's sector ML/TF Risk Assessment is not mechanistic: beyond sector vulnerability it draws on engagement with other agencies, e.g. intelligence from law enforcement and the Revenue Commissioners on threats and vulnerabilities. (p. 73)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this is not a mechanistic process"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The Central Bank of Ireland assigns sectors to four ML/TF risk categories (high, medium-high, medium-low, low), and AMLD created an ultra-high sub-category within high to inform its inspections strategy. (p. 73)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AMLD has created within the high category an ultra-high risk sub-category"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The Central Bank of Ireland's supervisory risk model considers both inherent and residual risk: high inherent risk signals need for closer attention, while residual risk influences supervisory intensity/scope and can prioritise between entities. (p. 73)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The residual risk rating influences the intensity/scope of supervision"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Under the Central Bank of Ireland's ML/TF risk model, inherent risk carries 80% of the overall risk score weighting and is the main driver of the rating. (p. 73)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "inherent risk carries 80% weight of the overall risk score"</sub>

## 7.1.5. Russia: Assessing entity level risks

- **descriptive / case example**: In 2013 the Bank of Russia's mandate was extended to non-credit financial institutions (securities market participants, asset managers, insurers, non-state pension funds, microfinance organisations, consumer credit cooperatives, agricultural consumer credit cooperatives, pawnshops), to be supervised quickly despite scarce resources, lack of information and an insufficient regulatory framework. (p. 75)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In 2013, the Bank of Russia’s supervisory mandate and powers were extended to a range of non-credit financial institutions"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The Bank of Russia filled information gaps on non-credit financial institutions by analysing their transactions through their bank accounts, largely visible via its payment system, as a strategic 'bridge' to launch AML/CFT supervision of them. (p. 75)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "non-credit financial institutions are always customers of banks"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The Bank of Russia's principal entity-level risk criteria are: involvement in complex or unusual transactions with no apparent economic or lawful purpose; level of technical compliance with AML/CFT legislation; and effectiveness and efficiency of the AML/CFT system. (p. 75)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "involved in transactions of a complex or unusual nature that have no apparent economic or visible lawful purpose"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The Bank of Russia's additional criteria include information from Rosfinmonitoring, law enforcement and tax authorities; prudential information (legislative breaches, opaque business model or transactions, weak financial resilience, heightened risk to lenders and depositors); and information from foreign supervisors via AML/CFT co-operation. (p. 75)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "information provided to the Bank of Russia by FIU (Rosfinmonitoring), law enforcement and tax authorities"</sub>

## 7.1.6. Kingdom of Saudi Arabia

- **descriptive / case example**: SAMA's AML/CFT Department uses a Risk Matrix Tool rating each financial institution's ML/TF risk as Very High, High, Upper Medium, Lower Medium or Low, based on residual risk after weighing internal controls against inherent risks plus the institution's impact on the Saudi financial sector. (p. 75)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Very High, High, Upper Medium, Lower Medium, or Low"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: SAMA Risk Matrix element 1 assesses inherent risk quantitatively using structural data (branches, employees, customers, transaction volume) and business risk factors (high risk customers, products and services, geography, delivery channels), each criterion weighted by importance. (p. 75)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "number of branches, number of employees, number of customers, and volume of transaction"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: SAMA Risk Matrix element 2 assesses the institution's internal controls, weighting each criterion to determine control effectiveness. (p. 75)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a weight is given to each criterion to determine the effectiveness of the internal controls implemented"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: SAMA Risk Matrix element 3 calculates residual risk by deducting the internal control ratio from inherent risk. (p. 75)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the residual risk is determined by deducting the internal control ratio from the inherent risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: SAMA Risk Matrix element 4 measures the institution's impact on the financial sector and sector ML/TF risk using two factors: asset size and reputation in the sector. (p. 75)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the size of the assets and the financial institution's reputation in the financial sector"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: SAMA updates institutional risk profiles from Risk Matrix outcomes, inspection and compliance reports, media news and trigger events (change in size, merger or acquisition, ownership change, new product or service), driving risk-sensitive inspection planning, frequency, intensity, scope, off-site supervision and inspection mechanism. (p. 75)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "change in the size of the company, merger or acquisition, changes in ownership"</sub>

## Planning on-site visits on a risk sensitive basis

- **descriptive / method**: SAMA's Supervisory Prioritizing Tool organises inspections and follow-ups on a risk-sensitive basis using risk assessment outcomes, available resources and trigger events. (p. 76)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Saudi Central Bank has developed the Supervisory Prioritizing Tool"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: SAMA's prioritisation score per institution is based on date of last visit, the institution's risk assessment result and the sector's risk; a higher score means higher visit priority and intensity. (p. 76)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This score is determined by the date of last visit"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In Saudi Arabia, higher ML/TF risk sectors have a dedicated AML/CFT on-site plan independent of prudential supervision, led by AML/CFT supervisors with prudential participation; lower risk sectors are inspected by prudential supervisors with AML/CFT participation, using fewer AML/CFT resources. (p. 76)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is a dedicated AML/CFT on-site inspection plan that is independent from the prudential supervision"</sub>

## Use of technology to risk-rate entities

- **descriptive / context**: FATF notes inherent risk assessment of banks typically relies on regular aggregate data collection, often labour intensive and time consuming, involving desktop peer comparison and considerable qualitative judgement. (p. 76)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is often a labour intensive and time-consuming process"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Singapore's supervisors worked with data professionals to compile ML/TF risk indicators, designed a machine-readable data collection form and developed a consistently applied risk scoring methodology. (p. 76)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "designed a form to collect the required data in machine readable format"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In Singapore each FI's inherent ML/TF risk rating and key risk driver report can now be generated quickly once data arrives, improving targeting of higher risk FIs and enabling more timely intervention where unexpected material year-on-year risk changes appear. (p. 76)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "together with a report on their key risk drivers can be quickly generated once data is received"</sub>

## Use of technology in risk surveillance

- **descriptive / method**: FATF notes techniques such as network link analysis in ML/TF risk surveillance could improve analysis of risk-relevant data and yield supervisory insight. (p. 77)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Applying techniques such as network link analysis in ML/TF risk surveillance"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Singapore supervisors built a network analysis tool detecting entities and individuals connected across STRs filed over different periods by different regulated entities, enriched with transaction data (size, counterparties) and corporate registry profiles (business activities, key appointment holders), to identify higher risk activities and FIs. (p. 77)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "detect networks of entities and individuals who are connected across different STRs"</sub>

## Use of technology to inform on-site inspections

- **descriptive / method**: Singapore supervisors use an automated analytical tool during inspections that examines the inspected entity's entire transaction pool over 2-3 years at the outset to target unusual accounts and transactions, including unfiled STRs, removing the need for manual review of sampled accounts. (p. 77)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "examines the inspected entity’s entire pool of transactions over 2-3"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Singapore inspection analytics tool made inspections much more risk-targeted and enabled deeper dialogue with FI senior management on risk governance, culture and controls framed around actual case examples. (p. 77)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "facilitated deeper dialogues with senior management of the FI on their risk governance"</sub>

## resource

- **descriptive / case example**: The UK FCA completed a proof of concept of a data and analytics tool to help identify firms needing offsite and onsite attention based on indicators collected from regulated firms through returns. (p. 78)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "successful completion of a proof of concept stage of a data & analytics tool"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The FCA tool is at an early development stage needing further work to embed into business-as-usual; it is expected to combine data with supervisory judgement and intelligence to focus on large and small firms with exploitable vulnerabilities, and the FCA is consulting on extending financial crime reporting to more firms. (p. 78)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "consulting on the extension of financial crime reporting requirements to more supervised firms"</sub>

## 7.2.3. Brazil: Off-site supervisory reviews

- **descriptive / case example**: Brazil applies SupTech to review policies, procedures and controls, analyse customised data, test samples and interview management, making off-site reviews more intrusive, and cross-verifies entity data against other supervisory data and public information. (p. 78)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "New technologies are also used to cross-verify data provided by entities"</sub>

## 7.2.4. Mexico: SupTech Inspection Tool

- **descriptive / case example**: Since January 2019 Mexico's financial institutions supervisor has used a SupTech inspection tool to receive client and transaction databases, alerts and reports, validating them via APIs in hours instead of days. (p. 78)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "validate this information in a matter of hours instead of days"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Mexico's SupTech tool uses machine learning to identify risk patterns and unusual scenarios based on supervisor-set risk criteria, supporting recommendations to optimise entities' warning systems, peer comparison with the sector, and reports for supervision and AML/CTF policy. (p. 78)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the tool utilised machine learning to identify risk patterns and unusual scenarios"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Mexico's tool analyses large inspection datasets which can exceed up to ten million records, summarises results in dashboards and helps select and review client files. (p. 78)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could exceed up to ten million records"</sub>

## 7.2.5. Tunisia: Use of Blockchain technology to assess cross-border cash transportation risks and target supervision of entities

- **descriptive / case example**: After the 2017 Tunisian National Risk Assessment identified cross-border cash transportation and smuggling as high risk, the FIU, Central Bank, Customs and Ministry of Interior with banks and exchange offices built a blockchain platform called "Hannibal" to gather, store and analyse related data. (p. 79)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "developed a national platform using the blockchain technology called “Hannibal”"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Hannibal produces dynamic dashboards on cross-border cash ML/TF risks, helps the FIU, LEAs, banks and exchange offices detect cash courier networks, and lets the Central Bank identify higher risk banks and exchange offices to target supervision. (p. 79)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identify and detect networks of cash couriers"</sub>

## 7.3.1. Saudi Arabia: Ongoing engagement with private sector

  <sub>anchor: "maintain an ongoing engagement with the private sector on a monthly basis through a number of permanent committees"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The Saudi committees help supervisors understand private sector risks and let industry share developments and best practice, discuss common risks, raise awareness of emerging risks and produce consolidated recommendations. (p. 79)[^original]

## 7.3.2.Russia: Know Your Customer database

- **descriptive / case example**: In October 2019 the Bank of Russia approved a SupTech and RegTech roadmap of major projects aimed at reducing regulatory burden and improving internal processes including monitoring. (p. 79)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "roadmap for the SupTech and RegTech solutions of the Bank of Russia"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The Bank of Russia's planned KYC Service will analyse large data in real time to give daily ML/TF risk assessments of each FI's customers (excluding natural persons) as high, medium or low, for FIs to use in compliance procedures; it was planned to start in 2021. (p. 79)[^original]

## 7.3.3. United States: Public-private partnerships

- **descriptive / case example**: FinCEN shares risk and trend information via channels including the Bank Secrecy Act Advisory Group and public and confidential advisories on illicit finance trends and threats. (p. 80)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These include the Bank Secrecy Act Advisory Group"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In 2017 FinCEN established FinCEN Exchange, a voluntary public-private information sharing partnership among law enforcement, FIs and FinCEN, convened on issues including illicit virtual currency use, ransomware and business email compromise. (p. 80)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In 2017, FinCEN built upon these existing initiatives by establishing FinCEN Exchange"</sub>
- **descriptive / requirement**: FinCEN Exchange was codified in January 2021 by the Anti-Money Laundering Act of 2020, which also permits discretionary information sharing with appropriate Federal financial institution regulators. (p. 80)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FinCEN Exchange was codified in January 2021 with the passage of the Anti-Money Laundering Act of 2020"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Treasury's Office of Terrorist Financing and Financial Crimes (TFFC) convenes multilateral and bilateral public-private dialogues and publishes national risk assessments on ML, TF and PF. (p. 80)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "publishes national risk assessments on ML, TF, and PF"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: US law enforcement (FBI, Homeland Security Investigations) engages FIs on ML typologies, methods, trends and, where appropriate, investigations; federal banking agencies, SEC, CFTC and SROs publish ML/TF risk guidance. (p. 80)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "regularly engage U.S. FIs and others on ML typologies, methods, and trends"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: FATF reports US public-private information sharing supports more and higher-quality SARs, helps law enforcement and FIs prioritise, and helps supervisors understand trends and assess in examinations whether institutions know, assess and mitigate these risks. (p. 80)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supports more, and higher-quality, suspicious activity reports"</sub>

## 7.4.1. Brazil: IT systems for ongoing monitoring and engagement

- **descriptive / case example**: The Central Bank of Brazil applies Conduct Continuous Monitoring (ACC) to the highest risk institutions, with at least one supervisor continuously assessing each one's governance, risk management and compliance, giving an updated risk profile. (p. 81)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Conduct Continuous Monitoring (ACC) for the highest risk financial institutions"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: For less risky banks and non-banks, BCB uses the APS-Siscom SupTech system for remote compliance inspections (ICR) of AML/CFT policies, procedures and controls, with all interactions and document requests handled through it. (p. 81)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "remote compliance inspections (ICR) assess AML / CFT policies, procedures and internal controls"</sub>
- **permissive / case example**: If an ICR detects a breach, the institution must present an action plan approved and tracked via APS-Siscom; depending on outcomes the supervisor may propose remedial actions or sanctions including termination of the business. (p. 81)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the supervisor may propose remedial actions or sanctions, including termination of the business"</sub>

## 7.5.1. Argentina: Co-operation between the FIU and financial sector supervisors

- **descriptive / case example**: Argentina's FIU oversees AML/CFT supervision of financial and DNFBP sectors and collaborates with the Central Bank, National Securities Commission, National Insurance Authority and National Supervisor for Co-operatives and Mutual Associations. (p. 81)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Argentina’s FIU oversees AML/CFT supervision of the financial and DNFBP sectors"</sub>

  <sub>anchor: "The FIU approves the risk matrices used by the financial sector supervisors"</sub>

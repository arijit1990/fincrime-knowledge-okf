## Box 3.6. AML/CFT supervisory colleges in the European Union

- **descriptive / context**: EU AML/CFT and prudential legislation obliges competent authorities to co-operate and exchange information but does not detail how, which can make prudential/AML-CFT co-operation difficult. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it does not set out in detail how this should be achieved"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The European Supervisory Authorities issued Guidelines on supervisory co-operation and information exchange in December 2019 setting rules for establishing and operating AML/CFT colleges. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Guidelines on supervisory co-operation and information exchange in December 2019"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: AML/CFT colleges, like prudential colleges, are forums for collaboration and information exchange that build a common understanding of an institution's ML/TF risks and allow supervisors to adopt a common approach and coordinated actions. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AML/CFT colleges serve as a forum for collaboration and exchange of information"</sub>
- **descriptive / requirement**: Under the ESA Guidelines, AML/CFT colleges are set up for all banks and financial institutions operating in at least three EU member states, with all involved EU AML/CFT supervisors as permanent members. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "be set up for all banks and financial institutions that operate in at least three EU member states"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: EU prudential supervisors and AML/CFT supervisors of non-EU countries where the institution operates are invited as observers; non-EU prudential supervisors and the FIU of the lead supervisor's member state may also be invited; other FIUs and relevant persons may join ad hoc. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "are invited to participate in the AML/CFT college as observers"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: College observers must be subject to confidentiality rules equivalent to the EU's and are expected to actively exchange information; prudential observers should ensure college information is shared with and acted on by prudential colleges. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "All observers have to be subject to confidentiality rules equivalent to those in force in the EU."</sub>

## 4.1. Use of technology by supervisors (“SupTech”)

- **descriptive / context**: The SupTech section shares supervisors' experiences with technology and does not advocate any specific mandatory tools. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It does not advocate any specific technological tools which must be adopted for supervision."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: New data sources and advanced analytics help supervisors detect and mitigate ML/TF risk more efficiently, including in collecting, storing, analysing and transforming supervisory data. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "collecting, storing, analysing and transforming supervisory data to sharpen risk assessment"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Technology can automate routine processes, freeing resources for tasks requiring human judgement, expertise and experience. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Technologies can automate routine processes and free up valuable supervisory resources"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Data processing, network-linked analysis, robotic process automation, machine learning and AI can yield supervisory insights and identify risk trends across sectors and entity groups. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "network-linked analysis techniques, robotic process automation, machine learning and artificial intelligence"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Supervisors with access to a larger pool of information than any single entity should not act as an FIU, but technology enabling system-wide risk analysis should be shared with other agencies and, as appropriate, the private sector. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "while it should not perform the role of an FIU, technology that enables analysis of system wide risk should be shared"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: SupTech applications include risk assessment of entities and sectors, system-wide risk surveillance supporting activity-focused supervision alongside entity-focused supervision, and machine-assisted analysis of large datasets in on-site/off-site reviews. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supporting activity-focused supervision to augment entity-focused supervision"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: Technology can deepen collaboration among regulators, law enforcement and regulated entities; where entities use technology for AML/CFT or offer technology-based services, effective supervision requires understanding that use and its impact. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "effective supervision also necessitates good understanding of the use of technology by these entities"</sub>
- **mandatory / limitation**: Supervisors must consider SupTech risks: amplified cyber and operational risks, over-reliance on tech models, reputational risk from incorrect algorithms causing wrong assessments, cost/benefit and data availability constraints, and insufficient scale in decentralised systems. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "over- reliance on tech-models and reputational risks (if incorrect algorithms are input"</sub>
- **expected / method**: Technological solutions need periodic effectiveness review; mitigations such as running new technology in parallel with existing processes for a reasonable period should be evaluated to manage residual risk. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "running new technology in parallel form to the existing process for a reasonable period of time"</sub>

## 4.2. Engagement with the private-sector

- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should maintain ongoing engagement with the private sector, which, through direct customer contact, is likely to detect rapidly evolving typologies before supervisors; coordinated messaging with other authorities clarifies risk management expectations, and newly regulated sectors need education and awareness raising. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The private sector is likely to identify these changes before supervisors since they have direct contact with customers."</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Features of good dialogue include ongoing forums, conferences or committees bringing together supervisors, LEAs, the FIU and industry to discuss risks and guidance (not entity-specific compliance feedback), and regular information sharing and outreach including public-private partnerships. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "standing consultation forums, conferences or committees"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Supervisors can seek private sector feedback, e.g. on outcomes of inspection cycles or thematic reviews, areas most needing guidance, or clarification of simplified requirements for proven low risk customers or products. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "seeking public feedback on the main outcomes from an inspection cycle or thematic review"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Japan's outreach includes trade associations and ship-owner associations to share risk information and raise awareness of inherent AML risks and needed transaction due diligence. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "engagement with trade associations and ship-owner associations"</sub>

## 4.3. Use of third-parties

- **permissive / context**: Supervisors may use third parties such as consultants or auditors for AML/CFT functions, but ultimate responsibility for supervisory obligations remains with the supervisor. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "ultimately the responsibility remains with supervisors to ensure compliance with their supervisory obligations"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Building internal capacity, with adequate skilled in-house staff, should be the priority; over-reliance on third parties can undermine internal expertise. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "overreliance or dependence on third parties can undermine the building of internal expertise and capacity"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Growing financial sector sophistication (FinTech, business models, IT) makes access to financial engineers, IT experts and data scientists essential, e.g. for exotic financial products and technologies like distributed ledgers or AI. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "tap into the expertise of financial engineers, IT experts, data scientists"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: AML/CFT supervision of banks and large FIs cannot be done without thorough examination of their IT systems (MIS), including monitoring systems, parameters and third-party compliance solutions. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "cannot be undertaken without a thorough examination and understanding of their IT systems"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Third parties can help monitor lower risk entities so supervisors focus on higher risk ones; the FATF MVTS RBA guidance notes third-party periodic reviews of lower-risk MVTS providers can prevent supervisors being overwhelmed. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "engaging third parties to assist in performing periodic reviews of lower-risk MVTS providers"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The UK FCA can require an entity to engage a 'Skilled Person' to review and report to the FCA, testing systems and controls, identifying weaknesses and sometimes remediating them. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Financial Conduct Authority can require an entity to engage the services of a ‘Skilled Person’"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors' employment practices should allow flexible access to technical expertise; external assignments and secondments can broaden staff experience. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "employment practices should allow enough flexibility to ensure that supervisors can access technical expertise"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: When engaging third parties, supervisors should evaluate candidates (competencies, credentials, risk experience, conflicts), ensure independent and efficient work per instructions, set communication protocols for issues, oversee work quality, and control access to confidential information under terms of reference and data protection laws. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "competencies, credentials, experience in the risk area, potential conflicts of interest"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: When reporting entities outsource AML/CFT functions (record keeping, parts of CDD, UN sanctions screening, PEP monitoring), legal responsibility stays with the entity, but supervisors should have power, at least via the entity, to examine the third parties' capability and effectiveness. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the legal responsibility to comply with AML/CFT obligations remains with the reporting entity"</sub>

## Annex A. Overview of supervisory tools

- **descriptive / method**: AML/CFT returns are regular or ad hoc, possibly automated, requests for quantitative and qualitative data on key risk indicators (business lines, product segments, customer types) used to risk-profile entities. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The collection of regular AML/CFT returns could beautomated"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Regular engagement with boards and senior management, especially of systemically important entities, gives timely information on strategy changes affecting inherent risk. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "particularly those that are systemicallyimportant"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Ongoing surveillance of emerging risks uses engagement with regulators, mining system-wide data, conversations with entities and news monitoring, and calibrates responses such as thematic inspections. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "mining ofsystem-wide risk-relevant data, conversations with regulatedentities, and ongoing news monitoring"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Regularly updated questionnaires on AML/CFT risk management controls give an early view of control adequacy, and sector-level common weaknesses can signal need for thematic reviews. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These questionnaires, which are typically updated on a regular basis"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: On-site or off-site reviews of board and management reporting and oversight, policies and procedures, and internal and external audit reports help assess risk culture, corroborate entity risk assessments and spot weaknesses. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "adequacy of AML/CFT reporting toboard and management, as an indication of the risk culture"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Interviews with board and senior management assess competency, risk awareness, appetite and tone from the top, while interviews with staff executing controls assess the 'echo from the bottom' alignment with that tone. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "allow supervisors to assessthe ‘echo from the bottom’"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Entity-specific inspections can be on-site or off-site; for sectors with many small lower-risk entities, off-site inspections and virtual meetings can be effective; on-site inspections usually include sample testing, typically not done off-site. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In sectors with large numbers ofsmall lower risk supervised entities, off-site inspections and virtual"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Triggered inspections are targeted and prompted by events such as whistleblowing, public allegations (e.g. Panama papers), new typologies, or findings from other supervisory actions or questionnaires. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "publicallegations of wrongdoing (such as the Panama papers)"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Thematic reviews cover multiple entities, often in one sector, focusing on specific controls (e.g. transaction monitoring, PEP treatment) or risks such as TF, proliferation financing and trade-based ML. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "specific risks such as TF, proliferation financing and trade-based money laundering"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Other tools include tracking remediation of past inspection lapses and industry outreach such as workshops, training, seminars and engagement with industry associations. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "monitor if past observed weaknesses havebeen satisfactorily remediated in a timely manner"</sub>

## Overview of challenges identified in Mutual Evaluations

- **descriptive / finding or result**: In three out of four mutual evaluations, major and fundamental improvements in risk-based supervision are required; most of the 102 countries evaluated under the 2013 Methodology are rated "moderate" on IO.3. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "in three out of four evaluations, major and fundamental improvements are required"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The largest gaps to "substantial" IO.3 ratings are in implementing risk-based supervision (Core Issue 3.2) and applying sanctions for non-compliance (Core Issue 3.4). (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the largest gaps to achieving “substantial” ratings are in the implementation of the risk-based approach"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In a sample of 59 evaluations, only 24% of FI supervisors and 7% of DNFBP supervisors had conducted an updated risk assessment. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "only 24% of FI supervisors and 7% of DNFBP supervisors have conducted an updated risk assessment"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Ability to supervise risk-sensitively is linked less to the sector supervised than to overall supervisory quality (means and tools); better-resourced supervisors mitigated, though did not eliminate, the gap across FI and DNFBP sectors. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is not necessarily connected to sector supervised, but rather to the overall quality of supervision"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Technical compliance on supervision is generally largely compliant to compliant, but 44% of countries are rated NC on R.28 (DNFBP supervision). (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "44% countries rated NC on R.28, related to the supervision of DNFBPs"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Risk-based supervision is generally more advanced for FIs than DNFBPs; DNFBP sectors are newer to regulation, have weaker understanding of obligations, less developed risk assessments, supervisory agencies lacking capacity or with overlapping responsibilities, and many diverse entities. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Implementation of risk-based supervision is generally more advanced for FIs than it is for DNFBPs."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: VASP supervision challenges resemble other sectors' but are also unique given the sector's novelty, global reach and transaction speed. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the novel nature of the sector, its global reach and the speed at which transactions can take place"</sub>

## 5.1. Disconnect from, or misalignments with, the NRA

- **expected / expectation**: NRAs inform national AML/CFT policy and risk-based regulation and supervision, give a point-in-time view, and should be regularly reviewed and kept up to date; without comprehensive risk assessment or with NRA-supervision misalignment, supervision cannot be effectively risk-based. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "NRAs should be regularly reviewed and kept up to date"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Some jurisdictions designing risk-based frameworks found NRA gaps, including not identifying all risks and lacking information on medium- and low-risk areas and DNFBP risks, leading them to supplement NRAs, particularly on sectoral risks. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the lack of information on medium-risk and low-risk areas/sectors, and ML/TF risks in the DNFBP sectors"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The NRA and SRA need not align perfectly in risk scoring, but their findings should be generally coherent. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there should be a general coherence between the findings of both assessments"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Supervisors should participate in the NRA process and share sectoral risk understanding; NRA findings should be accessible to them and used in strategies, and an incomplete NRA should be reviewed and improved; authorities should keep ongoing communication on the NRA to capture emerging risks. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisory authorities should participate in the NRA process and share and discuss their understanding of sectoral risks"</sub>

## 5.2. New areas of supervisory responsibility – identifying the regulatory population

- **descriptive / method**: When supervision extends to a new sector, the first step is identifying the regulatory population and risk environment, which drives resource and skill needs; this is harder for entities not otherwise supervised (e.g. VASPs) or based abroad but operating locally (e.g. online casinos or VASPs). (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a first step is to identify the regulatory population and begin to understand the risk environment"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In one jurisdiction early to regulate VASPs, the supervisor expected about 50 VASPs to register, but around 350 registered when the regime came into force. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the supervisor estimated that approximately 50 VASPs would register as obliged entities"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: UK OPBAS found after its first year that 18% of relevant DNFBP supervisors had not fully identified their supervised population; after workshops in June 2019 this was rectified by year end. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "18% of relevant DNFBP supervisors had not fully identified their supervised population"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Sources for identifying the population include revenue and tax agencies, corporate registries, trade or professional associations, already-supervised entities such as banks, open sources (web searches, directories), and outreach to DNFBP or VASP representative bodies. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "revenue and tax agencies, corporate registries and trade or professional associations"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should periodically identify and verify their regulated population and reassess strategies and resources; under point-of-consumption regulation, they should establish channels with jurisdictions hosting many entities serving foreign customers (e.g. online casinos). (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisors should continue to identify and verify their regulated population on a periodic basis"</sub>

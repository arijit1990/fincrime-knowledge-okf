## Executive Summary

- **descriptive / context**: The report says that while stablecoins are increasingly used for legitimate purposes, their distinctive features (price stability, high liquidity, interoperability) also make them attractive tools for threat actors. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "also make them attractive tools for threat actors"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report says stablecoins generally have the same vulnerabilities as other virtual assets, exacerbated by price stability and ample liquidity that can make them more likely to be used in P2P transactions; P2P transactions via unhosted wallets are a key vulnerability, conducted without AML/CFT-obliged intermediaries and potentially higher risk, especially when layered through unhosted wallets. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "P2P transactions via unhosted wallets represent a key vulnerability in the stablecoin ecosystem"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The report states stablecoins could exhibit more asset-specific vulnerabilities, notably from interconnections with traditional finance; cases indicate threat actors have used stablecoins to purchase prohibited goods without cashing out through intermediaries, but data remains limited on broader use of stablecoins for goods and services or for P2P transactions. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "data remains limited regarding the broader use of stablecoins for the purchase of goods and services or for P2P transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: The report identifies good practices for wider jurisdictions and the private sector, some common to all virtual assets and others unique to stablecoins: comprehensive legal frameworks complying with the FATF Standards; clear AML/CFT obligations on stablecoin issuers, intermediaries and custodians; assessing risk and implementing mitigation for transactions involving unhosted wallets; and leveraging advanced technology-based tools. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "imposing clear AML/CFT obligations on stablecoin issuers, intermediaries, and custodians"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The FATF report (March 2026) states that by mid-2025 more than 250 stablecoins were circulating, with combined market capitalisation above USD 300 billion and daily trading volumes higher than those of Bitcoin. Centrally governed, fiat-backed stablecoins, mostly referenced to the USD, make up most of the market and are used across several blockchains. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "over 250 stablecoins were in circulation, with total market capitalisation exceeding USD 300 billion"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report states that stablecoins, including via unhosted wallets, have become a common element of ML, TF and PF schemes that use virtual assets. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Stablecoins, including through unhosted wallets, have become a common component of ML, TF and PF schemes"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The report notes that although some uses of stablecoins are simple or direct, many schemes treat stablecoins as one step in a longer chain of transactions built to hide where the funds came from and to separate them from their intended use. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "many schemes use stablecoins as one feature in a more complex series of transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report says that, according to reporting, stablecoins are the virtual asset most commonly used in illicit transactions. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "stablecoins are the most popular virtual asset used in illicit transactions"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The report says it is important for jurisdictions and relevant stakeholders to keep a close watch on whether stablecoins are increasingly used to buy goods without relying on traditional on- and off-ramps, and on how far reliable data on the scale and share of P2P transactions can be obtained. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "continue to closely monitor whether stablecoins are increasingly used for purchases without reliance on traditional on- and off-ramps"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report makes available to members of the FATF Global Network indicators of the misuse of stablecoins, particularly through unhosted wallets, and makes internal recommendations for the FATF Global Network to consider to potentially mitigate risk in the stablecoin environment. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "indicators of the misuse of stablecoins, particularly through unhosted wallets"</sub>

## Objectives and Structure

- **descriptive / context**: The project's objective is to enhance understanding of emerging ML/TF/PF risks, threat actors and vulnerabilities related to stablecoins and unhosted wallets, particularly during P2P transactions, and to identify and share good practices to mitigate those risks; the report is organised as Background, Part One (current situation, threat actors, vulnerabilities) and Part Two (good practices, investigation and co-operation). (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "enhance the understanding of emerging ML/TF/PF risks, threat actors and vulnerabilities related to stablecoins and unhosted wallets"</sub>

## Previous FATF Work on Stablecoins

- **descriptive / context**: The FATF has worked on stablecoins since 2019, first through the public study on money laundering risks from "stablecoins" and other emerging assets and the FATF Report to the G20 on So-called Stablecoins, and continued to clarify application of the Standards; in its 2025 Targeted Update on VAs and VASPs it observed that use of stablecoins by illicit actors, including DPRK actors, terrorist financiers and drug trafficking networks, has continued to increase over time. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "use of stablecoins by a range of illicit actors, including DPRK actors, terrorist financiers, and drug trafficking networks"</sub>

## Executive Summary

- **recommended / recommendation**: The report stresses the need for more coordination between competent authorities and across borders, and the importance of technical assistance for jurisdictions that have not yet adequately put in place regulatory and supervisory frameworks for stablecoins. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the need for further coordination among competent authorities and across borders"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report notes that several jurisdictions have taken innovative approaches, such as obliging issuers to build programmable controls into stablecoin smart contracts so that freezing, deny-listing or other risk mitigation can be applied in secondary markets. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "requiring issuers to embed programmable controls in stablecoin smart contracts"</sub>

## Objectives and Structure

- **descriptive / definition**: The FATF treats a peer-to-peer (P2P) transaction as a virtual asset transfer made with no VASP or other obliged entity using or taking part, for example a transfer between two unhosted wallets whose users act on their own behalf. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "VA transfers conducted without the use or involvement of a VASP or other obliged entity"</sub>

## Scope

- **descriptive / context**: The report reflects developments in the stablecoin ecosystem until the end of 2025, explains how the FATF Standards apply to participants in stablecoin arrangements, and focuses on practical risk mitigation measures for stablecoins with a focus on P2P transactions, with case studies provided by jurisdictions. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This report reflects developments in the stablecoin ecosystem until the end of 2025"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The report states that, as with all FATF guidance products, the Guidance is non-binding. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "As with all FATF guidance products, the Guidance is non-binding"</sub>

## Methodology

- **descriptive / method**: The methodology involved two rounds of requests for information to the FATF project team and Virtual Asset Contact Group (VACG) members, yielding 29 case studies on threats, typologies and jurisdictional measures and 25 inputs on AML/CFT/CPF obligations of participants, regulatory challenges and good practices for mitigation. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "29 case studies on threats, typologies, and jurisdictional measures were received"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Private sector entities such as stablecoin issuers and blockchain analytics companies were invited to a targeted outreach survey on challenges and good practices in mitigating illicit finance risks associated with stablecoins, and a virtual roundtable with key stakeholders was held based on the survey results. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A virtual roundtable meeting with key stakeholders was held based on the survey results"</sub>

## Previous FATF Work on Stablecoins

- **descriptive / context**: The FATF stressed the importance of continued monitoring of two things: (1) whether stablecoins reach global mass adoption in a way that lets people buy goods and services with little or no reliance on traditional on- and off-ramps, and (2) how far jurisdictions and the private sector can obtain accurate data on the scale and proportion of P2P transactions. The aim is that the FATF Standards stay effective as virtual assets evolve. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to ensure that the FATF Standards remain effective despite ongoing virtual asset evolution"</sub>

## Basic Definitions

- **descriptive / definition**: The report describes a stablecoin as one form of virtual asset; the FATF recognises stablecoins are not a legal or technical category, its use of the term does not endorse any stability claims, and the term generally refers to a virtual asset with a stabilisation mechanism usable as a means of payment and/or store of value, often seeking to maintain price stability by linking value to reference assets such as fiat currencies or virtual assets. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "stablecoins are not a legal or technical category"</sub>

## Asset-Backed Stablecoins

- **descriptive / definition**: Asset-backed stablecoins are backed by financial assets such as fiat currencies or government bonds; their issuers often purport and commit to be able to redeem them for the backing asset 1:1 at all times, which could contribute to price stability, and they generally have centralised governance structures often managed by the issuer. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "backed by financial assets such as fiat currencies or government bonds"</sub>

## Algorithmic Stablecoins

- **descriptive / definition**: Algorithmic stablecoins are typically designed to maintain price stability by adjusting supply through smart contracts or algorithms (such as synthetically collateralised dollar-type stablecoins) without any backed assets, increasing supply when prices rise and decreasing it when prices fall; issuance and management are often by autonomous smart contracts, governance can be more decentralised, and they generally do not offer redemption into fiat currency, aiming instead to stabilise prices through mechanisms such as stablecoin swaps or burns. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "increase supply when prices rise and decrease supply when prices fall to maintain stability"</sub>

## Basic Definitions

- **expected / expectation**: The report says that however stablecoins are treated in national law, service providers dealing with stablecoins that meet the FATF definition of a VASP or FI should be subject to the matching obligations under the FATF Standards. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should be subject to the corresponding obligations in accordance with the FATF Standards"</sub>

## Asset-Backed Stablecoins

- **descriptive / definition**: VA-backed stablecoins use virtual assets and tokenised financial instruments as collateral. Because collateral prices are volatile, such arrangements normally require and maintain collateral worth more than the coins issued. Issuance, redemption, collateral management and liquidation often run through autonomous smart contracts, and sometimes users can issue stablecoins directly without a central governing entity. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "VA-backed stablecoins are issued using virtual assets (VAs) and tokenised financial instruments as collateral"</sub>

## Participants in the Stablecoin Ecosystem

- **descriptive / definition**: Stablecoin issuers are responsible for issuing and redeeming stablecoins and ensuring their purported stability in the interim, often minting in exchange for fiat or other assets while collecting fees; in many cases the issuer also deploys and maintains the smart contracts for on-chain transactions and can assign part of its functions, such as redemption, to intermediary VASPs. Reserve custodians store and manage the reserve assets backing the stablecoins on behalf of the issuer. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "responsible for issuing and redeeming stablecoins and ensuring their purported stability in the interim"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Intermediaries are VASPs, including VA exchanges, and financial institutions providing buying, selling, safekeeping, trading or conversion services for stablecoins; DeFi may also offer services such as remittance, payment, exchange and lending involving stablecoins. Payment service providers and card networks can enable stablecoin payments for goods and services by offering payment infrastructure to merchants and can facilitate remittances. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "VASPs including VA exchanges and financial institutions that provide buying, selling, safekeeping, trading or conversion services"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Unhosted wallets are virtual asset wallets not hosted or managed by a third-party service provider, where the user maintains exclusive control over the access keys; certain unhosted wallet service providers also offer stablecoin swap functions. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "where the user maintains exclusive control over the access keys"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Blockchain analytics tool providers produce analysis from examination of blockchains and entities with customer interfaces such as wallet service providers, which stablecoin issuers and intermediaries may use to support illicit finance risk mitigation including restrictive measures; the providers use different taxonomies, which makes outcomes difficult to compare. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "They use different taxonomies, which make the outcomes of their analysis difficult to compare"</sub>

## Stablecoin Lifecyle and Features

- **descriptive / context**: A stablecoin issuer typically issues stablecoins by receiving fiat currencies or VAs from contracting parties and minting stablecoins corresponding to the value received; the coins circulate among users and VASPs and are then redeemed for fiat currency, with mechanisms varying by stablecoin type. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "minting stablecoins corresponding to the value received"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Issuance creates a primary market, in which primary customers (typically VASPs or institutional clients, who undergo customer due diligence and onboarding, and who can also be retail customers) purchase or redeem directly from the issuer, and a secondary market of holders who may use a hosted wallet with an AML/CFT-obliged VASP or FI, or unhosted wallets to hold and transfer peer-to-peer without an obliged entity, so secondary customers using unhosted wallets may not be subject to AML/CFT obligations. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "secondary customers using unhosted wallets may not be subject to AML/CFT obligations"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Issuers often keep a degree of control over their stablecoins via smart contracts, programming them to prevent certain wallet addresses from transacting (e.g., blacklisting) or to remove stablecoins from circulation in secondary markets (freezing or burning); issuers can also view all on-chain transactions in their stablecoin given the nature of public blockchains. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "prevent certain wallet addresses from transacting in their stablecoin"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Redemption from the issuer is typically available only to primary customers subject to the issuer's rules, in some cases through issuer-designated intermediaries; primary and secondary holders may exchange stablecoins for fiat or other VAs through centralised intermediaries (VASPs), decentralised exchanges (DEXs) or peer-to-peer transactions. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "redemption from the issuer is available only to primary customers"</sub>

## Governance Structure

- **descriptive / context**: Stablecoin arrangements generally have a governing structure responsible for operating the stablecoin services; a centralised governance body such as issuers and intermediaries may perform core functions (such as AML/CFT/CPF and managing stabilisation mechanisms), the issuer often takes the key governing role, and decentralised structures can make responsibility ambiguous. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Stablecoin arrangements can also be structured in a decentralised manner"</sub>

## FATF Requirements for VASPs and Applicability to Stablecoins

- **mandatory / requirement**: FATF Recommendation 15 requires all jurisdictions to identify, assess and understand the ML/TF/PF risks emerging from VAs and VASPs; ensure VASPs are required to be licensed or registered in the jurisdiction where they are created; and apply sanctions to natural or legal persons carrying out VASP activities without the requisite licence or registration. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identify, assess and understand the ML/TF/PF risks emerging from VAs and VASPs"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: FATF Recommendation 15 also requires jurisdictions to ensure VASPs are subject to supervision, to apply proportionate and dissuasive sanctions to VASPs that fail to comply with AML/CFT/CPF requirements, and to ensure that targeted financial sanctions obligations apply to VASPs. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "apply proportionate and dissuasive sanctions to VASPs that fail to comply with AML/CFT/CPF requirements"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The FATF defines VAs as a digital representation of value that can be digitally traded or transferred and used for payment or investment purposes; VAs do not include digital representations of fiat currencies, securities and other financial assets already covered elsewhere in the FATF Recommendations, and given their characteristics the FATF considers stablecoins as virtual assets. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Given their characteristics, the FATF considers stablecoins as virtual assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Under the FATF Standards the sole act of issuing a VA on its own is not a covered service under the VASP definition; however, stablecoin issuers (natural or legal persons) who as a business engage in exchange or transfer activities (such as exchanging customers' fiat/VAs for their stablecoins during issuance or redemption) or participate in and provide financial services related to an issuer's offer and/or sale of a VA fall within the FATF definition of VASP or FI. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the sole act of issuing a VA on its own is not a covered service under the definition of VASP"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 13 says that, per the FATF Report to the G20 on So-called Stablecoins, the FATF considers developers and governance bodies of stablecoin arrangements will in general be financial institutions (e.g., a business involved in 'issuing and managing means of payment') or a VASP (e.g., a business involved in 'participation in and provision of financial services related to an issuer's offer and/or sale of a virtual asset'). (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these developers and governance bodies will be, in general, financial institutions"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Intermediaries that exchange stablecoins for fiat currency or virtual assets, transfer stablecoins, or offer safekeeping or administration of stablecoins fall under the FATF definition of VASP; the report lists exchanges, trading platforms, custodial wallet providers, certain types of unhosted wallet service provider, and over-the-counter brokers as examples. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Intermediaries involved in exchanging stablecoins for fiat currency or virtual assets, transferring stablecoins"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The report says unhosted wallets that, as a business, engage in or actively facilitate any VA activities or operations covered by Recommendation 15 for or on behalf of another person should be regulated as VASPs. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "operations covered by Recommendation 15, for or on behalf of another person, should be regulated as VASPs."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Creators, owners, operators and other persons who maintain control or sufficient influence in DeFi arrangements, even if seemingly decentralised, may fall under the FATF definition of VASP where they provide or actively facilitate VASP services; where no legal or natural person with control or sufficient influence can be identified there may be no central owner/operator meeting the VASP definition, though many DeFi arrangements in practice are decentralised in name only. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "many DeFi arrangements in practice are decentralised in name only"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FATF does not consider natural or legal persons providing ancillary services or products to a VA network, such as hardware wallet manufacturers or unhosted wallet providers that do not also engage in VASP activities, to be VASPs; P2P transactions via unhosted wallets are not explicitly subject to AML/CFT controls under the FATF Standards because obligations are generally placed on intermediaries rather than individuals, with some exceptions such as targeted financial sanctions requirements. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "P2P transactions via unhosted wallets are not explicitly subject to AML/CFT controls under the FATF Standards"</sub>

## Current Situation

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The first stablecoin promoted as pegged 1:1 to the US dollar appeared in 2014; there were 259 circulating stablecoins at the end of June 2025, market capitalisation reached USD 316 billion in October 2025 (about 8% of total VA market capitalisation), 24-hour trading volume reached USD 156 billion (almost three times Bitcoin's USD 55 billion), and stablecoins represented 30% of all on-chain VA transaction volume in 2025. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "259 circulating stablecoins as of the end of June 2025"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Fiat currency-backed stablecoins represented 95% of total stablecoin market capitalisation in October 2025, with USD-backed stablecoins 97% of those; VA-backed stablecoins accounted for 3% of overall stablecoin activity and algorithmic stablecoins only 0.2%; as of mid-2025, 90% of stablecoins were centralised. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "fiat currency-backed stablecoins dominate, representing 95% of the total market capitalisation in October 2025"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Reasons for increased adoption include faster settlement, lower transaction fees, cross-border capabilities and lack of reliance on traditional business hours, with some FIs accepting stablecoins for payments and individuals in countries with weak or unstable currencies using them as a store of value; the European Systemic Risk Board questions the volume of stablecoins used for payments and posits that market capitalisation is driven by trading volume of unbacked virtual assets. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "faster settlement times, lower transaction fees, cross-border capabilities, and lack of reliance on traditional business operating hours"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: As large jurisdictions establish or clarify regulatory frameworks, institutional interest in stablecoins has grown significantly, with banks, card networks, money transmitters, large technology companies and others expressing interest in entering the ecosystem; a footnote says nearly all FSB member jurisdictions have regulatory frameworks for virtual assets and stablecoins in place or planned. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "institutional interest in stablecoins has grown significantly over the last couple of years"</sub>

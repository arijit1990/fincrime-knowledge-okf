## Box 6. Case studies: – AML/CFT Obligations in the Stablecoin Ecosystem

- **descriptive / context**: In the EU, ART issuers are legal persons established in the EU that have obtained an authorization under Article 16 of MiCA, but issuing ARTs alone does not make an ART issuer an AML/CFT obliged entity. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "issuing ARTs alone does not make ART issuers AML/CFT obliged entities"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In the EU, exchange services between ARTs and other crypto assets or fiat currencies, custody of ARTs, and services around issuance (for example placing of ARTs, reception and transmission of orders) require Crypto Asset Service Provider (CASP) authorization. As obliged entities, CASPs have obligations including risk policies, customer identification and verification, transaction monitoring, suspicious transaction reporting, and sanctions/asset freezing procedures. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "require a Crypto Asset Service Provider (CASP) authorization, and as obliged entities CASP obligations include risk policies"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In the EU, competent authorities consider ML/TF risks when granting, refusing or withdrawing an authorization of an ART issuer, and typically check for AML/CFT-related criminal convictions of members of the management body and shareholders with qualified holdings. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "typically check AML/CFT related criminal convictions of members of the management body and shareholders with qualified holdings"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In the EU, an ART issuer needs to provide information on its arrangements with CASPs (if any), especially the procedures in place to ensure compliance with obligations on prevention of money laundering and terrorist financing under Directive (EU) 2015/849. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the procedures in place to ensure compliance with the obligations in relation to the prevention of money laundering and terrorist financing"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In the EU, competent authorities can refuse authorisation if the applicant issuer's business model might expose the issuer or the sector to serious ML/TF risks. The absence of any obliged-entity intermediary in the offering and placing of ARTs by the issuer may indicate such a risk, and a competent authority may rely on this provision to refuse authorisation. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The absence of any obliged entity intermediary in the offering and placing of ARTs by that issuer may be an indication of such a risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Under the EU's Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation (MiCA), stablecoins fall into two classes. E-Money Tokens (EMTs) are tied to one fiat currency. Asset Referenced Tokens (ARTs) are tokens that are not e-money tokens and aim to hold a stable value by pegging to some other value or right, or a combination of these, which can include one or more official currencies. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Asset Referenced Tokens (ARTs), which are not e money tokens and purport to maintain a stable value"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report says that ART issuers in the EU are not themselves AML/CFT obliged entities, yet they are still required to keep up sound ongoing ML/TF risk management. It adds that EMTs are issued by authorised credit institutions or electronic money institutions, and these issuers are subject to AML/CFT obligations. (p. 25)[^original]

## Stablecoin Issuers Applying Controls

- **mandatory / requirement**: Paragraph 61: The FATF Standards require preventive measures, as detailed under Recommendation 15, to be applied to stablecoin issuers. These include CDD, record keeping, detection and reporting of suspicious transactions, and compliance with the Travel Rule, among others. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF Standards require issuers to apply preventive measures, as detailed under Recommendation 15 of the FATF Standards"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 61: Stablecoin issuers should always apply preventive measures to their customers in the primary market, meaning those who purchase or redeem the stablecoin directly from or to the issuer. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Stablecoin issuers should always apply preventive measures to their customers in the primary market"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Paragraph 62: Jurisdictions may want to consider how existing AML/CFT/CPF obligations could apply in the secondary market, in particular how to leverage issuers' unique technological capabilities to execute smart contract functions such as a burn or freeze. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Jurisdictions may want to consider how existing AML/CFT/CPF obligations could apply in the secondary market"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 62: Issuers can determine the issuance and redemption of their stablecoins at any time, and can embed freeze and burn functions in the stablecoin's smart contract and exercise them as needed. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the issuer can embed freeze and burn functions in the stablecoin’s smart contract and exercise them as needed"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 63: Where stablecoins are held in unhosted wallets, only the pseudonymous blockchain address is observable on-chain and no underlying CDD information is available. Even though the actual holder cannot be identified, freezing remains feasible where an address is determined to be linked to criminal activity. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "executing freezing functions remain feasible"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 65: The smart contract is the core code controlling how a stablecoin works on the blockchain. It can be programmed with conditions that must be satisfied before an action is allowed in both primary and secondary market transactions, so issuers can allow only certain wallet addresses to transact (allow-listing) or prevent certain wallet addresses from transacting (deny-listing). (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The smart contract is the core piece of code that controls how the stablecoin works on the blockchain"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 63: Issuers are able to watch on-chain circulation, balances and transfers at blockchain-address level, but the blockchain does not publicly reveal who holds the stablecoins. When coins sit with a VASP, competent authorities may be able to get customer identity information through lawful requests to that VASP, whereas the issuer cannot readily obtain it. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the identities of the stablecoin holders are not publicly available on the blockchain"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Paragraph 64: Jurisdictions may want to consider whether, depending on risk and context, stablecoin issuers should have to track on a proactive basis where their stablecoins are and how they are used in the secondary market, using blockchain analytics tools. They may also want to consider whether issuers should face proportionate extra measures, for example regular reporting on how their stablecoins circulate in the secondary market. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "proactively monitor the location and use of their stablecoins in the secondary market with the support of blockchain analytics tools"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Allow-listing (whitelisting): an issuer controls who can hold, receive or transfer a stablecoin by permitting only pre-approved entities or addresses, via an access control list in the smart contract; transactions involving an address not on the list are automatically rejected. CDD, performed by the issuer or an approved third party, can form part of pre-approval and be periodically repeated as necessary for ongoing due diligence. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "if the wallet address sending/receiving the stablecoin is not on the access control list, the transaction is automatically rejected"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Deny-listing (blacklisting): an issuer restricts specific wallet addresses or entities from holding, receiving and transferring stablecoins by adding a deny control list to the smart contract; a transaction is automatically rejected if the sending or receiving address is on the list. Criteria for adding addresses may be informed by third-party solutions including blockchain analytics and law enforcement data. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "if the wallet address sending/receiving the stablecoin is on the deny control list, the transaction is automatically rejected"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Deny-listing can help competent authorities freeze wallet addresses holding a stablecoin balance. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Deny-listing can help competent authorities freezing wallet addresses with a stablecoin balance"</sub>

## Box 7: Switzerland – risk mitigation of unhosted wallets

- **descriptive / case example**: Switzerland case: transactions will only be permitted between wallets on the allow list, which may include hosted and unhosted wallets. The list will be automatically updated when a new qualifying wallet holder is identified. Unhosted wallet holders will be verified by Swiss banks, VASPs, or foreign banks subject to regulatory standards similar to Switzerland's; hosted wallets will be identified and allow-listed by the banks or VASPs that manage them. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Unhosted wallet holders will be verified by Swiss banks, VASPs, or foreign banks subject to regulatory standards similar to those in Switzerland"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 66: The deny-list approach is used more frequently but is more reactive, as issuers typically deny a wallet after outreach from law enforcement or identification on a national sanctions list. An allow-list is more proactive but may push users who do not want to be identified for privacy reasons out of the stablecoin ecosystem. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The deny-list approach is used more frequently, but it is more reactive"</sub>

## Box 8: France – Compliance measures taken by stablecoin issuers

- **descriptive / case example**: France: in practice, stablecoin issuers and VASPs use blockchain analytics tools to trace stablecoins on-chain and identify potentially risky transactions, covering both primary market participants and subsequent secondary market holders. The tools detect transactions involving addresses flagged as exposed to ML/TF risk, with exposure assessed directly (the holders) or indirectly (their counterparties). (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "use blockchain analytics tools in order to trace stablecoins on-chain and identify potentially risky transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: France: some stablecoin issuers have embedded features in the smart contract, such as allow-listing, deny-listing and blocking/freezing capabilities, to be activated on request of the public authorities. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "blocking/freezing capabilities to be activated on request of the public authorities"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Paragraph 67: Stablecoin issuers may also take, or be required to take, other risk mitigation measures. One is transaction limits: limits on the amount per transaction or total per day by wallet addresses, or clearance of pending transactions, to mitigate secondary market risk; these may complement deny-listing (reducing circumvention risk) or allow-listing (fostering inclusion). (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Stablecoin issuers may also take, or be required to take, other risk mitigation measures such as"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 31: Blocking is an issuer's ability to prevent a specific transaction from being processed without necessarily freezing the holder's entire wallet (for example stopping transfers to sanctioned entities, or rejecting a transaction when sender or receiver authorization conditions are not met). Freezing is the issuer's ability to immobilize a specific wallet address, preventing it from moving its tokens. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Blocking refers to a stablecoin issuer’s ability to prevent a specific transaction from being processed without necessarily freezing the holder’s entire wallet"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 30: The FATF framework permits transaction limits as a way to reduce financial crime risk tied to accounts that have not undergone regular KYC/CDD, as the FATF financial inclusion guidance (paragraph 142) indicates. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Transaction limits are allowed under the FATF framework to mitigate potential financial crime risks"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Paragraph 67: Issuers are encouraged to put in place technical means to block, freeze and withdraw stablecoins (for example by burning and re-issuing) at any time when transactions to or from wallets that are not allow-listed, or that are deny-listed, are attempted or occur. The same capability can let issuers comply with orders from an authority under applicable law, such as confiscation by law enforcement. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Stablecoin issuers are encouraged to implement technical measures to be able to block, freeze and withdraw"</sub>

## Public Sector Use of Tools

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 68: Blockchain analytics tools can be useful for identifying ML/TF/PF risks in the stablecoin ecosystem; the FATF has previously analysed and encouraged their use, and other bodies such as the UNSC Counter-Terrorism Committee have taken a similar approach. Advances in AI, machine learning and big data analytics may have the potential to enhance these tools for AML/CFT/CPF purposes. (p. 29)[^original]

Some large banks and third-party payment institutions conduct on-chain analysis and tracking of virtual currencies. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Track the upstream and downstream transaction-related wallets of core wallets through visualization tools"</sub>

## ML/TF/PF Risks Mitigation measures for Unhosted Wallets and P2P Transactions

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 77: The FATF Standards are built on the risk-based approach, and they do not explicitly apply to unhosted wallets and P2P transactions. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "While the FATF Standards do not explicitly apply to unhosted wallets and P2P transactions"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 77: Jurisdictions should consider the ML/TF/PF risks of unhosted wallets and P2P transactions and mitigate them. The FATF advises jurisdictions to seek to understand to the greatest extent possible the frequency of P2P transactions and associated risks, despite challenges in obtaining reliable and complete data on the unregulated market, and should also seek to implement appropriate risk mitigation measures in light of the estimated size and risk level of P2P transactions. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdiction should consider their ML/TF/PF risks, including for P2P transactions, and mitigate those risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Paragraph 78: Approaches taken across the Global Network to mitigate unhosted wallet and P2P risks include VASPs limiting the amount of funds customers can transfer to unhosted wallets, and VASPs applying enhanced CDD to transactions with unhosted wallets, including verifying the wallet's beneficial owner identity. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "VASPs limiting the amount of funds that can be transferred by their customers to unhosted wallets"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Paragraph 78 (continued): further approaches include VASPs using blockchain analytics to determine the risk level of customers' counterparties who own unhosted wallets; entities in stablecoin arrangements subjecting unhosted wallets to AML/CFT obligations including CDD at issuance and redemption; and prohibiting or denying licenses to platforms that allow transfers to unhosted wallets. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Prohibiting or denying licenses to platforms that allow transfers to unhosted wallets"</sub>

## Box 14. Germany: mitigation of risks associated with redemptions to unhosted wallets

- **descriptive / case example**: Germany (Box 14): an entity applied for a licence to issue euro-denominated e-money tokens redeemable on request; funds received will be invested, and clients, exclusively businesses, will distribute tokens to end customers. Permission was granted on conditions: reserves diversified and restricted to short-term, highly liquid instruments to mitigate liquidity and concentration risks; business clients not considered elevated AML/CFT risk, but because the entity has no visibility over ultimate beneficiaries, they will be subject to enhanced due diligence. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "as the entity has no visibility over the ultimate beneficiaries, business clients will be subject to enhanced due diligence"</sub>

## Box 13. Singapore – AML/CFT obligations for Unhosted Wallets

- **expected / expectation**: Box 13 (Singapore): for transactions with unhosted wallets, Digital Payment Token Services Providers (the VASPs in Singapore) are expected to apply enhanced risk mitigation because of the specific ML/TF risks of such transfers. Examples include restricting VA transfers to first-party transfers only, verifying ownership of the customer's unhosted wallet, identifying the beneficiary, enhanced monitoring of the customer account, and understanding the purpose of the transaction. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "are expected to put in place enhanced risk mitigating measures, given the specific ML/TF risks associated with such transfers"</sub>

## Box 14. Germany: mitigation of risks associated with redemptions to unhosted wallets

- **mandatory / requirement**: Germany (Box 14): because of risks of redemptions to unhosted wallets, the entity must verify the identity of the wallet owner before processing any redemption to a self-hosted wallet. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Entity must verify the identity of the wallet owner before processing any redemption to a self-hosted wallet"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Germany (Box 14): further safeguards are blockchain forensics tools to trace origin and destination of virtual assets with attention to high-risk or sanctioned addresses, and client-specific transaction thresholds to detect and prevent suspicious activity, with lower limits for higher-risk clients or jurisdictions. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "with lower limits for higher-risk clients or jurisdictions"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Germany (Box 14): the system must include ongoing IT monitoring against whitelists and blacklists, including integration with EU and international sanctions lists. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The system must include ongoing surveillance against whitelists and blacklists"</sub>

## Conclusion

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 79: Stablecoins have seen rapid adoption, but as they have grown more popular they have been used more frequently for ML, TF and PF, and reporting indicates they are the most popular virtual asset for illicit transactions. The FATF has addressed stablecoins as either virtual assets or traditional financial assets depending on structure, and has required jurisdictions to apply the same AML/CFT/CPF obligations to intermediaries involved in stablecoin arrangements, including VASPs and financial institutions. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "reporting indicates that they are the most popular virtual asset for illicit transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 80: stablecoins are exposed to heightened ML/TF/PF risks when transferred P2P via unhosted wallets, exacerbated by characteristics such as price stability and ample liquidity. Available analysis indicates a significant proportion of illicit stablecoin transactions occurs via unhosted wallets including P2P, allowing actors to circumvent AML/CFT obligations. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a significant proportion of illicit stablecoin transactions occurs via unhosted wallets including P2P"</sub>
- **expected / limitation**: Paragraph 80: cases indicate threat actors have used stablecoins to purchase prohibited goods without cashing out through intermediaries, but data remains limited on broader use for purchases of goods and services or for P2P transactions. Jurisdictions and stakeholders should continue to closely monitor (the report says it is important to do so) whether stablecoins are increasingly used for purchases without traditional on- and off-ramps, and whether accurate data on the scale and proportion of P2P transactions can be obtained. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "data remains limited regarding the broader use of stablecoins for the purchase of goods and services or for P2P transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 81: the report identifies good practices that jurisdictions and the private sector could implement, and makes recommendations, including tailored regulatory frameworks for stablecoin issuers, enhanced risk mitigation for unhosted wallet transactions, advanced blockchain analytics, programmable controls embedded in smart contracts, and strengthened domestic and international co-operation mechanisms. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "programmable controls embedded in smart contracts and strengthened domestic and international co-operation mechanisms"</sub>

## Threat Actors Use of Stablecoins

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The report says characteristics that appeal to legitimate users also attract criminals to misuse stablecoins for money laundering, terrorism financing, sanctions evasion and proliferation financing; compared with volatile assets such as BTC or ETH, stablecoins like USDT and USDC offer a relatively stable medium for moving proceeds, and they are attractive for liquidity, interoperability and ease of cross-border transfer. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "offer a relatively stable medium for moving proceeds"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Threat actors increasingly favour stablecoins across multiple blockchains including Aptos, Avalanche, Ethereum, Solana and Tron, and the majority of illicit activity in stablecoins occurs in the secondary market. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The majority of illicit activity in stablecoins occurs in the secondary market"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Common themes across threat actors include use of unlicensed or unregistered VASPs (often in jurisdictions without AML/CFT controls for VASPs, or non-compliant VASPs), often OTC brokers or peer-to-peer platforms; obfuscation techniques and services such as mixers and chain hopping; and use of stablecoins at various points in a longer transaction process, including as proceeds from the predicate crime or in the last leg converting illicit VAs into fiat. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "use of unlicensed or unregistered VASPs, which are often located in jurisdictions without AM-L/CFT controls for VASPs"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Terrorist financiers and drug traffickers abuse stablecoins and other VAs as one method to raise and move money but still rely on traditional methods; DPRK relies on VAs and leverages stablecoins in laundering and cash-out; and in general any threat actor using unlicensed OTC brokers to cash out to fiat relies on stablecoins because OTC brokers typically demand stablecoins. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "relies on stablecoins as OTC brokers typically demand stablecoins"</sub>

## DPRK and Iran Proliferation Financing

- **descriptive / case example**: State-linked cybercriminal groups, notably DPRK's Lazarus Group, Andariel and Onyx Sleet, have rapidly adopted stablecoins for laundering proceeds of ransomware, phishing and other cyber-enabled crimes; Lazarus used malware and social engineering to infiltrate VA firms, including a nearly $1.46 billion theft in February 2025, laundered through mixers, cross-chain bridges and over 125,000 Ethereum wallets, indicating quick, high-volume transactions likely intended to complicate tracing. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "laundered the stolen funds through mixers, cross-chain bridges, and over 125,000 Ethereum wallets"</sub>

## Threat Actors Use of Stablecoins

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report cites Chainalysis as indicating that in 2025 stablecoins made up 84 percent of the USD $154B of illicit virtual asset transaction volume, overtaking Bitcoin as the leading asset in cybercrime-related on-chain transactions. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "stablecoins accounted for 84 percent of the USD $154B illicit virtual asset transaction volume in 2025"</sub>

## DPRK and Iran Proliferation Financing

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: DPRK also uses decentralised exchanges (DEXs) and occasionally anonymity-enhanced cryptocurrencies (AECs) in laundering, and DPRK launderers often convert funds into stablecoins, often USDT on the Tron blockchain, before exchanging them into fiat through OTC brokers or peer-to-peer platforms. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "often USDT on the Tron blockchain, before exchanging them into fiat currency through OTC brokers or peer-to-peer platforms"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: According to the MSMT, since at least 2023 the DPRK's 221 General Bureau (assessed as DPRK's primary weapons trading organisation, designated by the UN under its former name Korea Mining Development Trading Corporation, KOMID) has sought to expand stablecoin use beyond cyber-enabled financial crime to a means of exchange and payment for goods and services prohibited under UNSCRs; DPRK entities were assessed to use USDT in sales and transfers of military equipment and raw materials such as copper used in munitions production, indicating an effort to operationalise stablecoins in WMD-related procurement. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to expand the use of stablecoins beyond cyber-enabled financial crime"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report says transacting in stablecoins such as USDT gives UN-designated DPRK entities an additional mechanism to evade sanctions; compared with cash-based transactions needing physical cross-border transport, USDT payments are less logistically burdensome, faster and potentially lower-risk, facilitating procurement prohibited under UN Security Council resolutions. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "less logistically burdensome, faster, and potentially lower-risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Iranian actors are leveraging stablecoins to finance proliferation: the IRGC has turned to VAs for evasion activities, with blockchain analytics companies assessing several billion dollars were received by IRGC-associated addresses on-chain in 2024 and 2025; UN-sanctioned Iranian actors use VAs to obtain drone components and other high-tech equipment, Iran has begun accepting VAs for weapons payments, and Iranian actors have been assessed to transfer funds to UN-sanctioned actors such as the Houthis for weapons procurement. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "several billion dollars in funds were received by IRGC-associated addresses on-chain in 2024 and 2025"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Stablecoins are often the VAs preferred by Iranian illicit actors because of their superior ability to finance international trade; Iran is adapting to disruptions, and freezes of USDT for IRGC-linked addresses in mid-2025 may encourage sanctioned Iranian entities to move to other stablecoins, such as DAI, that do not have a freeze function. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may encourage sanctioned Iranian entities to move towards other stablecoins, such as DAI, that do not have a freeze function"</sub>

## Money Laundering

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Money launderers and perpetrators use stablecoins to collect proceeds of investment fraud, impersonation fraud, romance scams, pig butchering and sextortion; perpetrators may request funds in VAs (most commonly bitcoin or stablecoins), and laundering has involved VASP services that may be unregulated or non-compliant, mixers, DEXs, coin-swap platforms and peer-to-peer platforms, often intersecting with unregistered or unlicensed VASPs including OTC brokers to exchange stablecoins for fiat. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "investment fraud, impersonation fraud, romance scams, pig butchering, and sextortion"</sub>

## Box 1. Case Study: Use of online gambling platforms

- **descriptive / case example**: In a French case, funds originating from online casinos using VAs were rapidly converted into stablecoins using a custodian wallet hosted by a VASP operating in France, then converted into fiat and deposited into several bank accounts; the VASP filed an STR with TracFin, the French FIU. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The funds were rapidly converted into stablecoins using a custodian wallet hosted by a VASP operating in France"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Red flags identified by the VASP in the French gambling case included use of online casinos by individuals whose gambling activity appeared inconsistent with their customer profile or declared source of funds, and rapid conversion of gambling winnings into stablecoins without economic purpose, suggesting an attempt to obfuscate the source of funds. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "rapid conversion of gambling winnings into stablecoins without economic purposes suggests an attempt to obfuscate the source of funds"</sub>

## Money Laundering

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Drug trafficking organisations increasingly use stablecoins, particularly USDT on TRON and USDC on Ethereum, to pay overseas suppliers of synthetic drug precursors, settle drug transactions and launder proceeds; laundering schemes often involve money mules, OTC brokers, peer-to-peer platforms and rapid VA transactions across multiple blockchains, which complicate detection and disruption. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "particularly USDT on TRON and USDC on Ethereum"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Drug trafficking organisations also exploit high-volume online gambling platforms and merchant refund loops, where goods are bought with stolen identities and returned for refunds in stablecoins to third-party wallets; proceeds are often exchanged for fiat via unlicensed or unregistered VASPs, including OTC brokers, in jurisdictions with weak or non-existent AML/CFT controls. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "merchant refund loops, where goods are purchased using stolen identities and returned for refunds in stablecoins to third-party wallets"</sub>

## Box 2. Case Study: Cross-Border Drug Trafficking Proceeds Laundered via Stablecoins

- **descriptive / case example**: In a Canadian case, an organised crime network suspected of laundering drug trafficking proceeds bought ETH at a regulated VASP, swapped it for USDT and USDC on DEXs and coin-swap platforms without KYC checks, and transferred them to wallets controlled by a shell import-export company in Canada; funds were layered through multiple wallets and off-ramped via OTC brokers and regulated VASPs, in other instances with fiat wired to criminals' domestic bank accounts as "investment proceeds". (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "swapped for USDT (Tether) and USDC (Circle) on decentralised exchanges (DEXs) and coin-swap platforms without Know Your Customer (KYC) checks"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In the Canadian case, the FIU's financial intelligence disclosures and strategic analysis enabled law enforcement to trace the drug trafficking proceeds and identify the organised crime financial network and tradecraft. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "enabled law enforcement to trace the proceeds of drug trafficking and identify the organised crime financial network and tradecraft"</sub>

## Money Laundering

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Professional money launderers use sophisticated stablecoin layering techniques including chain-hopping, smurfing (breaking transactions into smaller amounts) and cross-chain transfers; they often use unregulated, unlicensed or non-compliant VASPs, DEXs lacking KYC protocols, virtual asset ATMs and online gambling platforms, and in some cases stablecoins are used to settle transactions in underground banking arrangements. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "chain-hopping, smurfing (breaking transactions into smaller amounts), and cross-chain transfers"</sub>

## Box 3. Case Study: Remuneration of trafficked humans in Southeast Asia through a Southeast Asia-based payment service provider

- **descriptive / case example**: In an Indian case (FIU India operational analysis), an Indian VDA service provider identified customers whose IP addresses traced to Southeast Asia and who consistently funded accounts with USDT, immediately liquidated it and withdrew the equivalent in INR to bank accounts with no other transaction types; as part of EDD the provider found mobile numbers unreachable, then reached 21 customers via a messaging platform who were construction labour, restaurant workers etc. in Southeast Asia, several sharing the same device fingerprint and IP at the time of transacting. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "funding their accounts with USDT, immediately liquidating it, and withdrawing the corresponding amount in INR to their bank accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The Indian case found that Indians working in scam centres in Southeast Asia (mainly Cambodia and Myanmar) used a Southeast Asia-based payment service provider to send salary (paid in cash in Thai Baht or USD) to family in India; six VDA SP users were on the list of unreturned Indian passengers who went on visitor visas and had not returned in 3 months, and 241 user locations were in and around scam compounds in Cambodia, with new compound locations identified. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "241 user locations shared were found to be in and around the scam compounds operating in Cambodia"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In October 2025 FIU India initiated compliance action and issued a notice under Section 13 of the Prevention of Money Laundering Act 2002 against a Southeast Asia-based payment service provider found to be illegally operating in India, and its operations were blocked in Indian jurisdiction. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "notice under Section 13 of Prevention of Money Laundering Act 2002 was issued by FIU India"</sub>

## Terrorist Financing

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Terrorist organisations using VAs increasingly favour stablecoins over Bitcoin; ISIL (Da'esh) and its regional affiliates and Al-Qaeda have been reported to solicit donations in stablecoins via encrypted platforms and social media, use stablecoins to transfer funds within the group, provide rotating wallet addresses to obfuscate recipients, and break larger sums into many small transfers passing through multiple VASPs to lower detection risk. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "They provide rotating wallet addresses to receive virtual assets, including stablecoins, from supporters worldwide"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: As noted in the FATF 2025 Comprehensive Update on Terrorist Financing Risks, VAs are more often used in combination with traditional TF methods; for example, a fundraising campaign may be set up on a crowdfunding platform, shared via social media and collect payments in VAs, after which terrorist entities and facilitators use offline and online means, including HOSSPs, to manage and move funds. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "virtual assets are more often used in combination with more traditional TF methods"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Terrorist financiers are increasingly observed pairing stablecoins with decentralised finance aggregators, coin-swap exchanges, anonymity-enhancing tools and unhosted wallets to circumvent compliance controls; campaigns have used recycled QR codes, domains and change addresses to persist despite takedowns, with patterns of dense multi-hop transfers, micro-splitting and re-aggregation before off-ramping, often via OTC brokers advertising minimal or no CDD requirements. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "dense multi-hop transfers, micro-splitting of funds, and re-aggregation before off-ramping"</sub>

## Box 4. Case Study: Detection of a case of terrorism financing involving stablecoins

- **descriptive / case example**: In early 2025 a VASP in France detected a retail customer making regular outbound stablecoin transfers to a specific external wallet over several weeks, small in value, frequent and lacking a legitimate commercial or personal rationale; blockchain analysis showed the receiving wallet had direct exposure to previously sanctioned addresses linked to terrorism financing networks, the sender appeared to be a facilitator, and the pattern suggested structuring (smurfing) to avoid thresholds and monitoring. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These transactions were small in value, frequent, and lacked a legitimate commercial or personal rationale"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In the French terrorism financing case, on-chain analysis tools able to track wallet exposure to high-risk entities flagged the receiving wallet as linked to multiple addresses previously involved in terrorism financing cases, and an STR with full transactional details, blockchain links and customer identification data was filed with TracFin, the FIU. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "capable of tracking wallet exposure to high-risk entities"</sub>

## Issuance

- **descriptive / limitation**: The report says the borderless nature of stablecoin activities may create incentives for issuers to establish headquarters in jurisdictions with weaker regulatory frameworks while operating cross-border, so issuers (including offshore-based and decentralised entities) could pose challenges for monitoring and supervision similar to other offshore VASPs; related risks including regulatory arbitrage and supervisory blind spots are examined in the FATF paper 'Understanding and Mitigating the Risks of Offshore VASPs'. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may create incentives for stablecoin issuers to establish their headquarters in jurisdictions with weaker regulatory frameworks"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Multi-issuance stablecoin schemes, in which an entity from jurisdiction A jointly issues a stablecoin with an entity from jurisdiction B so the coins are fungible and indistinguishable across the two, pose potential financial crime risks: they may hinder LEAs in tracing, freezing or seizing, make compliance responsibilities and obtaining customer information difficult where issuers comply with different AML/CFT requirements, and geolocation is complicated by VPNs. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "resulting in the issued stablecoins being fungible and indistinguishable across the two entities"</sub>

## Circulation

- **descriptive / limitation**: The report says the integrity of the stablecoin ecosystem depends on regulation of intermediary VASPs and thorough implementation of AML/CFT measures; compliance varies greatly across participants (some use blockchain analytics tools, others have under-resourced or weaker programs), and intermediary VASPs in jurisdictions that have not implemented Recommendation 15 may have no compliance programs at all, a critical loophole. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may have no compliance programs at all, creating a critical loophole in the global stablecoin regulatory framework"</sub>

## Redemption

- **descriptive / limitation**: A key vulnerability in redemption is that stablecoin holders need not use official redemption channels: stablecoins can be exchanged for fiat or backing assets via informal channels or non-compliant providers, including OTC brokers or peer-to-peer platforms operating without a licensed or registered intermediary, letting criminals bypass formal controls and obtain assets for reinvestment or use outside the VA ecosystem. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "stablecoin holders do not necessarily need to use official redemption channels to convert stablecoins into fiat currency"</sub>

## Circulation

- **mandatory / requirement**: The report states that the FATF requires VASPs and financial institutions that provide redemption services to apply AML/CFT measures to the ultimate beneficiaries of the assets. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF requires that VASPs and financial institutions providing redemption services apply AML/CFT measures on the ultimate beneficiaries"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The report says regulated VASPs and financial institutions that exchange stablecoins for the backing asset must gather customer information, screen for sanctions and follow the Travel Rule where it applies, alongside their other compliance obligations. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "must collect customer information, conduct sanctions screening, and comply with the Travel Rule (where applicable)"</sub>

## Redemption

- **expected / expectation**: The report says jurisdictions should actively identify and monitor unofficial and unregulated exchange mechanisms that have redemption-effecting outcomes, as they represent a critical loophole. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Jurisdictions should actively identify and monitor these unofficial and unregulated exchange mechanisms"</sub>

## P2P Transactions via Unhosted Wallets

- **descriptive / limitation**: P2P transfers via unhosted wallets present illicit finance risks that may not be mitigated by the AML/CFT/CPF framework for the stablecoin ecosystem; they occur directly between individuals or entities without a regulated intermediary VASP or FI, fall outside AML/CFT/CPF obligations and are inherently of higher risk. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "without the involvement of a regulated intermediary VASP or FI and therefore fall outside AML/CFT/CPF obligations"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The FATF previously conducted market metric analysis on P2P transactions but could not present definitive figures because of significant discrepancies in data among companies; the report says it is critical that jurisdictions continue to monitor P2P and unhosted wallet-related risks. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "unable to present definitive figures due to significant discrepancies in data among companies"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Despite the FATF approach, threat actors may transfer stablecoins through layered unhosted wallets transactionally distant from a Travel Rule-covered wallet (TRW); risks are aggravated cross-border, and because of near-instant settlement to recipient addresses outside the originating jurisdiction, competent authorities may face greater difficulty monitoring and tracing. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "layered unhosted wallets that are transactionally distant from Travel Rule-covered wallet (TRW)"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Although P2P transactions on public blockchains remain visible and traceable, the information is inherently pseudonymous, letting criminals obscure attribution; risks are exacerbated where criminals frequently generate new wallet addresses and abandon old ones, which can complicate authorities' assessment of whether P2P transactions are linked to crime or sanctioned entities. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "criminals frequently generate new wallet addresses and abandon previously used ones"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: A structural limitation is that there is no obliged entity responsible for filing an STR on P2P transactions via unhosted wallets; VASPs may implement robust Travel Rule requirements for transactions involving customers' unhosted wallets, including monitoring and STR reporting, but have limited visibility over and ability to monitor or report suspicious transactions involving multi-layered unhosted wallets transactionally distant from a TRW. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "absence of an obliged entity responsible for submitting STR in respect of such transactions"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The report says the FATF Guidance for a Risk-Based Approach to VAs and VASPs makes clear that VASPs should obtain the required originator and beneficiary information even for a transfer between their customer and an unhosted wallet. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "VASPs should collect the required originator and beneficiary information even when conducting a transfer between their customer"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The report says jurisdictions that let VASPs carry out transfers with unhosted wallets should assess the associated risks and put in place risk mitigation measures proportionate to them. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Jurisdictions that allow VASPs to undertake transfers with unhosted wallets should assess the risks and implement commensurate risk mitigation measures."</sub>
- **permissive / context**: The report says stablecoin issuers may play a complementary role, as law enforcement authorities can request them to freeze unhosted wallets suspected of containing misused stablecoins; in responding, issuers may monitor transactions associated with identified unhosted wallet addresses and, where appropriate under the applicable legal framework, submit an STR to the FIU. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "can be requested by law enforcement authorities (LEAs) to freeze unhosted wallets suspected of containing misused stablecoins"</sub>

## Cross-Chain activity

- **descriptive / limitation**: Blockchain networks and ecosystem developers increasingly prioritise interoperability for stablecoins relative to other VAs, enabling efficient cross-border transfers and legitimate remittances, but it may increase opportunities for threat actors to exploit multi-chain flows and obscure transaction trails, posing additional AML/CFT/CPF challenges. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may also increase opportunities for multi-chain flows to be exploited by threat actors"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Market analysis indicates sanctioned entities and other threat actors increasingly use stablecoins including through cross-chain activities, which may fragment transaction flows and complicate traceability, particularly with chain-hopping, since individual blockchains operate in isolation; interoperability may also weaken the issuers' ability to freeze or blacklist assets where centrally issued stablecoins circulate on other blockchains as newly created (wrapped) stablecoins. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "in the form of newly created (wrapped) stablecoins of the original stablecoins"</sub>

## Data Gaps

- **descriptive / limitation**: The report says blockchain records are immutable but lack critical context and are pseudonymous, so collecting CDD information including geographical location in line with Recommendations 10 and 15 is critical for law enforcement to obtain information from VASPs and FIs; lack of information on the geographical location of underlying wallets may also undermine cooperation with foreign counterparts. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "collection of CDD information including geographical location, in line with Recommendations 10 and 15 is critical"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Not all transactions occur on-chain: VA transactions between customers of the same VASP are mostly off-chain, recorded only in the VASP's internal ledger with customer information held internally; these transfers are usually faster and cheaper, but where the VASP is unlicensed or unregistered no intermediary collects customer identification or files STRs, complicating law enforcement detection and investigation. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "VA transactions between customers of the same VASP are mostly conducted off-chain"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The report notes that exchanges, issuers and custodians may pair on- and off-chain data to identify potentially illicit activity, but regulators and law enforcement do not have direct access to off-chain data or identified patterns. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "regulators and law enforcement do not have direct access to off-chain data or identified patterns"</sub>

## Effective Implementation of FATF Standards

- **expected / expectation**: The report says it is critical that jurisdictions place AML/CFT obligations on VASPs in the stablecoin ecosystem, including issuers and intermediary VASPs; these obligations are detailed in Recommendation 15 and should include implementation of preventive measures in accordance with Recommendations 9-21, and clear delineation of each party's AML/CFT obligations is important to drive compliance. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should include the implementation of preventive measures in accordance with Recommendations 9-21"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The report says jurisdictions should also ensure relevant AML/CFT obligations apply to financial institutions involved in stablecoin arrangements, including custodians of reserve assets for stablecoin issuers. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "including custodians of reserve assets for stablecoin issuers"</sub>

## Box 5: Japan - Implementation of Measures on Issuers and Intermediaries

- **descriptive / case example**: In Japan, both stablecoin issuers and intermediaries have AML/CFT obligations under the Act on Prevention of Transfer of Criminal Proceeds. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Both stablecoin issuers and intermediaries are subject to AML/CFT obligations under the Act on Prevention of Transfer of Criminal Proceeds"</sub>
- **mandatory / case example**: In Japan, eligible issuers (funds transfer service providers and trust companies) must carry out CDD when stablecoins are issued and redeemed, and must apply risk mitigation such as restricting transfers linked to suspected crime, relying on information from blockchain analytics firms or investigative authorities. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "must perform CDD at issuance and redemption and implement risk mitigation measures such a restricting transfers"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In Japan, intermediaries handling domestic or foreign stablecoins must register as Electronic Payment Instruments Exchange Service Providers and comply with AML/CFT obligations such as CDD, STR and the Travel Rule; given ML risks of DEXs, consideration is being given to imposing proportionate and appropriate AML/CFT obligations especially on their apps and other user interfaces, subject to further international discussion. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "must register as Electronic Payment Instruments Exchange Service Providers"</sub>

## Effective Implementation of FATF Standards

- **descriptive / limitation**: The report says the stablecoin ecosystem is complex and allocating appropriate AML/CFT obligations to stakeholders is not straightforward; in practice a relatively small number of jurisdictions have implemented regulation for stablecoins that explicitly takes into account their characteristics that differ from other VAs. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "allocating appropriate AML/CFT obligations to the relevant stakeholders is not straightforward"</sub>

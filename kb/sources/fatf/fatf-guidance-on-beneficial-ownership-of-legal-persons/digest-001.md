## BENEFICIAL OWNERSHIP OF LEGAL PERSONS

- **descriptive / context**: The FATF Guidance on Beneficial Ownership of Legal Persons is dated March 2023; FATF describes itself as an independent inter-governmental body whose Recommendations are recognised as the global AML/CFT standard. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF Recommendations are recognised as the global anti-money laundering (AML) and counter-terrorist financing (CFT) standard"</sub>

## 1. INTRODUCTION

- **descriptive / definition**: FATF footnote 1: the guidance uses 'corporate vehicles' to mean legal persons and legal arrangements as defined in the FATF glossary, e.g., companies, trusts, foundations and partnerships. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This paper uses the term corporate vehicles to mean _legal persons_ and _legal arrangements_"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: FATF (para 1) notes that despite their legitimate role, corporate vehicles' unique legal status lends them to complex schemes concealing true beneficial owners and the real reason for holding assets and conducting transactions. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "their unique legal status also lends them to be used in complex schemes designed to conceal the true beneficial owners"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Corporate vehicles can be misused for ML, bribery and corruption, insider dealing, tax fraud, TF, sanctions evasion and other illegal activity, and are attractive to criminals to disguise identity and conceal the origin, destination or purpose of funds. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "including money laundering (ML), bribery and corruption, insider dealings, tax fraud, terrorist financing (TF), sanctions evasion"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF (para 2) states misuse of corporate vehicles can be significantly reduced if information on legal and beneficial owners, the source of assets, and activities is available to authorities in a timely manner. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The misuse of corporate vehicles can be significantly reduced if information regarding both the legal owner and the beneficial owner"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: FATF (para 3) lists ways beneficial ownership is obscured: shell companies; complex multi-layer ownership and control structures with shares registered to other legal persons; bearer shares and bearer share warrants; unrestricted use of legal persons as directors; formal nominee shareholders and directors with undisclosed nominators; and informal nominees such as close associates and family. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "complex ownership and control structures involving many layers of shares registered in the name of other legal persons"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: FATF footnote 3 defines shell companies, for the guidance's purposes, as incorporated companies with no significant operations or related assets. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "shell companies are considered to be companies that are incorporated that have no significant operations or related assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: FATF footnote 4: 'competent authorities' (per the FATF glossary) essentially means all public authorities with designated responsibilities for combating ML and/or TF. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "refers to all public authorities with designated responsibilities for combating money laundering and/or terrorist financing"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: FATF footnote 2 cites the 2011 World Bank/UNODC StAR 'Puppet Masters' report, which examined over 150 large-scale corruption cases and found most involved one or more corporate vehicles to conceal beneficial ownership. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "over 150 cases of large-scale corruption were examined"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF (para 4) says legal and beneficial ownership information helps competent authorities, especially LEAs and FIUs, identify natural persons responsible for activity of concern or holding relevant information, enabling them to 'follow the money' and locate a person's assets within a jurisdiction. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "allows the authorities to “follow the money” in financial investigations and financial intelligence"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: FATF paragraph 2 says that, in general, a shortage of adequate, accurate and up-to-date beneficial ownership information helps ML/TF by obscuring three things: the identity of known or suspected criminals, the real purpose of an account or property held by a corporate vehicle, and the source or use of funds or property associated with that vehicle. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the lack of adequate, accurate and up-to-date beneficial ownership information facilitates ML/TF by disguising"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Enhancing transparency of legal persons makes them less attractive to criminals, and legal persons, FIs and DNFBPs play an important role by obtaining beneficial ownership information. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Enhancing the transparency of legal persons makes them less attractive for criminals"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Ensuring adequate, accurate and up-to-date beneficial ownership information is challenging where ownership chains span multiple jurisdictions or involve complex multi-layered networks; lack of such information is a significant impediment to investigations and financial intelligence. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is a significant impediment to investigation and the production of financial intelligence"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: FATF footnote 6: multi-jurisdictional structures are particularly hard to trace when seemingly legitimate related-entity transactions launder proceeds, and delays in international co-operation may frustrate investigations. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "transactions between related entities that appear legitimate are used to launder criminal proceeds"</sub>

## Revisions to Recommendation 24

- **descriptive / context**: FATF (para 5) established the first international beneficial ownership transparency standard in 2003, strengthened it in 2012, and recently strengthened it again in response to misuse of legal persons for ML, TF and proliferation financing and to mutual evaluation outcomes showing generally insufficient effectiveness. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "established the first international beneficial ownership transparency standard in 2003 and strengthened it in 2012"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: FATF footnote 8: in the mutual evaluation stocktake, effectiveness under Immediate Outcome (IO) 5 is the lowest of all IOs, with 9% of countries meeting it, and fewer than half of countries are rated compliant or largely compliant on Recommendation 24. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "with 9% of countries meeting the effectiveness requirement of the IO"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In many countries company beneficial owner information is unavailable because it is not collected and/or sufficiently verified at creation or at any later stage. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is not available as it is not collected and/or sufficiently verified at the time the corporate vehicle is created"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: FATF (para 6) states revised Recommendation 24 explicitly requires countries to use a multi-pronged approach, combining different mechanisms to collect beneficial ownership information so adequate, accurate and up-to-date information is available and timely accessible to competent authorities. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The revised Recommendation 24 explicitly requires countries9 to use a multi-pronged approach"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: To the extent beneficial ownership information is available to FIs and DNFBPs, it may help them apply CDD on corporate vehicles, identify beneficial owners, manage ML/TF risks, and implement controls including suspicious transaction reporting and sanctions implementation. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "including suspicious transaction reporting and sanctions implementation requirements"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF footnote 9: references to country or countries apply equally to territories or jurisdictions. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "All references in this guidance paper to country or countries apply equally to territories or jurisdictions"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: FATF (para 7) says revised R.24 requires countries to follow a risk-based approach considering risks of legal persons created in the country and of foreign-created legal persons with sufficient links to it. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "but also by foreign-created legal persons with sufficient links with their country"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF (para 8) notes G20 Leaders committed to implement FATF beneficial ownership standards (G20 Finance Ministers, Jakarta, 17-18 February 2022) and G7 countries committed to implement and strengthen company beneficial ownership registries. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "G7 countries also committed to implementing and strengthening registries of company beneficial ownership information"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The OECD and World Bank developed resources to help countries assess beneficial ownership transparency risks, and IMF lending, including COVID-19 emergency financing, routinely includes beneficial ownership transparency commitments for procurement contracts. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "IMF lending, including IMF emergency financing related to COVID-19 routinely includes measures or commitments"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF (para 9) says the purpose of its transparency and beneficial ownership standards is to prevent misuse of corporate vehicles for ML/TF, but they also support efforts against other offences such as tax crimes and corruption and help meet obligations under conventions like UNCAC, the Criminal Law Convention on Corruption and the OECD Anti-Bribery Convention. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "support the efforts to prevent and detect other designated categories of offences such as tax crimes and corruption"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF (para 10) previously issued 2014 guidance and a 2019 best practice paper on R.24 and R.1; this updated version on legal persons follows the March 2022 amendments to R.24 and its Interpretive Note, with legal arrangements addressed separately. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "following the adoption of amendments to Recommendation 24 and its Interpretive Note in March 2022"</sub>
- **expected / context**: FATF paragraph 7 says the revisions to Recommendation 24 specify that competent authorities' access to information should be timely, and that information should be adequate for identifying the beneficial owner, accurate (based on verification) and up-to-date. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "access to information by competent authorities should be timely, and information should be adequate for identifying the beneficial owner"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF paragraph 7 states that the revisions to Recommendation 24 also include stronger controls against the misuse of bearer shares and nominee arrangements. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "stronger controls to prevent the misuse of bearer shares and nominee arrangements"</sub>

## Target Audience and Purpose

- **descriptive / context**: FATF (para 11) says the guidance's audience is mainly national policy makers and practitioners, plus private sector stakeholders (FIs, DNFBPs, companies) required to comply with AML/CFT requirements; for the private sector it explains the link between transparency measures and CDD. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "explains the connection between specific transparency measures and CDD measures"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: FATF (para 13) states the guidance is non-binding and does not override the purview of national authorities. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This guidance is non-binding and does not override the purview of national authorities"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: FATF footnote 18: R.24 applies to all legal persons; the guidance describes requirements mainly for companies but similar requirements should apply to other legal person types, adjusted for their forms and structures. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "R.24 applies to all legal persons"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: FATF (para 14) says countries, FIs and DNFBPs should also refer to FATF's risk-based sectoral guidance (banking, life insurance, securities, MVTS, virtual assets/VASPs and VA red flags, legal professionals, accounting, TCSPs, prepaid/mobile/internet payments, real estate agents, risk-based supervision) when complying with R.24. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Countries, FIs and DNFBPs should also refer to these guidance documents when considering measures to comply"</sub>
- **expected / context**: FATF paragraph 12 says this updated guidance focuses mainly on Recommendation 24 (legal persons); for legal arrangements under Recommendation 25, the 2014 guidance should be referred to until the guidance is subsequently updated (a footnote notes FATF completed its review of Recommendation 25 in February 2023). (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the 2014 guidance should be referred to until the subsequent update of guidance"</sub>

## Domestic legal persons

- **mandatory / requirement**: FATF (para 15) states countries must understand the legal persons that can be incorporated under their laws and the associated ML/TF risks. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries must understand the legal persons that can be incorporated under the laws of their jurisdiction"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Countries should have mechanisms to identify and describe the types, forms and basic features of legal persons, and the processes for creating them and for obtaining and recording their basic and beneficial ownership information. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identify and describe the different types, forms and basic features of legal persons in the country"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Countries should make this information publicly available and assess, manage and mitigate the ML/TF risks of the different types of legal persons. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "assess the ML/TF risks associated with the different types of legal persons"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: FATF (para 16) says countries should identify, assess, manage and mitigate ML/TF risks from foreign legal persons with sufficient links to the country, determining 'sufficient link' on a risk basis. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "in relation to foreign legal persons, which have sufficient links to the country"</sub>

## Foreign legal persons

- **descriptive / data or feature**: Indicative sufficient-link tests: permanent establishment, branch or agency in the country; or significant business activity, defined by a monetary threshold or other suitable parameters. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Significant business activity may be defined either in terms of a monetary threshold"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Another sufficient-link test: significant ongoing business relations with AML/CFT-regulated FIs or DNFBPs in the country, with significance judged by market size, market impact or sectors of operation. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Has significant, ongoing business relations with financial institutions or DNFBPs subject to AML/CFT regulation"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Another sufficient-link test: significant real estate or other investment (e.g., high value commercial or residential real estate, securities), with significance judged against average local asset prices or quantity held. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "with reference to the average price of the real estate/corresponding asset market in the country"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Another sufficient-link test: the legal person employs staff in the country or is tax resident there (e.g., place of effective management or administration); these examples mirror Footnote 49 to the INR.24. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Employs staff, or is a tax resident (e.g., by reason of having its place of effective management or administration there)"</sub>

## Risk assessment

- **expected / requirement**: FATF (para 17) says countries should conduct a comprehensive risk assessment of all types of legal persons, considering country-specific legal and regulatory context and international threats; it may be standalone or part of the national ML/TF assessment. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Countries should conduct a comprehensive risk assessment of all types of legal persons"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: FATF (para 20) notes the risk assessment may be done at national, supra-national or subnational level, ultimately to inform the national assessment. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may be undertaken at the national, supra-national or subnational levels"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: FATF paragraph 18 says steps countries can consider in a legal-person risk assessment include collecting and analysing registration statistics, such as incorporation volumes and trends, for all types of legal person that can be created under national law. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Collect and analyse registration statistics (e.g., incorporation volumes and trends)"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: Another step countries can consider (paragraph 18) is reviewing and analysing STRs and law enforcement and prosecution cases in which legal persons were misused, recording the nature of the abuse, the type of structure (e.g., nominee shareholders or directors, shell companies), jurisdiction of incorporation, concealment techniques, involvement of intermediaries such as lawyers, accountants and TCSPs, and other details. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Review and analyse suspicious transaction reports, and national law enforcement and prosecutorial cases"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: Another step countries can consider (paragraph 18) is identifying the most common typologies of abuse of domestic or foreign legal structures with a nexus to the jurisdiction and assessing how often they occur. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Identify the most common typologies of abuse of domestic or foreign legal structures"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: Another step countries can consider (paragraph 18) is investigating TCSP advertising that promotes the jurisdiction as an international incorporation centre to non-residents, including which attributes, such as anonymity or asset protection, are advertised. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Investigate advertising practices by TCSPs promoting the jurisdiction as an international centre for incorporation"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: Another step countries can consider (paragraph 18) is consulting external experts from the private sector, civil society and academia who have significant expertise in setting up legal structures and their benefits and risks. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Conduct expert consultations with external experts from the private sector, civil society, and academia"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: FATF paragraph 19 says that, when assessing risks of different legal-person types, countries should also consider exposure to legal persons created in foreign jurisdictions, such as FATF high-risk or increased-monitoring jurisdictions or jurisdictions under TF-related sanctions or embargoes (e.g., issued by the UN), and the types of service providers involved. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "high-risk jurisdictions subject to a call for action or under increased monitoring of the FATF"</sub>

## Risk mitigation

- **permissive / recommendation**: Example mitigation (para 22): disclosure requirements for legal persons wishing to operate in, own significant assets in, or obtain licences in the country. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Applying disclosure requirements to legal persons that wish to operate in, own significant assets, or apply for licenses"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Example mitigation: investigating violations of beneficial ownership reporting rules using specialised expertise in corporate structures and higher-risk entities. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Investigate violations of beneficial ownership reporting rules"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Example mitigation: increasing the investigative and enforcement capacities and powers of the corporate registry, beneficial ownership registry or other relevant body. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Increase investigative and enforcement capacities and powers of the corporate registry"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Example mitigation: requiring legal persons to have at least one natural-person resident director so they are traceable and sanctionable by competent authorities. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "at least one natural person resident director within the jurisdiction"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Example mitigation: setting an appropriate beneficial ownership threshold, and arrangements letting actors in higher-risk sectors detect and report activity of concern. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Setting an appropriate beneficial ownership threshold"</sub>

## Understanding and addressing cross-border risks

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: FATF (para 23) notes complex multi-jurisdiction company structures can carry higher risk, and criminal networks deliberately split company formation, asset ownership, professional intermediaries and bank accounts across countries to evade regulation. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "deliberately split company formation, asset ownership/administration, location of professional intermediaries, and location of bank accounts"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Because cross-border networks make it hard for any single jurisdiction to see the full picture, countries should include analysis of different types of cross-border risks in their risk assessments. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "important for countries to include consideration and analysis of different types of cross-border risks in their risk assessment"</sub>

## Sharing and disseminating results of the risk assessment

- **expected / expectation**: FATF (para 24) says risk assessment results should be shared widely among competent authorities and, with redactions, with key private sector partners and civil society and academic partners, consistent with Recommendation 1. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The results of the risk assessment should be shared widely among competent authorities"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Countries can consider periodic or ongoing public-private dialogue on risk assessments and typology sharing. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "maintaining periodic/ongoing dialogue between public and private sector stakeholder on risk assessments"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: FATF (para 25) suggests countries may publish their assessment results in full or part, with redactions, to advance international understanding of risk factors. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries may consider making results of their assessment (in full or in part) publicly available"</sub>

## Risk mitigation

- **expected / expectation**: FATF paragraph 21 says countries should take appropriate steps to manage and mitigate the risks identified in the legal-person risk assessment. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Countries should take appropriate steps to manage and mitigate the risks identified in the risk assessment"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: To enable that mitigation, FATF paragraph 21 says it is advisable that the risk assessment involve sufficient analysis of the sources, nature and extent of the risk involved. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is advisable that the risk assessment involves sufficient analysis of the sources, nature, and the extent of risk"</sub>

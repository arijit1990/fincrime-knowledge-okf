## Definitions

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: FATF (para 126) notes many nominee arrangements have legitimate purposes with minimal or no ML/TF risk, but nominees can be a deliberate device to evade BO transparency rules and facilitate misuse of corporate vehicles for ML and related crimes. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "nominees can also be used as a deliberate device to evade beneficial ownership transparency rules"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF (para 127) says the most common nominee types are nominee directors and nominee shareholders. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The most common types of nominees are nominee directors and nominee shareholders"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: FATF Glossary: a nominator is an individual, group or legal person that directly or indirectly instructs a nominee to act for them as director or shareholder, sometimes called a 'shadow director' or 'silent partner'. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "also sometimes referred to as a “shadow director” or “silent partner.”"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: FATF Glossary: a nominee is an individual or legal person instructed by a nominator to act on their behalf in a certain capacity regarding a legal person. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Nominee is an individual or legal person instructed by another individual or legal person (“the nominator”)"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: FATF Glossary: a nominee director (also 'resident director') routinely exercises director functions on behalf of and subject to the nominator's instructions, and is never the beneficial owner of a legal person. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A Nominee Director is never the beneficial owner of a legal person"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: FATF Glossary: a nominee shareholder exercises voting rights per the nominator's instructions and/or receives dividends for the nominator, and is never the beneficial owner based on shares held as nominee. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A nominee shareholder is never the beneficial owner of a legal person based on the shares it holds as a nominee"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF (para 128) notes nominator-nominee relationships take various legal or factual forms and terminologies across jurisdictions; footnote 70 notes 'director' may include other top management roles such as management board member or managing director. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "“director” may capture various other highest-level corporate roles responsible for general management of the legal person"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: FATF (para 129) describes nominee arrangements as a spectrum from a mere 'front' with no knowledge or control (a 'signature for sale') to a substantive, independent role such as representing shareholders of a large listed company or providing board expertise. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "ranging from situations in which the nominee is simply a “front” with no real connection with or knowledge or control of the company"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: FATF (para 130) lists relevant contracts: professional nominee arrangements and nominee director/shareholder services from corporate service providers, powers of attorney used with nominee arrangements, and 'signature for sale' agreements such as a 'nominee director declaration'. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Power of attorney arrangements used in concert with nominee arrangements"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: FATF (para 131) says countries should examine arrangements in their jurisdiction and assess applicability of nominee mitigation measures under INR.24 paragraph 13 and R.25, e.g., arrangements mandating acting for another person; footnote 72 says arrangements should be treated by actual purpose. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Countries should examine specific types of arrangements that exist in their jurisdiction72"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: FATF (para 132) examples: a nominator may be a principal, silent partner or shadow director; a nominee shareholder may be a licensed service provider, professional party representative or anyone; a nominee director may be a legal professional, corporate service provider or anyone, and resident or local directors may be nominees. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a resident director or local director may be a nominee"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: FATF (para 133) says nominees may be de jure (formal agreement with a TCSP, notary, lawyer or tax advisor), de facto (conduct makes them a nominee in law), or informal without a written contract, e.g., a family member, friend, employee or associate standing in for the nominator. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Nominees may be de jure, often the product of a formal legal agreement with a TCSP, notary, lawyer or tax advisor"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF footnote 74: some jurisdictions legally define hidden beneficial owners who control through a formal or informal nominee as 'shadow directors'. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Some jurisdictions have defined hidden beneficial owners “shadow directors” under the law"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: FATF footnote 75 examples of informal nominees: people in financial hardship signing for bogus companies for payment, often without contract and sometimes under coercion; and anyone acquiring, holding or using assets under a criminal organisation's power of disposal on its behalf. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "persons facing financial hardship sign for bogus companies against payment, often without a contract"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: FATF (para 134) states de facto (shadow) directors and informal nominees mean nominee arrangements may be common and risky even in jurisdictions with no legislative provisions for nominees, because they exist in practice. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may present ML/TF risks also in jurisdictions that have no specific legislative provisions for nominee directors"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: FATF (para 135) clarifies a nominee acts routinely on the nominator's instructions, whereas a one-off or non-routine delegation of powers 'in the name of' the nominator would usually not be a nominee relationship. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "on a one-off or non-routine basis, would under most circumstances, not qualify as a nominee relationship"</sub>

## Mechanisms to prevent and mitigate the risk of the misuse of nominee arrangements

- **mandatory / requirement**: FATF (para 136, citing INR.24 paragraph 13a-c) says countries are required to apply one or more mechanisms against nominee misuse: transparency requirements, licensing of nominees with nominator disclosure, or prohibition, regardless of whether parties are natural or legal persons. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries are required to apply one or more of the following mechanisms to prevent and mitigate the risk of the misuse of nominees"</sub>

## a) Transparency Requirement:

- **mandatory / requirement**: Under the transparency option, nominee shareholders and directors must disclose their nominee status and the nominator's identity to the company. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "must disclose that they are acting as a nominee (i.e., their nominee status) and the identity of the nominator"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: The company or nominee should report this to the designated register or alternative mechanism (shareholder register, company register or BO register), whether the arrangement is formal or informal, and the BO registry or alternative mechanism should obtain and hold it. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "regardless of whether the nominee arrangements are formal or informal"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Countries should include nominee status in public registry information, e.g., by labelling or asterisking nominee directors' and shareholders' names. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "by adding a label or an asterisk to the names of directors and shareholders who are nominee directors and shareholders"</sub>

## b) Licensing Requirement:

- **mandatory / requirement**: Under the licensing option, nominee shareholders and directors must be licensed to offer nominee services or belong to a licensed, AML-regulated profession; countries may create a dedicated regime or rely on existing FI/DNFBP and TCSP licensing under R.10, 22, 23, 26 and 28. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "must be licensed in order to offer nominee services or belong to a licensed and regulated profession under AML regulations"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Under licensing, nominee status and nominator identity must be held by the designated public body or mechanism, and nominees must maintain information on their nominator and the natural person ultimately represented, available to authorities on request. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "must maintain information identifying their nominator and the natural person on whose behalf they are ultimately acting"</sub>

## c) Prohibition:

- **descriptive / requirement**: Under the prohibition option, nominee arrangements are explicitly prohibited and the prohibition is enforced. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Nominee arrangements are explicitly prohibited and this prohibition is enforced"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Because de facto and informal nominees exist, merely lacking or removing legislative provisions for nominees usually will not ensure nominees are unavailable in practice; detection of undisclosed nominees and enforcement through proportionate and dissuasive sanctions would be required. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the absence or removal of legislative provisions for nominees will usually not be sufficient"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: A prohibition may be combined with transparency or licensing, e.g., banning corporate directors while licensing or requiring transparency for other nominee types. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a prohibition of corporate directors in combination with licensing or transparency requirements"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: FATF (para 137) notes the private, covert nature of informal nominees without written contracts makes preventive measures inherently hard to apply. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there are inherent challenges in applying preventive measures"</sub>
- **mandatory / expectation**: Addressing informal nominee risk requires greater emphasis on sanctioning false BO declarations rather than preventive measures; false declarations by undisclosed nominees can be evidence in investigations and grounds for sanctions, and countries may also use measures like statutory director duties. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a greater emphasis on applying sanctions for false declarations of beneficial ownership (vis-à-vis preventive measures) is required"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: FATF (para 138) says for informal nominee relationships FIs/DNFBPs could request and record the main elements of the relationship, and depending on risk, registries, FIs and DNFBPs may need sensitising to undisclosed nominee risk. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs/DNFBPs could request information on the main elements of the relationship and record this for documentation purposes"</sub>
- **descriptive / requirement**: R.24 adds more specific requirements: nominees disclose the nominator (the TCSP's 'client') upfront to the registry, have nominee director status publicly recorded, and/or countries license nominee services rather than only monitoring them. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to have their status as nominee director publicly recorded, and/or to ensure the country licenses such nominee service provision"</sub>

## Risks of misuse of nominee arrangements

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: FATF (para 140) says nominee arrangements may be deliberately used to obscure the beneficial owner by keeping their name off public records or undisclosed to transaction counterparties. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "keeping the owner’s name off public records, or not disclosing them to the counterparty in a transaction"</sub>

## “Signature for Sale” arrangements

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: FATF (para 141) notes TCSPs relatively commonly market 'signature for sale' nominee arrangements where the nominee has no substantive role; a separate private agreement, often a power of attorney, lets the beneficial owner retain control behind the scenes. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a separate private legal agreement with the nominee (power of attorney is a commonly seen form)"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: In documented cases (StAR 2022, 'Signatures for Sale'), nominees pre-sign undated resignation letters so the beneficial owner can dismiss them unilaterally and, if needed, retroactively. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "nominees pre-sign undated resignation letters to allow the beneficial owner to fire the nominee unilaterally"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Signature-for-sale arrangements may breach BO transparency law and directors' standard duties; the transparency, licensing and prohibition measures mainly strengthen enforcement of BO identification but also help ensure nominee directors substantively fulfil their duties. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they may also often violate directors’ standard responsibilities to the company"</sub>

## Situations where risks of misuse of nominee arrangements may be low

- **expected / expectation**: FATF (para 142, citing INR.1 paragraph 2) says the extent of R.24 nominee measures should reflect country ML/TF risk, and based on risk assessment countries may differentiate by entity nature and purpose (listed companies and subsidiaries, public entities, holding, operating or domiciliary entities), sector, officer risk profile and other factors. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the extent of the measures that countries should take in accordance with"</sub>

## c) Prohibition:

- **descriptive / context**: FATF (para 139) notes that under Recommendations 22 and 23, TCSPs offering nominee director services (defined there as acting as a director for a client) or nominee shareholder services are already subject to preventive measures, including identifying the beneficial owner. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "TCSPs offering nominee director services (there defined as “acting as a director for a client”) or nominee shareholder services"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: FATF (para 139) states that Recommendation 28 obliges countries to monitor the provision of nominee director and nominee shareholder services by TCSPs to ensure compliance. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Recommendation 28 obliges countries to monitor such service provision to ensure compliance"</sub>

## Situations where risks of misuse of nominee arrangements may be low

- **permissive / context**: FATF (para 143) says countries could exempt nominees from INR mitigation measures where abuse risk is low and adequate, accurate, up-to-date BO information is available, e.g., shareholders appointing a director to represent them in a large listed company, or directors delegated within a group or collaboration for expertise or coordination. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The typical corporate practice for a (group of) shareholders to appoint a director to represent their interests"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: Further low-risk exemption examples: legally required representative directors (e.g., employee representatives on pension scheme boards, state representatives in state-owned entities, German employee supervisory board representatives, directors appointed by a multilateral development bank). (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "director appointed by a multilateral development bank to subsidiaries and investee companies"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: Further examples: low-risk AML-regulated investment funds or pension funds acting for investors or pensioners, and delegation of shareholder rights incidental to a banking relationship (e.g., depository bank). (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Certain types of AML regulated investment funds acting on behalf of their investors"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: Further examples: independent voting proxies where shareholders cannot attend a general assembly (e.g., Swiss independent proxy and depository representative), and FIs acting as nominee shareholders when transacting in their own name for clients in banking or brokerage. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FI acting as nominee shareholder when carrying out transactions in their own name but on account of the client"</sub>

## 16. SANCTIONS

- **descriptive / context**: FATF (para 144, citing INR.24 paragraph 16) describes clear liability and effective, proportionate and dissuasive sanctions as a key feature of implementing R.24, applicable to both natural and legal persons. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Establishing clear liability and effective, proportionate and dissuasive sanctions is a key feature"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: For legal certainty, clear rules should set out obligations of legal persons (e.g., reporting to registers) and natural persons (e.g., shareholders informing the company) for each R.24 requirement. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is important to set out clear rules on the obligations of legal persons"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Depending on legal tradition, breaches caused by intentional or negligent conduct of senior management or authorised persons (e.g., to benefit the legal person) could be specifically addressed for liability, and senior management could be liable for employee breaches. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Liability could also be attributed to the senior management for breaches of requirements by employees under their responsibility"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: FATF (para 145) says sanctions may be administrative, civil or criminal, financial or non-financial (e.g., deprivation of rights to participate in companies, striking off the register), consistent with the overall legal framework. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "deprivation of rights with respect to participating in companies, striking off the register"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: FATF (para 146) says countries may apply criminal and/or administrative sanctions on the legal person or its representatives, including resident agents in certain circumstances. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "including, e.g., resident agents in certain circumstances"</sub>
- **expected / method**: FATF (para 147) factors for effective sanctions: an empowered sanctioning authority, and a sufficiently broad range (minor sanctions for late filing, personal liability for false declarations, restrictions on incorporation, penalties for non-reporting, warning letters, strike-off for repeat breaches). (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "minor sanctions for late filing, personal liability for false declaration to restriction on incorporation"</sub>
- **expected / method**: Further factors: sanctions dissuasive even for the largest legal entities, consistent with gravity and repetition (possibly considering turnover), and systematic detection and sanctioning of breaches. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Is this range of sanctions suitably dissuasive for even the largest legal entities in the jurisdiction?"</sub>

## Wire transfers and beneficial ownership as part of CDD

- **expected / requirement**: FATF (para 148) says for wire transfers FIs should apply R.10 CDD on occasional transactions, including identifying and reasonably verifying the beneficial owner of the originator or beneficiary, and R.16 requires collecting originator information that accompanies the transfer (INR.16 paragraphs 11-18). (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Recommendation 16 requires financial institutions to take further measures such as collecting certain originator information"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: FATF Glossary: a VASP is any natural or legal person not otherwise covered by the Recommendations that as a business conducts for others: VA-fiat exchange; VA-VA exchange; VA transfer; safekeeping/administration of VAs or control instruments; or financial services related to an issuer's VA offer/sale. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "participation in and provision of financial services related to an issuers offer and/or sale of a virtual asset"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: FATF (para 149) says countries should prevent misuse of VASPs and consider facilitating VASP access to BO and control information for R.10 and R.22 compliance. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "consider measures to facilitate access to beneficial ownership and control information by VASPs"</sub>

## Virtual assets

- **expected / requirement**: FATF (para 150) says in VASP licensing or registration authorities should prevent criminals or associates from holding or being beneficial owners of significant or controlling interests or management functions in a VASP. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to prevent criminals or their associates from holding, or being the beneficial owner of, a significant or controlling interest"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Countries, including those prohibiting virtual assets, should identify persons carrying out VASP activities without licence or registration and apply sanctions (INR.15 paragraph 3, footnote 44). (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "including those that have decided to prohibit virtual assets, should take action to identify natural or legal persons"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: FATF (para 151) says countries should ensure VASPs can manage risks of anonymity-enhancing technologies so they are not used to obscure legal or beneficial ownership of VAs. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "manage and mitigate the risks of engaging in activities that involve the use of anonymity-enhancing technologies"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Anonymity-enhancing technologies include anonymity-enhanced cryptocurrencies (AECs), mixers, tumblers, privacy wallets and other tools obfuscating sender, recipient or holder identity. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "cryptocurrencies (AECs), mixers, tumblers, privacy wallets and other technologies that obfuscate the identity"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: If a VASP cannot manage and mitigate those risks, it should not be permitted to engage in such activities (VA RBA Guidance paragraph 174). (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "then the VASP should not be permitted to engage in such activities"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: FATF (para 152) says VASPs should obtain and verify customer identification under R.10, and for covered VA activities (payments, transfers, issuance) verify customer and BO information before or during establishment of the relationship. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should be completed before or during the course of establishing the relationship"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: FATF (para 153) says counterparty VASP due diligence should be completed when entering cross-border correspondent relationships or before transmitting travel rule information, and refreshed periodically or when new risk emerges. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Counterparty VASP due diligence should be completed when engaging in cross-border correspondent relationship"</sub>
- **expected / method**: A VASP may obtain R.10 and R.13 information directly from the counterparty but should verify it against reliable independent sources, e.g., corporate registries, regulated-institution/VASP lists, BO registers and sources in the BCBS General Guide on Account Opening. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "corporate registries, registries maintained by competent authorities on the regulated institutions list"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF (para 154) does not intend an asset to be both a VA and a financial asset, though national frameworks may classify the same asset differently or under multiple categories; authorities should consider which regime can appropriately apply to a new digital asset. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FATF does not intend for an asset to be both a VA and a financial asset at the same time"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Where a digital asset is the functional equivalent of a bearer share (e.g., transferable tokens), authorities should consider how mitigation applies; one option is applying R.24 bearer share measures (INR.24 paragraph 12). (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "if the asset in question is the functional digital equivalent of a bearer share (e.g., in the case of transferable tokens)"</sub>

## 18. APPLICABILITY OF RELEVANT REGULATORY REGIMES

- **permissive / context**: FATF (para 155) says countries may let regulated institutions or recognised stock exchanges use their basic and BO information to comply with obligations ensuring BO is adequate, accurate, up to date and timely determinable. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Where information on basic and beneficial ownership exists within a regulated institution or a recognised stock exchange"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: FATF (para 156) says countries must consider factors: stock exchange information may be used subject to the exchange's processes, and FI/DNFBP information may be used if held by entities effectively supervised in line with FATF risk-based supervision guidance. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "If this information is held by entities that are effectively supervised in line with the FATF Guidance on risk-based supervision91"</sub>

## Information held by stock exchanges

- **expected / expectation**: FATF (para 157) says countries should consider whether exchanges have processes to determine accuracy of BO information, including mechanisms such as sanctions for violating shareholder and BO reporting. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "sanctions in case of violation of shareholder and BO reporting"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: For listed companies, BO information in public corporate information such as annual reports could qualify as a supplementary measure, and where stringent disclosure and exchange processes exist, current public shareholder and BO information may be sufficient. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "publicly available current information on shareholder and beneficial ownership may be sufficient"</sub>

## Trust and company service providers (TCSPs)

- **descriptive / context**: FATF (para 158) notes TCSPs play an important role conducting CDD during establishment and ongoing management of corporate vehicles, with services offered by various entities including lawyers and accountants. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "TCSPs play an important role in undertaking CDD on their clients both during the establishment of corporate vehicles"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Company services are an area where criminals may engage lawyers and accountants to conceal beneficial ownership, and specialist TCSPs may be unregulated absent specific AML/CFT regulation and a designated supervisor. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the provision of company services is one area where criminals may seek to engage such professionals to conceal beneficial ownership"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: FATF (para 159) notes that even where legal professionals and TCSPs are subject to AML/CFT, BO-related CDD implementation is often deficient and supervision often ineffective. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "deficiencies often exist in how the CDD obligations with respect to beneficial ownership are being implemented"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Countries should ensure all legal professionals and TCSPs are required to conduct CDD under Recommendation 22. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries should ensure that all legal professionals and TCSPs are required to conduct CDD pursuant to Recommendation 22"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: FATF (para 160) says countries should refer to RBA sectoral guidance when deciding whether TCSP-held information is reliable for determining basic and BO information. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "when considering if entities can utilise information held by TCSPs as reliable information"</sub>

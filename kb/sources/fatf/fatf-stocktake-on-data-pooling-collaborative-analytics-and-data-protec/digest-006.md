## 6.6. Market Structure and Competition

- **descriptive / limitation**: The report says the larger the amount of data, the more likely the analytical outcome is to be accurate, and that currently only large incumbent FIs have sufficiently vast data sets to use advanced data analytics efficiently; it says there is a need to raise awareness of cross-institution data processing so small to mid-sized FIs can also benefit and any cost advantages can be shared. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "only large incumbent FIs have sufficiently vast data sets to use advanced data analytics efficiently"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Respondents noted that processing large sets of customer information between FIs could raise competition concerns, and could lead to selective sharing with only a small group of "trusted" participants and an uneven sharing framework, with a potential transfer of ML/TF risks from FIs that have information sharing mechanisms to those lacking them, as bad actors thwarted by the former may gravitate to the latter; FIs or sectors lacking such mechanisms may face additional ML/TF risks and additional risk mitigation may have to be considered. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there might be a transfer of ML/TF risks from FIs that have information sharing mechanisms to those lacking such arrangements"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: FIs may be reluctant to share commercially sensitive information that has the potential to distort competition, and varying IT capabilities, differing or inadequate IT systems and diverse data formats can hinder pooling and collaborative analytics, potentially disadvantaging institutions with legacy IT infrastructure and excluding them from data sharing initiatives. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "reluctant to share commercially sensitive information that has the potential to distort competition in the market"</sub>

## 6.7. Technology Costs and Constraints

- **descriptive / limitation**: A number of respondents noted that the scalability of privacy enhancing technologies and advanced analytics is affected by significant start-up cost; large FIs may be able to invest or buy licences while many small or middle-sized institutions still lag in updating legacy technologies, which may result in a reduced data pool covering only institutions that can afford the start-up costs. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "scalability of privacy enhancing technologies and advanced analytics, in particular, is impacted by the significant start-up cost"</sub>

## 6.6. Market Structure and Competition

- **expected / expectation**: The stocktake says that letting a limited number of FIs access and exchange data should not hand them an unfair advantage, since the competitiveness of financial services firms increasingly depends on access to real-time big data sets. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Access and exchange of data amongst a limited number of FIs should not provide them with an unfair advantage"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: The stocktake says competition law concerns may also be relevant when assessing an AML/CFT data sharing arrangement, so that a level playing field is kept and exclusionary conduct by potential competitors is avoided. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "competition law concerns may also have a place in the assessment of an AML/CFT data sharing arrangement"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The stocktake states that where access to data is warranted, it must be given on fair, reasonable and non-discriminatory terms and in a way that neither enables nor facilitates collusion. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "when data access is warranted it must be granted on fair, reasonable and non-discriminatory terms"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The stocktake states that any data exchange among FIs must be restricted to what is strictly necessary. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Furthermore, the data exchange must be limited to what is strictly necessary."</sub>

## 6.7. Technology Costs and Constraints

- **descriptive / limitation**: Respondents noted advanced analytics such as machine learning are expensive to implement and maintain, compounded by the need to integrate new analytics with legacy systems (additional upgrade costs), by the excessive costs of trialling new analytics while running existing systems, and by the cost of maintaining technical experts to develop and refine complex models over time. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "trialling of new advanced analytics while simultaneously running existing systems was also noted as barrier"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Pooling and analysing large amounts of data requires sufficient computing power to run algorithmic models, and the size of data sets can significantly influence the cost of computing. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "need to have sufficient computing power in place to run the algorithmic models"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The report says large AML/CFT data sets (transactions and customer information) are "heavy" and difficult to move, referred to as data gravity, and it is essential to consider data gravity, and its growth potential once centralised, when developing data pooling initiatives. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is therefore essential to consider data gravity when developing data pooling initiatives"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Deploying advanced analytics such as machine learning in AML/CFT requires validating data or confirming whether a pattern truly indicated criminal conduct; model validation can be difficult in ML/TF because investigations may take several years, and in many cases FIs are not informed of the ultimate outcome of an STR filed with the FIU or whether it led to a ML or TF conviction. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs are not informed of the ultimate outcome of the STR filed to the FIU"</sub>

## 6.8. Defensive Reporting and De-risking

- **descriptive / limitation**: The report says that, by leveraging privacy enhancing technologies, an institution may overcome data localisation and STR confidentiality requirements and learn whether another institution filed an STR against its customer without obtaining the sensitive underlying data, but this could exacerbate defensive STR filing behaviour. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this has the possibility of exacerbating defensive STR filing behaviour"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The report says overreliance on a system of sharing suspicious information could lead an FI to regard a customer as suspicious based solely on third-party information that may be inaccurate or whose grounds for suspicion were ultimately rejected by the FIU, which could unintentionally and unethically deny a legitimate customer access to the financial system or subject customers to further clarifications and delays in service. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "regard a customer as suspicious based solely on third party information, which may be inaccurate"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The report says the mere existence of a suspicion does not necessitate systematic STR filing by other institutions receiving such information; it may instead be an element of the institution's risk analysis resulting in enhanced CDD. Widespread collaborative analytics and data pooling may increase identified suspicions (particularly where defensive reporting has occurred) and substantially increase enhanced CDD, raising compliance costs, which could discourage use of the technology or lead to de-risking behaviour. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the mere existence of a suspicion does not necessitate the systematic filing of an STR by other institutions"</sub>

## 6.9. Security

- **descriptive / limitation**: The report says introducing new technology to pool and process data brings new and significant vulnerabilities for cybercriminals to exploit; a centralised data pool raises serious cyber security and national security concerns and policy questions about who is responsible for monitoring the security of repositories and who is accountable for failures or cyber-attacks, pooling larger quantities of data creates possibility of a catastrophically large leak by a single party, and insider threat protections become more important as pooling grows. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the use of technologies to establish a centralised data pool raises serious cyber security vulnerabilities"</sub>

## 6.10. Avoidance of Analytical Bias in Artificial Intelligence

- **descriptive / expectation**: The report says a crucial consideration for AI analytics on pooled information is that advanced analytics exclude human bias and so prevent discrimination (e.g., based on religion, race, gender, age, sexual orientation, ethnicity); bias may be introduced through the inclusion or exclusion of certain data or in the programming of the algorithmic model, and it is imperative that development and training use unbiased data and algorithms that do not reinforce human prejudice or discrimination. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Bias may be introduced to a system through the introduction (or exclusion) of certain data"</sub>

## 6.11. Human Rights

- **descriptive / limitation**: The report says that for commercial gain private sector entities could support profiling of individuals, which could lead to discrimination (e.g., based on race, gender, political or religious beliefs); full transparency and stringent monitoring and/or oversight from supervisory bodies and DPP authorities is needed over any tools implemented for data pooling and collaborative analytics, including tools using pseudonymisation, which have the potential of re-tracing to deduce personal data and identities. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Full transparency and a stringent monitoring and/or oversight is therefore needed from supervisory bodies and DPP authorities"</sub>

## 6.9. Security

- **descriptive / context**: The stocktake notes that collaborative analytics using pseudonymisation technologies can offer some protection, on condition that identifying information is held separately and covered by technical and organisational measures preventing personal data from being attributed to an identified or identifiable natural person. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Advancements in collaborative analytics using pseudonymisation technologies offer some protections, provided that identifying information is kept separately"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The stocktake notes that, despite progress in pseudonymisation technologies, data protection legislation continues to apply. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "despite the advancements in pseudonymisation technologies, data protection legislation still applies"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The stocktake says stringent oversight by data protection and privacy (DPP) authorities would also be necessary for initiatives involving collaborative analytics that use pseudonymisation technologies. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Stringent oversight by DPP authorities would also be necessary in initiatives involving collaborative analytics"</sub>

## 7. Enabling the wider use of data pooling and advanced analytics

- **descriptive / finding or result**: According to respondents, the most frequently cited solution to facilitate greater use of new technologies for data pooling and collaborative analytics is promoting regulatory certainty around their use; the report states the solutions summarised in the section are not presently endorsed by the FATF and offer a launching point for dialogue between the FATF, the private sector and DPP authorities and potential future FATF work. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "promote regulatory certainty around the use of these new technologies"</sub>

## 7.1. Regulatory Clarity

- **descriptive / recommendation**: Respondents noted increasing urgency to develop a data framework covering sharing or pooling of data by the private sector, including AML/CFT data, which could be achieved through enhanced collaboration between AML/CFT authorities (including regulators) and DPP authorities and could mitigate industry risk aversion arising from perceived lack of clarity. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "increasing urgency to develop a data framework encompassing all the areas surrounding sharing"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: A large proportion of respondents called for clear guidance from national financial regulators on the kinds of data that can be shared between FIs and on whether certain technologies (e.g., homomorphic encryption) and processes let organisations remain compliant with national and supranational privacy requirements as well as financial-sector-specific regulations, giving assurance to invest in technology, training, human resources and production deployments; some called for consolidation and publication of strong use cases (best practices). (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "clear guidance from national financial regulators as to the kinds of data that can be shared between FIs"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: Some respondents called for national legal safe-harbour provisions permitting voluntary data sharing between FIs for defined AML/CFT purposes based on principles of necessity and proportionality, and for changes to regulatory STR tipping-off requirements enabling FIs to more freely share whether an STR has been filed against a person, or the underlying STR data. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "national legal safe-harbour provisions, which permit voluntary data sharing between FIs for defined AML/CFT purposes"</sub>

## 7.2. Promotion of Enabling Environments

- **descriptive / recommendation**: A number of respondents called for AML/CFT supervisors to launch more pilot programs, regulatory sandboxes and innovation hubs so new technologies for data sharing and analysis can be tested without punitive or overly aggressive regulatory enforcement during implementation, so FIs would not face supervisory criticism even if a pilot proves unsuccessful. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "without punitive or overly aggressive regulatory enforcement as the FIs undertake implementation phases"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report says the success of such initiatives is also affected by the engagement of national DPP authorities, which could facilitate alignment, common learning and clarity on model governance, modelling techniques, and how data analytics can target particular ML/TF risk areas and facilitate data sharing while respecting DPP requirements. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The success of such initiatives is also impacted by the level of engagement and involvement of national DPP authorities"</sub>

## 7.3. Data Standardisation and Governance

- **descriptive / recommendation**: To enhance data quality, some respondents called for standardised formats for data collection and for open APIs enabling customers to share data between FIs; jurisdictions could then use their compliance powers as a feedback mechanism to inform FIs of data quality requirements and enforce measures to improve quality to acceptable levels. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "standardisation of formats for data collection, as well as to promote the use of open APIs"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: Respondents stressed that FIs need data governance policies, frameworks and controls ensuring the quality of data (completeness, how recently collected or updated, whether structured in machine-understandable form, whether the source affects interpretation or reliance, and means of assessing veracity) and that the origin of data is tracked, including keeping an audit trail of data. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the origin of data is tracked, including keeping an audit trail of data"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: Respondents referenced digital identities as a possible solution to data quality as a standard tool to support third party reliance on identification and increase data sharing, and said LEIs could be a documentation source for CDD of legal persons, ensuring a unique identifier is associated with each legal person instead of relying on name matching. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "LEIs could be used as a documentation source for the CDD of legal persons"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In interviews, respondents referenced the Common Reporting Standard (CRS) as a model: it calls on jurisdictions to obtain specific information from their FIs and automatically exchange it with other jurisdictions annually to counter tax evasion, and includes specific data standardisation requirements; they believe it could inform the data quality discussion for AML/CFT information sharing. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "automatically exchange that information with other jurisdictions on an annual basis to counter tax evasion"</sub>

## 7.4. Bias Prevention in Artificial Intelligence

- **descriptive / recommendation**: To prevent human bias and discrimination in AI, respondents stressed regularly reviewing the legitimacy and credibility of data sources, extensive model validation and continuous model monitoring; data sets that under- or over-represent certain groups may need additional training data to improve accuracy and fairness. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "regularly reviewing the legitimacy and credibility of data sources, extensive model validation, and continuous model monitoring"</sub>

## 7.2. Promotion of Enabling Environments

- **descriptive / context**: The FATF has identified Suggested Actions to Support the Use of Technology in AML/CFT (Annex C), which advance the 2017 San Jose Principle of pursuing positive and responsible innovation. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "advance the 2017 San Jose Principle to pursue positive and responsible innovation"</sub>
- **mandatory / expectation**: According to the Suggested Actions, new technologies for AML/CFT must be developed and implemented in a way that reflects threats as well as opportunities, ensuring their use is compatible with international standards of data protection and privacy, and cybersecurity. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "must be developed and implemented in a way that reflects threats as well as opportunities"</sub>

## 7.4. Bias Prevention in Artificial Intelligence

- **mandatory / requirement**: The OECD Principles on AI state that AI systems must function in a robust, secure and safe way throughout their life cycles. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI systems must also function in a robust, secure and safe way throughout their life cycles"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The OECD Principles on AI state that potential risks of AI systems should be continually assessed and managed. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "potential risks should be continually assessed and managed"</sub>

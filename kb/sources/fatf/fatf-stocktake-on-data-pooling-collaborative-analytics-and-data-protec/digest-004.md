## 5.1. Identified technologies for private-to-private information sharing

- **descriptive / limitation**: Table 5.1 says differential privacy may create a trade-off between precision of data and privacy, which might imply it would be best suited to analysing broad trends rather than detecting anomalies or detailed patterns. It is described as involving cryptographic protocols that permit each party to keep its own data anonymous while working with others on joint computations over their collective inputs. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may create a trade-off between precision of data and privacy"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Table 5.1 describes machine learning (supervised, unsupervised and reinforced learning) as a sub-field of AI where computers learn via learning algorithms when exposed to new data, instead of being explicitly programmed for tasks. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "computers are able to learn (via learning algorithms) when exposed to new data"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Table 5.1 gives examples of machine learning uses: supervised learning to build compliance risk scoring models from historical examination/audit results; optimising decision points in business processes by understanding current states and predicting optimal decisions; and scoring or classification models to identify suspicious networks or entities from financial transactions or other data. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A scoring model or a classification mode can be used for identifying suspicious networks or entities"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Table 5.1 gives the example of a travelling algorithm that can access and interrogate data sets in different FIs without moving the data, to learn new types of criminal trends and techniques it could not learn if it resided in only one institution, leading to more dynamic risk assessment tools such as dynamic red flag indicators. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a travelling algorithm can access and interrogate data sets in different FIs without moving the data"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Table 5.1 describes robotic process automation as software automation technology in which software "robots" are programmed on human behaviour to mimic such interactions and carry out numerous repetitive tasks at high volume, speed and accuracy. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "at high volume and with speed and accuracy"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Table 5.1 describes network analytics as the use of network data to detect potentially obscured trends and patterns in large pools of data, allowing visualisation of intricate networks of entities and attributes of the linkages; it can identify networks of related entities based on known subjects of interest and derive patterns not otherwise seen at end-point level. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "use of network data to detect potentially obscured trends and patterns in large pools of data"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Table 5.1 (citing Shiffman, 2020) describes federated learning as a machine learning technique that trains an algorithm over several decentralised databases holding local data; the algorithm picks up new information, such as trends, in each separate database without the data being exchanged or moved. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "trains an algorithm across multiple decentralised databases that contain local data"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Table 5.1 describes deep learning as a branch of machine learning that applies several layers of learning algorithms to draw meaning out of large volumes of data. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "uses multiple layers of learning algorithms to extract meaning from large quantities of data"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Table 5.1 (citing SAS, 2020) describes natural language processing (NLP) as helping computers communicate with humans in their own language; it makes it possible for computers to read text, hear speech, interpret it, gauge sentiment and decide which parts matter. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "makes it possible for computers to read text, hear speech, interpret it, measure sentiment and determine which parts are important"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Table 5.1 also notes that text mining allows STRs or any other documents to be annotated automatically so that they can be retrieved more easily later. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Using text mining, STRs or any documents, can be annotated automatically to facilitate better retrieval later on."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Table 5.1 describes trusted execution environments (confidential computing) as protecting data-in-use by isolating computations in a hardware-based trusted execution environment, protected by securing a portion of the hardware's processor and memory; an example is two parties agreeing to share data such as transaction data and analyse it in such an environment. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the protection of data-in-use through isolating computations to a hardware-based trusted execution environment"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Table 5.1 says advances in cloud technology let firms collect, store and analyse very large structured and unstructured data sets at very low cost and can facilitate collaboration among those with access to a secure cloud environment; however, even if two FIs hold data in the same cloud environment, the legal barriers to data sharing remain the same. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "regardless whether two FIs have their data in the same cloud environment, the legal barriers for data sharing remain"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Table 5.1 describes distributed ledger technology as an encrypted common ledger of transactions maintained by parties in a network with no single central authority, which can share data between several parties without one party having full power of data disposal; it states that the legal barriers for data sharing remain. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "without one party having the full power of data disposal"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Table 5.1 describes an application programming interface (API) as an interface allowing regulated institutions to submit data, facilitating communication between regulated institutions and authorities by integrating the data production process, allowing greater automation and lower reporting costs. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An API is an interface that allows regulated institutions to submit data"</sub>

## Box 5.1. Federated Learning

- **descriptive / case example**: A team of a hardware technology provider and software vendors is working to launch a secure federated learning platform where machine learning models are trained across multiple data sets to detect and analyse "normal" and "abnormal" patterns; the model moves across databases in different locations and the data never moves, letting it learn new criminal trends and techniques while preserving privacy, and the learned knowledge can continuously refine and tune risk indicators across participating institutions. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the model moves across the databases in different locations and the data never moves"</sub>

## Box 5.2. Secure Multiparty Computation

- **descriptive / case example**: A Regtech has developed technology enabling two or more FIs to collaboratively compute a risk assessment function by processing encrypted customer KYC data and behavioural transaction and login activity information without exposing it; participating institutions only exchange completely random strings containing no customer data, and neither party discloses its data to the other at any time. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Participating institutions only exchange completely random strings containing no customer data"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In the Box 5.2 SMPC solution, the technology can be executed before the transaction is sent or in the AML monitoring and analytics phases, and leaves a cryptographic audit trail at each FI; an external auditor given the audit trails from all transacting customers can reconstruct the whole decision process. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An external auditor provided with the audit trails from all the transacting customers, can reconstruct the whole decision process"</sub>

## Box 5.3. Homomorphic Encryption

- **descriptive / case example**: A Regtech built a flexible trust framework for FIs enabling secure and private KYC and CDD processes using homomorphic encryption, which allows data to be processed while remaining encrypted; FIs can search, share and collaborate with third-party data assets without revealing the contents of the search or compromising security or ownership of the underlying data, and participants never move or consolidate data assets. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "enables FIs to securely search, share, and collaborate with third-party data assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In the homomorphic encryption model, KYC information is validated between multiple trusted participants or jurisdictions via encrypted search without moving or pooling data; analysts were able to securely and privately cross-match and search regulated data across privacy jurisdictions in a business-relevant timeframe while sensitive assets remained protected during processing. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "cross- match and search regulated data across privacy jurisdictions in a business-relevant timeframe"</sub>

## 6. Challenges related to the use of new technologies for data collaborative analytics

- **descriptive / context**: The report says pooling and collaboration of data amongst FIs, particularly across national borders and with third parties, raises policy concerns, and that additional considerations arise beyond the 2017 FATF Guidance on Private Sector Information Sharing when processing larger data sets using privacy-enhancing technologies and advanced analytics such as AI. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "additional considerations arise when attempting to process larger sets of data while using privacy-enhancing technologies"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In the Questionnaire, data protection and privacy requirements were identified as the primary policy consideration for new technologies in private-private data sharing; other highly cited challenges were regulatory challenges (including explainability/interpretability and a lack of regulatory incentives) and data quality (including lack of data standardisation). Figure 6 also lists cost, technology (upgrading legacy systems), STR tipping off requirements, complexity, lack of a regulatory sandbox, data localisation requirements, commercial interests, risk aversion, de-risking concerns and cyber crime concerns (each respondent selecting up to four). (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "DPP requirements were identified as the primary policy consideration when developing and deploying new technologies"</sub>

## 6.1. Ensuring and Enhancing Data Protection and Privacy

- **mandatory / requirement**: The report states that under international law one of the most crucial requirements is the existence of a valid legal basis for the processing of personal data, and that proportionality in terms of alternative measures to achieve the same goal must be respected. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "one of the most crucial requirements is ensuring the existence of a valid legal basis"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 14 notes that the right to privacy is a universal human right under the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, and at regional level under the European Convention on Human Rights. The report also notes that Convention 108 (CETs No. 108), opened for signature on 28 January 1981, was the first legally binding international instrument in the data protection field. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "first legally binding international instrument in the data protection field"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report says financial data can be among the most sensitive information about individuals, exposing their financial standing, family interactions, behaviours and habits, health and more. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial data may include some of the most sensitive data about individuals"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Because of that sensitivity, the report states that regard must be given to both AML/CFT and DPP and the two weighed in a balanced way, in line with Member States' obligations under international law, including human rights law. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "regard must be given to both AML/CFT and DPP and weighed in a balanced fashion"</sub>

## Box 6.1. Convention for the Protection of Individuals with regard to Automatic Processing of Personal Data (Convention 108+)

- **permissive / context**: Under Convention 108+, processing of personal data could be based on (1) free, specific, informed, unambiguous consent from the data subject, or (2) another legitimate basis laid down by law (e.g. performance of a contract, public interest, ensuring public security, legitimate interest of the controller); the underlying rationale for requiring consent, public interest or legitimate interest should be carefully analysed and articulated by international stakeholders from the AML/CFT, DPP and human rights fields. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "free, specific, informed, unambiguous consent from the data subject"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Under Article 11 of Convention 108+, every category of data shared must be clearly defined, as well as the purpose of its processing, which is necessary to define how long data can be retained and whether interference with the right to respect for private life is proportionate and justified. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "every category of data shared must be clearly defined, as well as the purpose of its processing"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Under Article 6 of Convention 108+, processing of sensitive data for legitimate purposes must have additional appropriate safeguards to prevent adverse effects for the data subject, for instance the subject's explicit consent, a clear legal provision covering the case, a professional secrecy obligation, and a particular technical security measure (such as data encryption). (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "processing of sensitive data for legitimate purposes must have additional appropriate safeguards"</sub>

## 6.1. Ensuring and Enhancing Data Protection and Privacy

- **descriptive / context**: The report says ML and TF activities often involve multiple institutions and jurisdictions, so FIs benefit from sending and receiving information and analysis on customers including across borders and may want to process larger data sets to refine understanding of emerging criminal trends and typologies; equally, FIs have a legal obligation to protect their customers' personal data. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs also have a legal obligation to protect their customer’s personal data"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The report says divergent national and international DPP laws across jurisdictions may present challenges for FIs implementing AML/CFT obligations or developing information sharing initiatives, compounded where there is insufficient regulatory guidance or misaligned approaches between AML/CFT requirements and DPP obligations; the complexity of different DPP approaches influences the availability, access, processing and sharing of information by the private sector. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may present challenges for FIs when implementing AML/CFT obligations or when developing private sector information sharing initiatives"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The report identifies as a significant challenge the perceived conflict between FIs' wish to share information to make AML/CFT compliance more efficient and effective and existing legal restrictions that protect customer privacy. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "perceived conflict between FIs’ desire to share information to improve the efficiency and efficacy of AML/CFT compliance"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: In many jurisdictions, data privacy requirements restrict AML/CFT information sharing between FIs outside the financial group context. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In many jurisdictions, such sharing outside of the financial group context is restricted due to data privacy requirements."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: By contrast, in one jurisdiction, the United States, exemptions or safe harbours let FIs that are not part of the same financial group share information for AML/CFT purposes, as set out in the report's following box. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "“safe harbours” exist that allow AML/CFT information sharing between FIs that are not part of the same financial group"</sub>

## Box 6.2. 314(b) USA PATRIOT Act

- **descriptive / context**: Per FinCEN (2020), to rely on the Section 314(b) safe harbour it is sufficient that the institution or association has a reasonable basis to believe the information shared relates to activities that may involve money laundering or terrorist activity and shares it for an appropriate purpose under Section 314(b) and its implementing regulations; it can share even if it cannot identify specific proceeds of a specified unlawful activity being laundered. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "has a reasonable basis to believe that the information shared relates to activities that may involve"</sub>

## 6.1. Ensuring and Enhancing Data Protection and Privacy

- **descriptive / limitation**: The report says that, although there is a perception global data protection standards may be needed for digital cooperation, no organisation is currently mandated to coordinate their development; standards are developed nationally and supra-nationally, leaving a paucity of guidance on the kinds of data that can be shared between FIs (even with collaborative analytics) and on whether new technologies let organisations remain compliant with privacy requirements. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "no organisation is currently mandated to coordinate their development"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: According to the United Nations Conference on Trade and Development, 132 out of 194 countries have enacted some form of legislation to secure data and privacy protection, though the level of safeguards and compliance varies considerably among States; the entry into force of the EU GDPR harmonised DPP rules within the EU and European Economic Area and acted as a catalyst for many countries to consider modernising privacy rules. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "132 out of 194 countries have enacted some form of legislation to secure the protection of data and privacy"</sub>

## Box 6.2. 314(b) USA PATRIOT Act

- **permissive / context**: Section 314(b) of the USA PATRIOT Act lets two or more FIs, and any association of FIs, voluntarily share with one another information about individuals, entities, organisations and countries suspected of possible terrorist or ML activities. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may voluntarily share information with one another regarding individuals, entities, organisations, and countries suspected of possible terrorist or ML activities"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Under Section 314(b), an FI or association that transmits, receives or shares such information to identify and report activities that may involve terrorist acts or ML is shielded from liability to any person under US law or regulation, State law, or any contract or other legally enforceable agreement (including arbitration agreements), both for the disclosure and for failing to notify the person it concerns or anyone else named in it. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "shall not be liable to any person under any law or regulation of the US"</sub>

## 6.1. Ensuring and Enhancing Data Protection and Privacy

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Questionnaire results show respondents largely believe new technologies can address earlier concerns about private sector data sharing while respecting fundamental and individual DPP rights. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "respondents largely believe that new technologies can address previous concerns related to private sector data sharing"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A number of respondents specifically asked for national and international rules clarifying when, for what purpose and which type of data may be shared or pooled between FIs for AML/CFT purposes, particularly outside financial groups. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "called for national and international rules to clarify when and for what purpose data"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Respondents noted that new and emerging technologies cannot be called privacy-preserving when the goal is to use the data to identify a specific natural or legal person, for example when on-boarding a customer. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "are not “privacy-preserving” when the aim is to use the data to identify a specific natural or legal person"</sub>

## Box 4.2. Japan’s Proof of Concept on Machine Learning and Artificial Intelligence

- **descriptive / case example**: Japan developed a proof of concept (POC) project, with participation from several Japanese FIs, supported by the Japan Financial Services Agency and sponsored by the New Energy and Industrial Technology Development Organisation, that integrates AI algorithms with instructions from each FI's transaction dataset, without sharing or pooling the data, resulting in a single AI model. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "integrates AI algorithms with instructions from each FI transaction dataset, without sharing or pooling the data"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The Japanese POC aims to build an AI model that facilitates human judgement by calculating the likelihood of a true positive score for transaction monitoring and sanctions screening. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "calculating the likelihood of a true positive score for transaction monitoring and sanctions screening"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: In the Japanese POC the transaction data of individual FIs were not shared or pooled; instead two approaches were used: (1) integrating the AI models themselves that had learned each institution's dataset, and (2) tuning an AI model that had learned one institution's dataset to re-learn another bank's data, continuing this process to improve accuracy. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "integrating the AI models themselves that learned each institutions’ dataset"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: According to the Japanese POC results, the Shared Transaction Monitoring and Screening System with AI has enough potential to reduce workload, including triage of detections and dealing with false positives; in terms of accuracy and interpretability, results indicate some human operations in conventional triage can be replaced by AI-powered judgement. The report says that if the initiative spreads to a wider range of financial industry participants it could improve the efficiency and effectiveness of AML/CFT as a whole. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "some human operations at the conventional triage process can be replaced by AI-powered judgement"</sub>

## Box 4.3. China’s Information-Sharing Platform Trial

- **descriptive / case example**: Under the guidance and supervision of the People's Bank of China AML bureau, several Chinese FIs are trialling an AML risk information-sharing platform integrating blockchain, digital identity and trusted privacy-enhancing technologies. Participating FIs encrypt high-risk customer information, including digital identity number (DID) and institution-identified risk labels, and upload it to the blockchain. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "trial operation of an AML risk information sharing platform"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: In the Chinese trial, when a participating institution inquires about a customer, a match is made on the blockchain through a secure computing platform; DID is only matched when the searched individual's name and national ID number have been uploaded by other participating FIs. After matching, the platform extracts the ML risk information and returns it in cipher text for decryption, and the processing unit immediately deletes records of the computations; the inquiring FI is alerted that the individual is high-risk at another FI, with no actual exchange of customer data amongst institutions. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "DID will only be matched when and if that searched individual’s name and national ID number have been uploaded"</sub>

## Box 4.4. Transaction Monitoring Netherlands (TMNL)

- **descriptive / case example**: TMNL is a joint initiative of five Dutch banks to collectively monitor their payment transactions for signals that could indicate ML or TF; at the time of writing the TMNL utility was being built. Its primary goal is to improve detection of money laundering by identifying unusual transaction patterns that individual banks cannot identify alone, focusing on multi-bank alerts. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "joint initiative of five Dutch banks to collectively monitor their payment transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Participating banks will continue to monitor their own transactions in accordance with their existing obligations under Dutch AML legislation. The data pooled will relate only to transactions on bank accounts administered in the Netherlands, other banks are foreseen to be able to join in the longer term, and the project is currently focusing on transaction information related to corporate clients. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "participating banks will continue to monitor their own transactions in accordance with their existing obligations under Dutch AML legislation"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Once TMNL is in operation, if it flags a potential unusual transaction or series of transfers indicative of presumed ML or other illegal activities, all participants in the payment chain will receive an alert. The receiving banks will independently review the alerts and individually decide whether to file an unusual transaction report to the Dutch FIU; that decision will not be shared in the platform. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The receiving banks will independently review the alerts from TMNL and individually decide whether to file an unusual transaction report"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The TMNL platform will be cloud-based and built on a copy of an accelerator platform of one participating bank, with a componentised design principle to allow use of state-of-the-art tools going forward; privacy sensitive information in the transaction data exchanged between the banks and TMNL will be pseudonymised. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The privacy sensitive information of the transaction data to be exchanged between the banks and TMNL will be pseudonymised"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The Dutch participating banks worked with government partners including the Data Protection Authority, the Ministries of Finance and Justice and Security, the Fiscal Information and Investigation Service and the FIU. TMNL aligns with the Dutch Government's 2019 ML Action Plan, under which an amendment of the AML/ATF Act is foreseen to enable full-scale collective transaction monitoring. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an amendment of the AML/ATF Act is foreseen to enable full-scale collective transaction monitoring"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The foreseen Dutch amendment seeks to enable banks to share more transaction data and information on presumed unusual transactions, lift the ban on outsourcing of their transaction-monitoring processes, and allow use of the Civil Service Number (the unique private individual identification number) in collective transaction monitoring. A footnote says that at the time of writing the legislation permitting TMNL's activities was still being developed and had not been brought before parliament. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to lift the ban on outsourcing of their transaction-monitoring processes"</sub>

## Box 4.5. Enabling Regulatory Environments

- **descriptive / case example**: In the United Kingdom, the FCA held a TechSprint in 2019 on how privacy enhancing technologies can facilitate sharing information about ML and financial crime concerns while remaining compliant with data security laws, with AML supervisors and the ICO attending. The FCA also ran a Digital Sandbox Pilot, concluded in February 2021, providing synthetic banking transaction data sets to firms developing solutions against fraud and scams, several of which employ PET technologies. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "access to synthetic banking transaction data sets, to innovative firms looking to develop new solutions"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In France, the ACPR created a FinTech innovation hub that acts as an innovation observatory and carries the "suptech" mission of integrating new technologies in supervisory tasks; it led four AML-CFT working groups in the past two years with industry and public authorities (FIU, security and data protection authorities) on issues such as distant KYC, AML-CFT processes in the crypto-asset sectors, and bank relationships with crypto-asset service providers. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The ACPR FinTech innovation hub has led four working groups in the past two years related to AML-CFT"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In March 2020 the ACPR organised an event on data sharing and pooling highlighting techniques that share knowledge without necessarily sharing data: differential privacy guarantees can be used to train predictive models (e.g., those embedded in transaction monitoring systems) on data too sensitive to be disclosed to any given FI, and secure multi-party computation can build secured collaborative processes such as producing KPIs on fraudulent IBANs from transactions and user data pooled from multiple FIs. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "privacy guarantees provided by differential privacy can be used to train predictive models"</sub>

## 4.4. Drivers and preconditions for the use of new technologies

- **descriptive / context**: Respondents stressed the need for thorough data protection impact assessments when developing new technologies for data pooling and collaborative analytics, and in some countries such an assessment is a regulatory requirement before data processing, to minimise the identified risks to individuals' rights. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "need to conduct thorough data protection impact assessments when developing new technologies for data pooling"</sub>

## 5.1. Identified technologies for private-to-private information sharing

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The most cited technology used for data pooling and collaboration was cryptography, and for analysis of large data sets was machine learning. Respondents often noted that data sharing and analysis initiatives require multiple types of technologies, often applied together, to secure and analyse large data sets and ensure alignment with national and international DPP requirements. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The most cited technology used for data pooling and collaboration was cryptography"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Table 5.1 describes zero-knowledge proofs as a cryptographic and verification method between a prover and a verifier in which the prover proves it has information without disclosing the underlying data; the stated benefit is that Bank A could establish whether Bank B held data on an individual without sharing that individual's identity. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The prover is able to prove to the verifier that they have information without disclosing the underlying data"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Table 5.1 describes secure multiparty computation (SMPC) as enabling several parties to evaluate a function on private data from distinct sources without aggregating or sharing the data, with the parties learning nothing more than the value of the function at the end; it may be applied to disparate data sources to extract credible suspicions from different parties while keeping the data sovereign. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "SMPC enables several parties to evaluate a function on private data coming from distinct data sources"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: In Table 5.1, the entry attributed to Microsoft (2016) next to homomorphic encryption says it lets organisations cross-match and search third-party data assets without revealing what is being searched for or putting the security or ownership of the underlying data at risk, so that different parties can work together on sensitive data while preserving privacy, confidentiality and regulatory compliance. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "cross-match and search third-party data assets without identifying the contents of the search"</sub>

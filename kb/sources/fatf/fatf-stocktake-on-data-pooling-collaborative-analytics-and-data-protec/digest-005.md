## Box 6.3. EU’s Data Protection Rules

- **descriptive / context**: The rights to privacy and to data protection are enshrined in the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights (Articles 7 and 8). The EU GDPR, effective 25 May 2018, regulates how companies process personal data of individuals. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The EU GDPR, which entered into effect on 25 May 2018"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The GDPR requires companies to collect and process only personal data that is necessary (data minimisation) to fulfil a specific and defined purpose (purpose limitation) and not to further process it in a manner incompatible with those purposes; it also requires that individuals be informed, among other things, of when their data is collected and the purposes of processing. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "requires companies to only collect and process personal data that is necessary"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The GDPR sets a limitative list of legal bases for processing personal data and establishes individual rights including access, rectification and erasure, and the right not to be subject to decisions solely based on automated processing, including profiling. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It sets a limitative list of legal basis allowing to process personal data"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The GDPR is supervised and enforced by the data protection authorities in each EU Member State; the EDPB, made up of representatives from each DPA and the European Data Protection Supervisor, ensures consistent application across the EU. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "ensures that GDPR is applied consistently throughout the EU"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Transfers of personal data to third countries or international organisations are subject to specific conditions so that GDPR guarantees are not undermined; transfers can in particular be based on European Commission "adequacy decisions" when a third country's level of data protection is essentially equivalent to the EU's. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "adequacy decisions” adopted by the European Commission when the level of data protection in a third country is essentially equivalent"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The GDPR notes that data protection principles do not apply to anonymised data (personal data rendered anonymous so the data subject is not or no longer identifiable), which falls outside its scope; pseudonymised data (personal data that can no longer be attributed to a specific data subject without additional information) is considered personal data and falls within the GDPR's scope. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Pseudonymised data (i.e., personal data that can no longer be attributed to a specific data subject"</sub>

## 6.1. Ensuring and Enhancing Data Protection and Privacy

- **descriptive / context**: The report says that while DPP laws differ between jurisdictions there is a trend towards convergence, including adoption of frameworks sharing key features: an overarching law rather than sectoral rules, a core set of principles and obligations, enforceable individual rights to control data (e.g., rectification and erasure), and an independent supervisory authority with enforcement powers. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is a trend towards convergence"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report describes Convention 108+ as the only multilateral legally binding instrument on data protection, and notes that the OECD Privacy Guidelines and other international standards point to a positive trend; the United Nations is developing recommended legislative provisions and a compendium of good practices on data protection to facilitate international cooperation in counter-terrorism (the "UN CT Programme on Data Protection"). (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the only multilateral legally binding instrument on data protection"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The report says that even where a valid legal framework authorises private sector data transfers, data shared by an FI may be inaccurate or incomplete; FIs using pooled data remain responsible for ensuring its accuracy and should verify the quality of shared data by assessing whether the elements collected and checks carried out by other FIs are up-to-date, suitable and sufficient for their AML obligations. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FIs using this data remain responsible for ensuring its accuracy"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: In some jurisdictions FIs as data controllers are required to give individuals access on request to the information they collect, transmit and retain, so individuals can request correction or deletion of inaccurate and unnecessary data; tension may arise if individuals suspected of illegal activity or under investigation request deletion of incriminating information, though legislation may provide duly justified restrictions where exercising individual rights may affect compliance with a legal obligation or jeopardise an ongoing investigation. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Tension may arise if individuals suspected of illegal activities or under formal investigation request the deletion of incriminating information"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Some Questionnaire respondents noted ambiguity about the ability to share anonymised data, because of a perception that requirements for anonymisation and pseudonymisation lack clarity or may vary across jurisdictions. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is ambiguity in relation to the ability of organisations to share anonymised data"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 16 describes anonymous data as information not related to an identifiable natural person, or information where the data subject is no longer identifiable and there is no possibility of re-identification. Footnote 17 describes pseudonymisation as a security measure that replaces or removes identifying information in a data set, yet the data is still personal data because the data subject could be re-identified (e.g., if someone holds an encryption key). (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Pseudonymisation is a security measure that replaces or removes information in a data set that identifies an individual"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Referring to the 2017 FATF Guidance on Private Sector Information Sharing, the stocktake notes that it can be challenging or impossible for FIs to depend on consents or public interest exemptions as the basis for processing customer data to combat financial crime, including when passing it to other parties. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it can be challenging or impossible for FIs to rely upon consents or public interest exemptions"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The stocktake states that, for international transfers of customer data to combat financial crime, the conditions for legality of such transfers must be met. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In addition, the conditions for legality for international transfers must be met."</sub>
- **permissive / context**: The stocktake says that legal provisions setting out suitable safeguards, or guidance specifying when (where possible) customer data may move across jurisdictions for these purposes, can make information sharing easier. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Express legal provisions providing for appropriate safeguards or guidance defining the circumstances"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Rules on international transfers may affect private-to-private data sharing outside financial groups; in many cases public authorities or private entities cannot transfer data out of their jurisdiction without equivalent protections in the target jurisdiction or appropriate safeguards. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "rules on international transfers may affect private-to-private data sharing (outside of financial groups)"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Data localisation laws may typically involve two main requirements: (1) personal data about citizens is hosted in data centres located in a country or group of countries, and (2) data is manipulated and processed inside the same country. As a result FIs may be prohibited from sharing specific data on suspicions of financial crime risk with counterpart FIs across borders, including within the same financial group, or may not be shielded from liability for doing so as in a domestic context. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "that personal data about citizens are hosted in data centres located in a country or group of countries"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Respondents said an impediment to wider deployment of new technologies for data pooling and collaborative analytics is the lack of interaction between national and international AML/CFT and DPP authorities; the report says such lack of coordination might implicate the FATF Recommendation 2 requirement for cooperation and, where appropriate, coordination to ensure compatibility of AML/CFT requirements with DPP rules. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "might implicate FATF’s Recommendation 2 requirement for cooperation"</sub>

## 6.2. Data Quality

- **descriptive / limitation**: The report says data standards and formats vary significantly across institutions, jurisdictions, infrastructures and message networks, which can impede data analytics, delay banking processes and increase compliance cost; low quality data (inaccurate or out-of-date) could nullify the benefits of data pooling and collaborative analytics by causing erroneous outcomes, and automated and advanced analytical tools in particular depend on standardised inputs. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Automated and advanced analytical tools, in particular, depend on standardised inputs"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: According to the Questionnaire, data quality is a major challenge for deploying advanced analytics on centralised or decentralised data sets; respondents noted poor data quality at some FIs and variation and incompatibility of data standards, which is a major obstacle to producing consistently formatted data sets needed to prevent biased conclusions that could, in a worst case, lead to financial exclusion. The challenges are amplified when an encryption layer is added, because errors become harder to identify, which can lead to errors in output. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "makes it more difficult to identify errors in the data, which can in turn lead to errors in output"</sub>

## 6.3. Lack of Regulatory Clarity

- **descriptive / finding or result**: A significant number of respondents noted that the lack of explicit regulatory requirements and guidance on new technology is a challenge for private sector data pooling and collaborative analytics; some jurisdictions are seeking to clarify or adapt their rules and a few have financial intelligence sharing partnerships in place. Some respondents said that without regulator guidance and certainty there is less incentive to prioritise investment in expensive new technologies for collaborative analytics. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the lack of explicit regulatory requirements and guidance for the use of new technology is a challenge"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: One respondent noted that current legal frameworks were not created with new privacy enhancing technologies in mind and so do not clearly describe the boundaries for applying such techniques; a number of respondents noted that existing national regulations bar private-to-private data sharing completely, except within financial groups. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "existing national regulations bar private-to-private data sharing completely"</sub>

## 6.4. Explainability and Interpretability

- **descriptive / finding or result**: In January 2020 the European Banking Authority (EBA) released a paper on big data and advanced analytics in the European banking system, noting European FIs appear to be at an early stage of using advanced analytics, using simple machine learning models that prioritise explainability and interpretability; a challenge with more complex models is their explainability and interpretability to regulators, plus potential for bias and unintended results. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "European FIs appear to be at an early stage of using advanced analytics"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The report says that without such understanding the technology can be perceived as a 'black box' by regulators, which could affect deployment because regulators cannot conduct adequate inspections and risk assessments or manage identified risks; this is especially so when a decision is highly automated and directly affects customers. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the technology can be perceived as a ‘black box’ by regulators"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: The report says regulators could work with public and private sector technologists and other stakeholders to evaluate and help drive adoption of appropriate practices to explain, document and govern advanced analytics in AML/CFT; this work could include whether and how explainability requirements could be applied under a risk-based approach, e.g., more stringent explainability requirements as the model's potential impact on business continuity and/or potential harm to customers increases. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "imposing more stringent explainability requirements as the potential impact of the model on business continuity"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Some respondents noted FIs have been deterred from implementing new AML/CFT technologies for data sharing and analysis because regulators expect cryptography and machine learning models that can be clearly understood; this is particularly challenging where a third-party vendor has proprietary rights over the technical specifications or where FIs lack staff with adequate technical literacy in advanced cryptography and analytics. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs have been deterred by implementing new AML/CFT technologies for data sharing and analysis"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The stocktake reports the EBA view that a model counts as explainable if humans can directly grasp how it behaves internally (interpretability), or if justifications can be given for the main factors behind its output. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a model is explainable when its internal behaviour can be directly understood by humans"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: One respondent noted that in regulators' view algorithmic models should be designed so results can be reproduced given the same input data, which is not always possible with machine learning. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "results to be reproduced given the same input data, which is not always possible with machine learning"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: Respondents raised the need for manual (human) intervention to review outcomes of advanced analytics (e.g., AI and machine learning) to ensure accuracy and continuously refine the models, and noted as good practice hybrid approaches in which humans review more complex outcomes while simple models complete basic routine tasks. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Hybrid approaches – where humans review more complex analytical outcomes"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Respondents voiced concern that some advanced analytics may be used by an FI without in-depth understanding of the technology's functioning and objective, risking unverified and unreliable outputs; the report says AML/CFT supervisors should also understand, or have access to a team who can understand, advanced analytical models to test how FIs designed and validated them. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AML/CFT supervisors should also understand or have access to a team who can understand the advanced analytical models"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Respondents noted it is important that regulated entities can confirm, to their supervisor and to themselves, that any new technology deployed produces better outcomes than the previous system, especially where it is unclear whether it improves AML/CFT effectiveness. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "producing better outcomes than the previous system"</sub>

## 6.5. STR Confidentiality and Tipping Off

- **descriptive / limitation**: The report states STR confidentiality rules can impede sharing of STRs (or the fact an STR was filed or its underlying information); confidentiality is essential so the subject and third parties are not tipped off, which can adversely affect intelligence gathering and investigation and enable persons to abscond with or dispose of assets. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "STR confidentiality rules can impede the ability to share STRs"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report says STR confidentiality also protects the reputation of the STR subject and the safety and security of the person filing the report, breaches have the potential to undermine the entire STR regime, and unauthorised disclosure of STRs could result in an FI facing criminal liability in many jurisdictions, which places necessary limits on STR sharing (FATF, 2017). (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Unauthorised disclosure of STRs could also result in a FI facing criminal liability in many jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: STR confidentiality is more complex when sharing crosses borders because national laws differ, for example on discoverability and production of records in judicial proceedings; some countries require regulator notification of judicial requests and subpoenas about domestic STRs so regulators can intervene, but these rules may not protect foreign STRs submitted to a foreign FIU. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these regulations may not protect foreign STRs submitted to a foreign FIU"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: The report says that although STR confidentiality can challenge private-to-private information sharing, preventing tipping-off is important, and alternative legal or technological mechanisms guaranteeing anonymisation or encryption of personal data may be able to provide safeguards without a corresponding cost to AML/CFT effectiveness. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Alternative legal or technological mechanisms that guarantee the anonymisation or encryption of personal data may be able to provide safeguards"</sub>

## 3. Background

- **mandatory / requirement**: FATF Recommendation 18 requires information sharing inside financial groups, both for customer due diligence (CDD) and for managing ML/TF risk. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Recommendation 18 requires information sharing within the context of financial groups for customer due diligence (CDD) purposes"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report says the group-level sharing under Recommendation 18 covers information and analysis on transactions or activities that look unusual (where such analysis was carried out), and may extend to an STR, the information underlying it, or the fact that an STR was submitted. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Such sharing includes information and analysis of transactions or activities which appear unusual (if such analysis was done)"</sub>
- **permissive / requirement**: FATF Recommendation 21 ensures that FIs and their directors, officers and employees are able to disclose that an STR or related information has been submitted, provided this is under the group-wide ML/TF risk management requirements set out in Recommendation 18. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Recommendation 21 further ensures that FIs and their directors, officers and employees are able to disclose"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report says some FATF Recommendations already include elements related to private-to-private information sharing, so data pooling and collaborative analytics is not an entirely new topic for the FATF. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Some of the FATF’s Recommendations include elements related to private-to- private information sharing"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Recommendation 18 requirement applies to all entities (domestic and cross-border) captured by the FATF Glossary definition of financial group. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This requirement applies to all the entities (in domestic and cross border environments)"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: FATF Recommendation 2 requires authorities to cooperate and coordinate to ensure the compatibility of AML/CFT requirements with data protection and privacy (DPP) and other similar provisions; the report says this highlights the important role authorities play in addressing actual or perceived impediments to information sharing. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "requiring authorities to cooperate and coordinate to ensure the compatibility of AML/CFT requirements with DPP"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The report states that the FATF Standards do not presently include requirements for information sharing outside of the financial group similar to those in the financial group context. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FATF Standards do not presently include similar requirements for information sharing outside of the financial group"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In 2017 the FATF published its Guidance on Private Sector Information Sharing, which highlights initiatives in information sharing amongst FIs that go beyond the FATF Recommendations (pages 22-25); since then a number of regional and national initiatives have emerged. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In 2017, the FATF published its Guidance on Private Sector Information Sharing"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In December 2020 the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) adopted a statement that the upcoming update to the EU AML legislation is an opportunity to address the interplay between protection of privacy and personal data and AML/CFT measures; the EDPB is convinced that closer articulation between the two sets of rules would benefit both personal data protection and efficiency of the AML framework. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is an opportunity to address the interplay between the protection of privacy and personal data and AML/CFT measures"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: In a footnote the FATF Glossary defines a financial group as a group made up of a parent company, or another kind of legal person, that exercises control and coordinating functions over the rest of the group for group supervision under the Core Principles, together with branches and/or subsidiaries that are subject to AML/CFT policies and procedures at group level. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "control and coordinating functions over the rest of the group for the application of group supervision under the Core Principles"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report cites non-binding recital 46 of the EU's fifth Anti-Money Laundering Directive, which observes that criminals pass illicit proceeds through many financial intermediaries to avoid detection and says it is therefore important to let credit and financial institutions exchange information with each other and not only within a group, with due regard to national data protection rules. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is important to allow credit and financial institutions to exchange information not only between group members"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The EDPB reiterated the need for a clear legal basis for the processing of personal data, stating the purposes and limits of such processing in line with Article 5(1) of the EU GDPR, in particular regarding information sharing and international transfers of data. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the need for a clear legal basis for the processing of personal data and stating the purposes"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report says that with privacy enhancing technologies, the private sector has launched a number of initiatives and pilot programs to pool and collaboratively analyse data, including CDD data, to enhance AML/CFT compliance; it says these initiatives show FIs' appetite to collaborate and the need for the FATF to go further than existing guidance on data pooling and collaborative analytics in the context of new technology. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the need for the FATF to go further than the existing guidance"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 9 cites a definition of privacy enhancing technologies (PETs, or privacy-enhancing cryptography) as specialist cryptographical capabilities that allow computations on underlying data without the data owner necessarily divulging that data, and that can keep a search query and its results encrypted or undisclosed so they are visible only to the requester. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Specialist cryptographical capabilities, which allow computations to take place on underlying data"</sub>

## 4. Objectives and Preconditions for Private Sector AML/CFT Information Sharing and Analysis

- **descriptive / context**: The stocktake builds on earlier FATF work on case-by-case information sharing within and between FIs and financial groups (e.g., to review a customer that triggered a red-flag indicator) by considering how technological innovations relying on large-scale private-to-private data pooling and collaborative analytics can facilitate AML/CFT/CPF objectives while respecting DPP requirements. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "large-scale private-to-private data pooling and collaborative analytics can facilitate AML/CFT/CPF objectives"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: The report says emerging technologies tested in other domains that encrypt sensitive personal data, such as in the health sector, may offer innovative solutions to respect diverging national and international DPP laws while allowing exchange and analysis of information for AML/CFT purposes. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may offer innovative solutions to respect diverging national and international DPP laws"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: According to the Questionnaire results, 93% of respondents believe that new technologies may help overcome data sharing challenges in AML/CFT (for example, protection of proprietary information for competitive purposes). (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "93% of respondents believe that new technologies may help overcome these and other data sharing challenges"</sub>

## 4.1. Why share data?

- **descriptive / context**: The report states data sharing is critical for combatting ML, TF and the financing of proliferation (PF), because multinational ML/TF/PF schemes do not respect national boundaries and criminals do not exploit only one institution; illicit activity often becomes apparent only when institutions and authorities can examine an actor's aggregated activity across borders and platforms. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "illicit activity only becomes apparent when institutions and authorities can examine aggregated activity of an actor"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report cites international laundromat cases that exploited weaknesses in FIs across multiple jurisdictions to launder significant proceeds of crime, and says coordinated assessments of aggregated activity by multiple institutions may improve the overall quality of financial intelligence developed. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Coordinated assessments of aggregated activity by multiple institutions may improve the overall quality of financial intelligence developed"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The report says FIs should be conscious of the liability they may incur for breach of DPP requirements, and that generally FIs are not recommended to share personal data unless the parameters of such sharing (types of data, circumstances, communication channels, etc.) are explicitly prescribed by legislation of the jurisdiction of their operation. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs should be conscious of liability they may incur for the breach of DPP requirements"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: To better prevent and detect abuse of the international financial system for ML/TF, FIs could consider collaborating, where this complies with DPP requirements, both inside a financial group and between FIs that are not in the same group. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs could consider collaborating if and where it is compliant with DPP requirements within a financial group"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: The report says such information sharing can have authority support but could equally take place at industry level without necessarily needing government involvement, provided the parameters and purpose of the data collaboration are clearly set by law and there is effective data protection oversight of private sector implementation. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could equally occur at the industry level, and does not necessarily require governmental involvement"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Respondents noted that access to a wider set of data could improve outcomes and enable intelligence-led decision making by reducing false positives, driving prioritisation, advancing the efficiency of financial crime investigations, improving enterprise data quality and enabling greater operational efficiency; the report adds that data quality and data standardisation are important to the accuracy of collaborative analytics (section 6.2). (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "enable intelligence-led decision making by reducing false positives,"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report says advanced analytics applied to data shared by multiple FIs can reveal trends or potentially suspicious activities that could go undetected by a sole institution; respondents noted that entity resolution and network analysis allow links to be identified that are much more likely to go undetected when data is fragmentary and investigator tools are geared to compliance-style checks on individual entities. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "entity resolution and network analysis allow links to be identified"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report states that analytics allows FIs to analyse financial crime risk at scale and permits much more proactive identification of risk, and that new technologies are likely to increase the value and usefulness of the information exchanged. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "analyse financial crime risk at scale and permits much more proactive identification of risk"</sub>

## Box 4.1. United Kingdom Tribank Pilot

- **descriptive / case example**: The TriBank Pilot, in the United Kingdom in 2019 (described in the report as a 2018-2020 proof of concept), involved three large banks combining pseudonymised transactional data (dates, amounts, and tokenised sender and receiver accounts) for holistic analysis; personally identifiable information such as names and addresses was not disclosed by the participating banks. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "involved three large banks combining pseudonymised transactional data"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The TriBank Pilot demonstrated that pseudonymised transactional data can be collected from multiple participant FIs safely and effectively, combined and linked into a meaningful unified dataset, and analysed centrally; without knowledge of the underlying transacting accounts, the platform automatically identified large and complex clusters and brought them forward as candidates for further analysis by the participating institutions. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "large and complex clusters can be identified automatically"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The TriBank Pilot demonstrated two complementary approaches: (1) participating FIs supplied initial information about suspicious or concerning accounts and the platform significantly expanded this leading intelligence to show the big picture; and (2) the platform itself automatically identified significant areas of concern without leading intelligence from the FIs. Together they create a cross-bank transaction monitoring framework in which each institution contributes and benefits from others' intelligence without disclosing confidential customer information. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the platform itself automatically identified significant areas of concern without any leading intelligence being provided by the FIs"</sub>

## 4.1. Why share data?

- **descriptive / context**: Some respondents noted that in some regions, with the emergence of FinTechs and other new market participants, customers are moving away from traditional incumbent banks and using multiple institutions, so data about individual customers is increasingly dispersed across many FIs, making ML and TF insights harder to gain from a single institution's data and creating a further incentive for private-to-private data sharing and collaboration. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "data about individual customers is becoming increasingly dispersed across a wide array of FIs"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The report states that data processing must be proportionate to the legitimate purpose pursued, that a fair balance between all interests and rights must be assured at all stages of processing, and that personal data must be processed fairly and transparently and collected for explicit, specified and legitimate purposes, in compliance with data retention rules. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "data processing must be proportionate in relation to the legitimate purpose pursued"</sub>

## 4.2. What are the stated goals for private-to-private data pooling initiatives?

- **permissive / context**: The report states that, in a non-exhaustive list, FIs may decide to share data, including outside financial groups and potentially across jurisdictions, to facilitate customer due diligence measures and end-to-end technical flows. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs may decide to share data, including outside financial groups and potentially across jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Stated CDD goals for data sharing include institutional risk assessment (to more accurately gauge ML/TF risks and employ better metrics for new products and services) and on-boarding customers (to identify whether a person previously raised flags or concerns with another institution within or outside a financial group, and to verify customer risk ratings by checking for similar behaviour across business lines). (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to identify if a natural or legal person has"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Stated transaction monitoring goals for data sharing include detecting layering by examining a customer's transaction pattern to assess the financial profile, following up on abnormal activity detected across institutions, better identifying suspicious activity, and applying transaction thresholds. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to detect layering by examining the transaction"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Stated business relationship risk management goals for data sharing include updating customer information on an ongoing basis, identifying global risk exposure from on-boarding the same customer across multiple institutions, and dynamic risk management reflecting new information or changes in customer behaviour. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identify global risk exposure as a result of on-boarding of the same customer across multiple institutions"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Stated beneficial ownership goals for data sharing include enhancing accuracy of beneficial owner identification, identifying the same beneficial owner across institutions, enhancing detection of shell companies, and more efficient record-keeping of beneficial owner information. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to identify the same beneficial owner across institutions"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Stated end-to-end technical-flow goals for data sharing include identifying typologies of crime (to more rapidly and accurately identify emerging typologies, implement safeguards and share findings with other institutions and the public sector) and intelligence-driven investigations (to align investigative efforts and reach more definitive conclusions). (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to more rapidly and accurately identify"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Based on the Questionnaire, the primary reason to share or pool data for AML/CFT purposes is transaction monitoring, although some respondents said such initiatives could serve multiple purposes. Figure 2 shows the primary purposes (each respondent selecting one): transaction monitoring, risk management, identifying typologies, onboarding customers (KYC), other, and beneficial ownership identification. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the primary reason to share or pool data for AML/CFT purposes is for transaction monitoring"</sub>

## 4.1. Why share data?

- **expected / expectation**: The report says every aspect of data sharing and use of technology, including AML/CFT effectiveness and the DPP and competition impacts, should be assessed first, so that everything is properly taken into account before projects go live. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should first be assessed so that all aspects are appropriately taken into account before projects are deployed"</sub>

## 4.2. What are the stated goals for private-to-private data pooling initiatives?

- **descriptive / context**: Examples of other reasons for sharing AML/CFT data include risk reduction for better decision-making in detecting, preventing and investigating AML/CFT, feedback loops for developing and optimising data processing parameters, intelligence-driven investigations, and development of data-driven criminal typologies. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to facilitate feedback loops for developing and optimising data processing parameters"</sub>

## 4.3. What type of data could be shared?

- **descriptive / finding or result**: According to the Questionnaire, the primary types of data shared (presently or under consideration) are customer information (including beneficial ownership information), red flag information and transaction data, often in combination depending on the initiative's objective; some respondents noted customer information is shared only in an encrypted state and in a limited proof of concept. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "sharing of customer information is only occurring in an encrypted state and in the context"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: The report says the encrypted data shared could include CDD information, transactions, red flags, and indications of customer risk such as whether an STR has been filed, plus updated information on institutions in a correspondent banking relationship, including customer information where it helps their risk assessments and ongoing due diligence. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "encrypted shared data could include: CDD information; transactions; red flags"</sub>

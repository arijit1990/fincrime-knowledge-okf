## Challenges assessing ML/TF risks of VAs and VASPs

- **descriptive / finding or result**: As in 2023, jurisdictions continue to face challenges assessing and mitigating ML/TF risks from VAs and VASPs. Assessment results show 75% (98 of 130) of jurisdictions are not sufficiently implementing the requirement to conduct a VA/VASP risk assessment (R.15 sub-criteria 3), consistent with the March 2024 survey in which 29% (42 of 147) reported no such risk assessment. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "75% (98 of 130) of jurisdictions are not sufficiently implementing the requirement to conduct a risk assessment"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Recent assessment results indicate that jurisdictions have difficulty implementing measures in line with ML/TF risks identified through national risk assessments and in requiring VASPs to identify, assess, manage and mitigate ML/TF risks. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions face difficulties in implementing measures in line with ML/TF risks identified through national risk assessments"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Jurisdictions are encouraged to refer to the FATF's 2021 Guidance (paras 31-43), which includes factors jurisdictions should consider in undertaking a VA risk assessment and taking a risk-based approach. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Jurisdictions are encouraged to refer to the FATF’s 2021 Guidance"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Jurisdictions could consult the FATF's Community Workspace on Virtual Assets, which holds several examples of VA risk assessments and materials from the December 2023 VACG symposium, the December 2023 VACG meeting and the April 2024 IOSCO/FATF workshop. The workspace is available to all members of the global network; authorities request access from the FATF Secretariat. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions could consult the FATF’s Community Workspace on Virtual Assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Figure 1.4 (survey): in 2024, 105 respondents (71%) had conducted a VA/VASP risk assessment and 42 (29%) had not; in 2023 the figures were 99 (66%) and 52 (35%). (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Has your jurisdiction conducted a risk assessment of VAs/VASPs?"</sub>

## Developing and implementing a regime for VASPs

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Over a quarter of survey respondents (27%; 39 of 147) had not yet decided if and how to regulate the VA sector, a minor improvement from 2023 (29%; 45 of 151). Most (60%; 88 of 147) decided to permit VAs and VASPs. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Most jurisdictions (60%; 88 of 147 respondents) have decided to permit the use of VAs and operation of VASPs."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: 14% of respondents opted to prohibit VASPs, a steady increase from 7% in 2022 (7 of 98) and 11% in 2023 (16 of 151). Figure 1.5: 2024 permit 88, prohibit 20, undecided 39; 2023 permit 90, prohibit 16, undecided 45. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "rising from 7% of survey respondents in 2022 (7 of 98) and 11% in 2023 (16 of 151)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A prohibition approach appears more common in certain regions: survey responses show members of MENAFATF (Middle East and North Africa) and APG (Asia Pacific) more commonly chose prohibition than members of other FSRBs. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "members of MENAFATF (Middle East and North Africa region) and APG (Asia Pacific region) have more commonly"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Most jurisdictions taking a prohibition approach (70%; 14 of 20) reported explicitly prohibiting all VASPs rather than specific VASP activities. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "explicitly prohibit all VASPs rather than specific VASP activities (70%; 14 of 20)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Partial prohibitions vary; the most common is prohibiting VAs as a means of payment for goods and services while allowing VA trading and related services. Others prohibit VA use for retail investment, transfer of VAs to decentralised systems (DeFi, DEX), or use of VAs for settlement. Because the range and depth of prohibitions differ and enforcement results are limited, the FATF will keep monitoring these approaches. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the most common approach is prohibiting VAs from being utilised as a means of payment for goods and services"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: One jurisdiction that opted for prohibition noted the considerable resources and expertise needed to implement it and to ensure it is not circumvented, countering an assumption by some jurisdictions that prohibition requires fewer resources than a licensing/registration and supervisory regime. Under the FATF Standards, jurisdictions taking a prohibition approach are exempt from the full suite of R.15 requirements (e.g. licensing/registration, supervision, AML/CFT preventive measures), per the FATF Methodology footnote 44. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "considerable resources and expertise required to implement the prohibition"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A 2023 FSB report flagged that prohibiting VAs in one jurisdiction could lead activity to migrate to other jurisdictions, creating spillover risks, and concluded that blanket bans making all asset activities (e.g. trading and mining) illegal can be costly and technically demanding to enforce. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "prohibiting VAs in one jurisdiction could also lead to activity migrating to other jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Assessment results show successfully prohibiting VAs and VASPs is still challenging: only two jurisdictions partially or explicitly prohibiting VA/VASP use were assessed largely compliant, and only two jurisdictions taking a prohibition approach met the relevant R.15 requirement (sub-criteria 15.3), although more of them have conducted a risk assessment. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "only two jurisdictions taking a prohibition approach have met the relevant R.15 requirement"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Jurisdictions have not progressed in taking supervisory or enforcement actions to sanction VASPs operating illegally: nine of 14 jurisdictions explicitly prohibiting VAs and VASPs reported such actions, similar to 2023 (56%; 9 of 16). (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Nine of 14 jurisdictions explicitly prohibiting the use of VA and VASPs reported taking such actions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Figure 1.8: of 20 jurisdictions taking a prohibition approach, R.15 compliance was largely compliant 2 (10%), partially compliant 7 (35%), not compliant 7 (35%) and not applicable 4 (20%). (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Compliance with R.15 – 20 jurisdictions taking a prohibition approach to VA and VASPs"</sub>

## Licensing/Registering VASPs and taking enforcement and supervision actions

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Eighty seven percent of respondents (82 of 94), excluding those that prohibit or plan to prohibit VASPs explicitly, report requiring VASPs to be licensed or registered, and 69 of those 82 report having licensed or registered a VASP in practice. In 2023 only 44% (60 of 135) reported having issued a licence or registration in practice. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "69 of those 82 jurisdictions report having licensed or registered a VASP in practice"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Survey progress is not yet fully reflected in assessment results, possibly due to improvements since an assessment or inflated survey responses. 31% of assessed jurisdictions (40 of 130) satisfactorily require VASP licensing or registration (criterion 15.4 rated met or mostly met), compared with 30% (29 of 98) in 2023. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "possibly due to improvements made since an assessment or inflated responses to the survey"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Of the 69 jurisdictions that have licensed or registered a VASP, 78% (54 of 69) reported supervisory inspections of VASPs and 77% (53 of 69) reported enforcement or other supervisory actions against VASPs, up from 61% reporting enforcement or other supervisory actions in 2023. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "having conducted supervisory inspections of VASPs (78%; 54 of 69)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Jurisdictions struggle with identifying VASPs and with the licensing or registration process, in particular for offshore VASPs, especially lower capacity jurisdictions or those with general AML/CFT regulation and supervision shortcomings. This highlights a continued need for information, expertise-sharing and technical assistance, particularly from FATF members. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions struggle with identifying VASPs and the licensing or registration process, in particular with offshore VASPs"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Among jurisdictions that rated VAs and VASPs as high risk and neither prohibit nor plan to prohibit VASPs explicitly, 15% (5 of 34 respondents) still have no legislation in force requiring VASPs to be registered or licensed, unchanged from the 2023 Targeted Update report. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "15% (5 of 34 respondents) do not yet havelegislation in force requiring VASPs to be registered/licensed"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Figure 1.9 gives survey answers on whether a jurisdiction has legislation in force requiring VASP registration or licensing. For 2024: 69 jurisdictions have legislation and have issued licences or registrations, 13 have legislation but have issued none, 9 are introducing legislation, and 3 answered none of the above. For 2023 the corresponding figures are 61, 16, 11 and 2. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "2024 69 13 9 32023 61 16 11 20 10 20 30 40 50 60"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: An offshore VASP is a VASP that is not incorporated or physically based in the jurisdiction. Several jurisdictions require even such VASPs to be licensed or registered if they engage in certain activities (e.g. advertising to or offering services to persons in the jurisdiction), to manage ML/TF/PF risks. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "even VASPs that are not incorporated or physically located in their jurisdiction are required to be licensed/or registered"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Jurisdictions reported difficulty identifying offshore VASPs and assessing whether they undertake activities requiring registration or licensing, because VASPs sometimes take, or instruct users to take, measures to obscure users' location or other identifying information. Some jurisdictions have no licensing or registration requirements for VASPs not based or incorporated in them. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "VASPs sometimes take or instruct users to take measures to obscure their users’ location or other identifying information"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: Jurisdictions are not required but may regulate VASPs based or incorporated outside their jurisdictions (Interpretive Note to R.15 says jurisdictions may require VASPs offering products or services to customers in, or conducting operations from, their jurisdiction to be licensed or registered there). The report notes this optionality may limit the ability to mitigate ML/TF/PF risks of such VASPs. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "While jurisdictions are not required but may regulate VASPs that are based or incorporated outside their jurisdictions"</sub>

## Offshore VASPs

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Offshore VASPs may often switch locations between jurisdictions, creating additional regulatory challenges. Some continue to serve customers in jurisdictions that prohibit VAs and VASPs by circumventing requirements through multiple procedures. Industry reported that unlicensed/unregistered offshore VASPs operating in domestic markets pose additional implementation challenges for licensed/registered VASPs. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "offshore VASPs may often switch locations, moving from one jurisdiction to another"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Besides identifying jurisdictions with materially important VASP activity, the FATF held the December 2023 VACG symposium and, in April 2024, co-hosted a workshop with IOSCO to support R.15 implementation and share knowledge. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FATF held the 2023 December VACG symposium to support jurisdictions"</sub>

## FATF work to accelerate global implementation of R.15

- **descriptive / context**: The initiatives aimed to support targeted capacity building, training and knowledge sharing on AML/CFT frameworks for VAs and VASPs. Audiences were approximately 550 participants (December 2023 VACG symposium) and 260 (April 2024 IOSCO/FATF workshop), and VACG member jurisdictions shared case studies and best practices on regulatory and supervisory challenges. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "approximately 550 participants for the 2023 December VACG symposium and 260"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FATF remains committed to working with jurisdictions to facilitate R.15 implementation, including effective supervision and enforcement, and to mitigate abuse of VAs and VASPs by illicit actors. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF remains committed to working with jurisdictions to facilitate the implementation of R.15"</sub>

## SECTION TWO: Implementation of FATF'S Travel Rule

- **descriptive / definition**: The Travel Rule applies the FATF payment transparency requirements (Recommendation 16) to VAs: it requires VASPs and financial institutions to obtain, hold and transmit specific originator and beneficiary information immediately and securely when transferring VAs. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Travel Rule applies the FATF’s payment transparency requirements (FATF Recommendation 16) to the VAs context."</sub>

## Overall status of jurisdiction implementation and enforcement of the Travel Rule

- **descriptive / finding or result**: In the 2024 survey, 70% of respondents (65 of 94, excluding those that prohibit or plan to prohibit VASPs explicitly) have passed Travel Rule legislation and 15 are in the process of adopting it. This is a notable improvement on 2023 (35 passed, 27 in process) and 2022 (30 passed, 25 in process). (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "70% of respondents (65 of 94 jurisdictions, excluding those that prohibit or plan to prohibit VASPs explicitly) have passed legislation"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Almost one third of respondents have not yet passed Travel Rule legislation, and the 58 non-responding jurisdictions likely have not either, so global implementation remains incomplete and leaves VAs and VASPs vulnerable to misuse. Figure 2.1: 2024 - 65 passed, 15 in process, 14 none; 2023 - 35, 27, 28. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "global implementation remains incomplete and leaves VAs and VASPs vulnerable to misuse"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: One third of jurisdictions (32%; 11 of 34) that assessed VAs/VASPs as high risk and do not take an explicit prohibition approach have not yet passed Travel Rule legislation. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Concerningly, one third of the jurisdictions (32%; 11 of 34)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: As in 2023, Travel Rule enforcement remains weak: among the 65 jurisdictions with Travel Rule legislation, fewer than one third (26%; 17 of 65) have issued findings or directives or taken enforcement or other supervisory actions against VASPs focused on Travel Rule compliance, although there is some improvement since 2023. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "As in 2023, enforcement of the Travel Rule remains weak."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Several jurisdictions shared Travel Rule enforcement cases during public sector-only dialogues. One said that although regulated VASPs suffer from lack of interoperability among Travel Rule compliance tools, non-compliant VASPs would still be penalised. Another reported imposing regulatory orders on a VASP for non-compliance related to tool deficiencies such as incomprehensive coverage of VAs or delayed data submission. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "non-compliant VASPs would still be penalised for their compliance shortcomings"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Some jurisdictions pointed to VASP shortcomings that led to enforcement action: poor-quality data collection, possibly caused by internal instructions, which undermined screening and the STR framework; late transmission of data; no Travel Rule implementation for transactions in certain kinds of VAs; and missing the regulator-set implementation date, including not sending or receiving Travel Rule data to and from certain foreign VASPs. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "low-quality data collection possibly due to internal instructions, leading to ineffective screening and STR framework."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report calls the substantial outstanding gaps in Travel Rule implementation a serious concern, states that effectiveness depends on consistent, effective, global implementation and enforcement, and urges jurisdictions to make immediate progress to enact and enforce implementing legislation. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF urges jurisdictions to make immediate progress to enact and enforce legislation implementing the Travel Rule."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The private sector reported positive developments: increases in VA transaction volume using tools, more VASPs considering Travel Rule obligations in operations, and use of tools to mitigate illicit finance risks of counterparties. It emphasised, however, that cross-jurisdiction implementation gaps and lack of enforcement, even where obligations exist, fail to incentivise increased private sector implementation. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "failed to incentivise increased Travel Rule implementation by the private sector"</sub>

## Public and private sector challenges in Travel Rule implementation

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Industry identified continuing interoperability challenges among Travel Rule compliance tools: some progress was reported, but architectural differences and data protection requirements remain challenges. Approaches short of full interoperability were said not yet viable or to add friction; for example, VASPs can internally integrate multiple tools but this may add technical, operational and financial burdens. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "architectural differences and data protection requirements continued to pose challenges"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Industry reported common use of interVASP Messaging Standards (IVMS) as a messaging standard for Travel Rule information, replicating ISO20022 for the VA sector, and recognised potential future work to align message functionality (e.g. how to reject transactions or follow up with questions). (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "interVASP Messaging Standards (IVMS) as a messaging standard for Travel Rule information"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Industry noted increased sophistication of VA transfers, including the role of professional traders and over-the-counter brokers, suggesting some Travel Rule compliance tools are designed for transactions between individual users and may not be fully fit for purpose for broader transaction types. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "some Travel Rule compliance tools are designed for transactions between individual users"</sub>

## Sunrise Issue

- **descriptive / definition**: The 'sunrise issue' arises from delays and inconsistencies in country implementation of the Travel Rule: compliance needs both the ordering and beneficiary VASP to provide information, but both may not be required by their national authorities to comply. Ordering institutions can require Travel Rule compliance regardless of beneficiary jurisdiction regulation, but until all VASPs are required to implement the Travel Rule, VASPs in or from compliant jurisdictions will face challenges executing all transactions compliantly. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Delays and inconsistencies in country implementation of the Travel Rule results in the ‘sunrise issue’."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Of 80 jurisdictions that have implemented or are implementing the Travel Rule, nearly one third (33%; 26 of 80) still take a phased approach (e.g. requiring implementation only in certain circumstances, a higher threshold, or manual data processing with short delay) or allow a grace period with exemptions or flexibility, as in 2023. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "nearly one third (33%; 26 of 80) are still taking a phased approach to implementation"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Of the 65 jurisdictions that have passed Travel Rule legislation, 55% (36 of 65) have measures to ensure domestic VASPs only transact with regulated and/or Travel Rule-compliant counterparts or otherwise mitigate risks: transact only with licensed/registered foreign counterparts (5 respondents); only with licensed/registered and Travel Rule-compliant ones (15); with foreign VASPs licensed/registered in specific jurisdictions and/or complying with the Travel Rule (4); or with unlicensed/unregistered foreign counterparts only where risk mitigating measures are taken (12). (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "allowing domestic VASPs to transact only with licensed/registered and Travel Rule-compliant foreign counterparts (15 respondents)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: 15% of jurisdictions (10 of 65) that have passed Travel Rule legislation still allow domestic VASPs to transact with any foreign VASP, regardless of licensing/registration, Travel Rule compliance or risk mitigating measures. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "still allow domestic VASPs to transact with any foreign VASP, regardless of licensing/registration"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FATF will continue to accelerate R.15 implementation, as the sunrise issue will only be resolved with widespread implementation of the Standards including the Travel Rule, and calls on all jurisdictions to rapidly enact and enforce the Travel Rule. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF calls on all jurisdictions to rapidly enact and enforce the Travel Rule."</sub>

## Counterparty due diligence

- **descriptive / definition**: Per the FATF 2021 Updated Guidance, counterparty due diligence ensures VASPs avoid dealing with illicit or sanctioned actors and helps ensure a counterparty can comply with the Travel Rule, including protecting confidentiality of shared information; counterparty due diligence for R.16 purposes is distinct from the obligations for cross-border correspondent relationships (R.13). (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Counterparty due diligence ensures VASPs avoid dealing with illicit or sanctioned actors"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: To transmit required Travel Rule information a VASP must identify and conduct due diligence on its counterparty VASP; this remains challenging because of difficulty identifying the counterparty VASP from a VA wallet address and varying counterparty due diligence requirements across jurisdictions. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "difficulties in identifying the counterparty VASP based on VAs wallet address"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The private sector said public national lists of registered or licensed VASPs facilitate counterparty due diligence; research by one private sector participant found at least 34 jurisdictions with a VA regulatory regime lack public data on licensed or registered VASPs. Absence of such information can make it hard to determine which providers may undertake VA activity in a jurisdiction or require manual collection of VASP data per transaction. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "at least 34 jurisdictions with a VAs regulatory regime lack a public data on licensed or registered VASPs"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Where only one of the originator and beneficiary VASPs has Travel Rule obligations because of differing national requirements, VASPs should still take steps to comply with targeted financial sanctions obligations. One possible mitigation, chosen by 12 jurisdictions, is permitting VASPs to transact with unlicensed/unregistered foreign counterparts only if the originator VASP takes risk mitigating measures. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "VASPs should still take steps to comply with targeted financial sanctions obligations"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The private sector said that VASPs' ability to transact with foreign VASPs that are unlicensed/unregistered and/or not Travel Rule compliant adds counterparty due diligence difficulty, given trouble assessing if and where counterparties are registered or licensed, whether they have Travel Rule obligations, and their risks. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "difficulties in assessing if and where counterparties were registered or licensed"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Survey results show that many jurisdictions continue to let domestic VASPs deal with foreign VASPs that are unlicensed or unregistered and/or not Travel Rule compliant. If the counterparty VASP and beneficiary location are not properly identified before a VA transfer completes, the originator VASP is unable to fully manage the risk of breaching economic sanctions, for example by sending to a restricted economic area. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the originator VASP cannot fully manage the risk of breaching economic sanction obligations"</sub>

## Issues with Travel Rule compliance tools

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The 2022 and 2023 Targeted Update reports note the industry has developed a range of Travel Rule compliance tools, but many do not fully meet FATF standards and interoperability challenges remain. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "many do not fully meet the FATF standards and there remain challenges regarding interoperability"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: As set out in the 2023 Targeted Update (Table 2.1), common tool shortcomings include failure to transmit information immediately, which undermines the Travel Rule's purpose (e.g. enabling sanctions screening) and a VASP's ability to conduct counterparty due diligence. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "common shortcomings include a failure to transmit information immediately"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: To avoid large-scale adoption of non-compliant tools, regulators and supervisors should continue engaging with their VASP sector to identify commonly used compliance tools and ensure they meet all FATF requirements, and take supervisory or enforcement action for Travel Rule violations in their jurisdiction to set precedent and deter non-compliance. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Regulators and supervisors should continue engaging with their VASP sector to identify commonly used compliance tools"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Travel Rule compliance tool providers should evaluate and address possible shortcomings, including deficiencies identified in the 2023 Targeted Update, and take appropriate remediation. Some of those deficiencies remain, and some VASPs have faced enforcement actions for failing to comply while using deficient tools. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Travel Rule compliance tool providers should evaluate and address possible shortcomings"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: VASPs should take a deliberative and informed decision and select compliance tool(s) that allow them to meet all FATF Travel Rule requirements. Box 2.1 sets out guiding questions VASPs should ask, and regulators and supervisors should also use the questions when engaging VASPs and tool providers. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "VASPs should take a deliberative and informed decision and select a compliance tool(s)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Interoperability among Travel Rule compliance tools is not required by the FATF standards, but lack of it creates friction and reduces VASPs' ability to identify counterparties and send and receive Travel Rule information, with knock-on effects for transaction monitoring and detecting suspicious activity. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "While interoperability among Travel Rule compliance tools is not required in the FATF standards"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Discrete, rather than interconnected, Travel Rule tools with closed participant lists may complicate identification of counterparty VASPs and could cause a counterparty VASP to be misidentified as an unhosted wallet simply because it did not use the same tool as the beneficiary. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could result in the misidentification of a counterparty VASP as an unhosted wallet"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: The FATF urges the private sector to progress towards greater compatibility among Travel Rule compliance tools, whether via technological advances enabling interoperability, relationships permitting transactions through a chain of interoperable tools, or other methods. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF urges the private sector to progress towards increasing compatibility"</sub>

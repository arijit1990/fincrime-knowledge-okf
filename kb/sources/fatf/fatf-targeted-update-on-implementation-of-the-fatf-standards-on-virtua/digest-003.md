## Issues with Travel Rule compliance tools

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Seven additional jurisdictions noted in 2024 survey responses that VASPs use newly and locally developed in-house Travel Rule compliance tools, an increase from 3 jurisdictions in 2023, rather than third-party tools. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "VASPs are using in-house Travel Rule compliance tools that are newly and locally developed"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Some VACG members want to better understand how in-house tools transmit or receive data from VASPs using other tools or from foreign VASPs, and one delegation expressed concern about the effectiveness of these tools in enabling VASPs to comply. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "one delegation expressed concerns around the effectiveness of these tools"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Supervisory authorities and regulated VASPs are encouraged to engage in a collaborative process with Travel Rule compliance tool providers to ensure tools enable VASPs to meet regulatory requirements, ideally before VASPs start using the tool(s). The VACG set out a list of questions and considerations in Box 2.1. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "are encouraged to engage in a collaborative process with Travel Rule compliance tool providers"</sub>

## Box 2.1. Guiding questions and considerations for Travel Rule compliance tool providers

- **recommended / recommendation**: Box 2.1 states its questions may be useful to help VASPs and jurisdictions engage with tool providers, and that VASPs and jurisdictions should consider them in addition to the guiding questions on p. 25 of the 2023 Targeted Update. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "VASPs and jurisdictions should consider the below in addition to the guiding questions included on p. 25"</sub>

## Timing and scope of Travel Rule data submission

- **descriptive / method**: Box 2.1 question on coverage: does the tool cover all VA types offered or transferred by a VASP, and if not, what alternative plan or method does the VASP use for Travel Rule compliance? (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Does the tool cover all VA types that are offered/transferred by a VASP?"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Box 2.1 question on data format: does the tool use an embedded and structured data format meeting global industry standards such as ISO20022, which could enable VASPs to conduct sanction screening and transaction monitoring more effectively? (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Does the tool use an embedded and structured data format that meet global industry standards such as ISO20022?"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Box 2.1 question on timing: does the tool allow a VASP to submit and obtain Travel Rule information securely in sufficient time for originating, beneficiary and intermediary institutions, i.e. simultaneously or before the transaction is executed on the blockchain, with no exceptions? (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "simultaneously or before the transaction is executed on the blockchain, with no exceptions"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Box 2.1 question on selective sending: does the tool let ordering VASPs submit Travel Rule information to certain beneficiary VASPs, or have a function (possibly automatic/pre-programmed) letting the originator VASP choose not to send data to a counterparty VASP, for example to avoid providing services to sanctioned jurisdictions, high TF/PF risk areas, or jurisdictions with lower data privacy protection? (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "choose not to send TR data when the originator VASP does not want to send the data"</sub>

## Counterparty VASP identification and due diligence

- **descriptive / method**: Box 2.1 question on communication: does the tool give VASPs a secure communication channel to follow up with a counterparty VASP, both to seek information enabling the VASP to conduct required counterparty due diligence and to request information on a transaction to determine whether it involves high-risk or prohibited activities? (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Does the tool provide VASPs with a secure communication channel to help follow-up with a counterparty VASP"</sub>

## Questions on interoperability with other Travel Rule compliance tools and "in-house" tools

- **descriptive / method**: Box 2.1 interoperability questions (in addition to the 2023 Targeted Update list): how does the tool transmit data to or receive data from external counterparty VASPs, and for transaction types and amounts it does not cover; does it have limitations on which VASPs it can send to or receive from; and what is its customer base and does it sufficiently cover the VASP's need to transfer VAs overseas? (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Does the tool have limitations regarding to/from which VASPs it can send/receive data?"</sub>

## Counterparty VASP identification and due diligence

- **mandatory / requirement**: Box 2.1 reminds that VASPs are required to assess counterparty risk independently, as set out in the FATF 2021 Guidance. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "VASPs are required to independently assess counterparty risk as set out in the FATF’s 2021 Guidance"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Using the same Travel Rule compliance tool, or belonging to the same financial group, does not by itself remove a VASP's need to verify the information independently and to ensure that all relevant domestic obligations are met. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "does not automatically remove the need for VASPs to independently verify the information"</sub>

## Proposed revisions of R.16 and implications on the Travel Rule implementation

- **descriptive / context**: In February 2024 the FATF began a public consultation on potential changes to Recommendation 16 and its Interpretive Note on payment transparency. The proposed revisions seek to adapt the Standard to changes in payment system business models and messaging standards (ISO 20022) and ensure R.16 remains technology-neutral and follows the principle of 'same activity, same risk, same rules'. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In February 2024, the FATF began a public consultation on the potential changes to Recommendation 16"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: As R.16 is the source of the Travel Rule, its updates may affect the VA sector, including what originator and beneficiary information must be transmitted and the roles and responsibilities of VASPs in complex and/or hybrid payment chains. Once the R.16 revision is finalised, the FATF will decide whether the Travel Rule requirement for VASPs will change. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FATF will decide whether the Travel Rule requirement for VASPs will change"</sub>

## Use of VAs for predicate offences, money laundering, terrorist financing and proliferation financing

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Industry reported that the DPRK continued to steal or extort virtual assets. One firm reported additional cybersecurity measures by some firms and DeFi arrangements reduced the amount stolen year over year; another blockchain analytics company noted DPRK-stolen funds fell from USD 1.7 billion in 2022 to USD 1.0 billion in 2023. One jurisdiction noted price fluctuations in VAs also contributed to the reduction. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "DPRK-stolen funds fell from USD 1.7 billion in 2022 to USD 1.0 billion in 2023"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Two private sector firms described DPRK use of increasingly sophisticated laundering methods, often involving anonymity-enhancing coins, mixers, DeFi arrangements and cross-chain bridges, before converting stablecoins into fiat at over-the-counter brokers concentrated in certain jurisdiction(s). (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "often involving anonymity-enhancing coins, mixers, DeFi arrangements, and cross-chain bridges"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Private sector participants highlighted VAs in investment fraud schemes, in particular 'pig butchering' scams, where fraudsters gain victims' confidence before enticing them to invest in fraudulent VASPs. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "in which fraudsters gain the confidence of their victims before eventually enticing them to make investments in fraudulent VASPs"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: One participant noted an increase in ransomware attacks and payments, which are almost always demanded in virtual assets, focusing on large, high-value or high-profile organisations that attackers think are more likely to pay. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "ransomware attacks and payments, which are almost always demanded in virtual assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Participants noted increasing use of VAs by terrorist groups, in particular groups in Syria and ISIL in Asia, while recognising that the majority of TF continued to occur in cash and other traditional methods; terrorist groups using VAs often used stablecoins and experimented with anonymity-enhancing cryptocurrencies. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the majority of TF continued to occur in cash and other traditional methods"</sub>

## Stablecoins

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Public and private sector participants recognised increased adoption of stablecoins for illicit activities, including fundraising campaigns by terrorist groups, supported by blockchain analytics reports finding increased use in particular of Tether's USDT. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "increased adoption and use of stablecoins for illicit activities, including fundraising campaigns by terrorist groups"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Mass adoption of VAs, including stablecoins, could potentially decrease the use of AML/CFT-obliged entities, since stablecoins stored in unhosted wallets could potentially be used to purchase goods without conversion to fiat. As discussed in the Updated Guidance, certain stablecoins could have greater potential for mass adoption, which could amplify illicit finance risks associated with VAs more broadly. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could potentially decrease the use of AML/CFT-obliged entities"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The report notes that 'stablecoins' are not a legal or technical category and use of the term does not endorse stability claims; under the revised FATF Standards a stablecoin is considered either a virtual asset or a traditional financial asset depending on its exact nature. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "So-called ‘stablecoins’ are not a legal or technical category"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A range of entities in a stablecoin arrangement, such as issuers and intermediaries, may be VASPs or otherwise have AML/CFT obligations; if they offer services in a jurisdiction where they are not based, incorporated, or licensed/registered (offshore), they could present particular illicit finance risks and require international cooperation. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A range of entities in a stablecoin arrangement such as issuers and intermediaries may be VASPs"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report stresses that relevant FATF Standards need to be effectively implemented before mass adoption of stablecoins to manage and mitigate ML/TF/PF risks. Several jurisdictions reported implementing regulatory frameworks for stablecoin issuers and intermediaries, including the Travel Rule. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "importance of relevant FATF Standards being effectively implemented before the mass adoption of stablecoins"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Stablecoin service providers said they can use risk mitigation measures including blockchain analytics to monitor use of their stablecoins, even for transactions to which the provider is not directly party. Some stablecoin arrangements can freeze or burn stablecoins via programmability in the token code, most frequently at the request of law enforcement or in line with targeted financial sanctions obligations. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "some stablecoin arrangements have the ability to freeze or burn stablecoins via programmability"</sub>

## Decentralised Finance (DeFi)

- **descriptive / finding or result**: As in the 2022 and 2023 Targeted Updates, identifying individuals or entities exercising control or influence over DeFi arrangements remains challenging. VACG members agree many DeFi arrangements are decentralised in name only, while recognising regulatory challenges in applying the FATF Standards, particularly in identifying regulatory anchor points and where DeFi platforms are located, operating or licensed/registered. The FATF will keep monitoring. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "many DeFi arrangements are decentralised in name only"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Several discussion forums during VACG outreach considered the degree to which DeFi arrangements are in practice decentralised but reached no consensus. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Several discussion forums considered the degree to which DeFi arrangements are in practice decentralised"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Around half of jurisdictions (49%; 39 of 80) more advanced in regulating VASPs (those that passed Travel Rule legislation or are in process) require certain DeFi arrangements to be licensed or registered as a VASP, e.g. where the creator, owner or operator maintains control or sufficient influence. Of the 51% (41 of 80) that do not apply their VASP AML/CFT framework to DeFi entities, 44% (18 of 41) are taking steps to identify and address risks and 42% (17 of 41) are taking no specific steps. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "around half of jurisdictions (49%; 39 of 80) that are more advanced in regulating VASPs"</sub>

## Box 3.1 Case study: How the SCA dealt with a Defi exchange

- **descriptive / case example**: Case study: the UAE Securities and Commodities Authority (SCA) noticed an entity advertising on its website that it was licensed by UAE competent authorities; investigation found it was a DeFi exchange claiming to be licensed in every jurisdiction where it advertises. The SCA issued an investor alert that the company is not licensed by the SCA or any other UAE authority, asked the Competent Telecommunications Authority to consider blocking the website from the UAE, and shared findings and actions with relevant competent authorities. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Securities and Commodities Authority (SCA) noticed an entity advertising on its website that it is licensed"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Figure 3.1: in 2024, 39 jurisdictions required DeFi arrangements conducting VA activities and/or meeting certain criteria to be licensed/registered as VASPs and 41 said the VASP AML/CFT framework does not apply to DeFi; in 2023 the figures were 37 and 25. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AML/CFT framework for VASPs does not apply to DeFi arrangements."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: As in 2023, most jurisdictions (77%; 30 of 39) have not identified any unregistered/unlicensed DeFi entities qualifying as VASPs, which may indicate continued difficulty identifying DeFi entities within the VASP regulatory perimeter. 9 jurisdictions identified DeFi entities qualifying as VASPs and/or took supervisory or enforcement action; 2 (5%; 2 of 39) have registered or licensed DeFi entities as VASPs in practice, one more than in 2023. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "most jurisdictions (77%; 30 of 39) have not identified any unregistered/unlicensed DeFi entities that qualify as VASPs"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Table 3.2 (approach to DeFi): 2 jurisdictions have registered/licensed DeFi entities as VASPs; 5 have taken supervisory or enforcement action against DeFi entities qualifying as VASPs; 2 have identified unregistered/unlicensed DeFi entities qualifying as VASPs without taking action; 30 have identified none. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "My jurisdiction has not identified any unregistered/unlicensed"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: One jurisdiction identified automated market making (AMM) services as VASP-relevant activities: programming and maintenance of the protocol, control over the smart contracts needed to use the AMM, and collection of fees from the AMM lead to attributing the DeFi activity to the programmer/provider of the smart contracts. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "services offered in the area of automated market making (AMM) were identified as VASP relevant activities"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Another jurisdiction's definition of virtual asset trading platform covers both centralised and decentralised digital platforms, and the operator is deemed the owner of the entity where no single entity or group controlling the platform can be identified. One entity is registered there as a decentralised exchange but in reality has a centralised oversight structure. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "covers both a centralised or decentralised digital platform under the existing regulatory framework"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Many jurisdictions, including those without a regulatory approach to DeFi, recognise DeFi risks and are developing or conducting risk assessments. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "recognise the risks associated with DeFi arrangements and are in the process of developing or conducting risk assessments"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Some jurisdictions are exploring other ways to address DeFi ML/TF risks reflecting DeFi's technical characteristics, such as composability, which allows integration of compliance functions like targeted financial sanctions screening. One jurisdiction launched a pilot to develop, deploy and test embedded supervision of DeFi at the technical protocol level, with automated supervisory data gathering directly from the blockchain to identify potential prudential policy measures; another is researching embedded supervision to potentially mitigate risks from DeFi and P2P transactions. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "composability which allows the integration of compliance functions such as screening for targeted financial sanctions"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: One jurisdiction emphasised that efforts for embedded supervision should be deliberately assessed before implementation and may not be appropriate for all jurisdictions. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should be deliberately assessed prior to implementation and may not be appropriate for all jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A jurisdiction's DeFi-focused risk assessment recognised that AML/CFT compliance tools and public blockchain data analysis could partially mitigate DeFi risks, but found many tools need further technical development and adjustment to meet AML/CFT requirements, and that anonymity-enhancing tools and methods could limit the ability to mine data from public blockchains. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AML/CFT compliance tools and public blockchain data analysis could partially mitigate risks DeFi arrangements"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In line with the 2023 Targeted Update, jurisdictions recognised that DeFi arrangements still account for a relatively low percentage of overall VA activity, while noting the need to monitor them for illicit finance risks. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "DeFi arrangements still account for a relatively low percentage of overall VA activity"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Private sector participants posited that illicit finance activity could migrate to DeFi arrangements from centralised VASPs as jurisdictions progress on R.15, and survey findings support that even jurisdictions with mature VASP AML/CFT frameworks may not cover DeFi. DeFi arrangements were most commonly used to launder proceeds from investment fraud, and DeFi hacks fell from USD 3.1 billion in 2022 to USD 1.1 billion in 2023 per blockchain analytics estimates, perhaps partly due to increased cybersecurity measures. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "hacks of DeFi arrangements had decreased from USD 3.1 billion in 2022 to USD 1.1 billion in 2023"</sub>

## Unhosted Wallets, including Peer-to-Peer (P2P) Transactions

- **descriptive / finding or result**: As in 2023, even among jurisdictions with more advanced VASP regulation, the majority (64%; 51 of 80) have not yet evaluated or started evaluating specific risks of self-hosted wallets or P2P transactions; data gaps remain a main challenge. Several (15%; 12 of 80) are collecting and assessing P2P market metrics to evaluate ML/TF/PF risks from unhosted wallets and reflect them in the NRA, strategy or other policy documents, mostly as part of updating the NRA. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "have not yet evaluated or started evaluating the specific risks related to self-hosted wallets or P2P transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Public and private sectors agreed that volume going through unhosted wallets decreased in 2023. The private sector observed unhosted wallets being used in the layering stage of money laundering, but emphasised that VASPs continued to play a critical role as on- and off-ramps to fiat currency. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "agreed that volume going through unhosted wallets decreased in 2023"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: One jurisdiction analysed the size of its P2P market, identified its mode as a vulnerability in its risk assessment and issued guidelines on the risks of this business; another developed guidance for VASPs on Enhanced Due Diligence (EDD) for unhosted wallets and unregistered VASPs. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "developed a guidance for VASPs on how to undertake Enhanced Due Diligence (EDD) for unhosted wallets and unregistered VASPs"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: For transactions between unhosted wallets and VASPs, many jurisdictions require VASPs to take risk mitigation measures per the 2021 Guidance. Examples: requiring blockchain analytics tools to identify and assess the risk of destination unhosted wallets, allowing transfers only to unhosted wallets controlled by the originator, applying Travel Rule-like requirements to VASP-unhosted wallet transactions, and requiring enhanced due diligence. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "only allowing transfers to unhosted wallets controlled by the originator"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Since July 2023 outreach, participants emphasised the importance of Travel Rule implementation as wide implementation can increase VASPs' risk awareness and ease identification of transactions to or from unhosted wallets. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "wide implementation of the Travel Rule can increase VASPs’ risk awareness"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: One jurisdiction described lower-risk unhosted wallet transactions common at local VASPs, where users earn VAs through play-to-earn apps and transfer them from an app-connected unhosted wallet to a VASP wallet. Because of domestic data collection and due diligence requirements on unhosted wallet transactions, regulated VASPs must contact customers to obtain required information, assess transaction risk and determine whether the information is trustworthy. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "regulated VASPs have the obligation to contact customers to obtain required information"</sub>

## Non-Fungible Tokens (NFTs)

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Among jurisdictions more advanced in regulating VASPs, most (53%; 42 of 80) primarily regulate NFTs as VAs where appropriate (e.g. where NFTs are used for payment or investment purposes), while 45% (36 of 80) do not apply their AML/CFT framework to NFTs. Two jurisdictions reported regulating NFTs as art or cultural objects, where none had in previous reports. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "primarily regulating NFTs as VAs where appropriate"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: One jurisdiction's NFT illicit finance risk assessment found NFTs and NFT platforms rarely used to date for proliferation or terrorist financing, but highly susceptible to fraud and scams and subject to theft; criminals can also use NFTs to launder proceeds of predicate crimes, often combined with other methods to obfuscate the illicit source of funds. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "NFTs and NFT platforms are to date rarely being used for proliferation or terrorist financing"</sub>

## SECTION FOUR: Next Steps for the FATF and VACG

- **descriptive / context**: In line with the Roadmap, the FATF and VACG will continue outreach and assistance to jurisdictions with materially important VASP activity and lower capacity jurisdictions, including by using FATF internal online platforms to share R.15 material (training, example legislation, guidance, risk assessments, other jurisdictions' approaches). (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Using FATF’s internal online platforms to share material related to R.15"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FATF will work to expand VACG participation to the FSRBs to accelerate R.15 implementation by FSRB members, and will publish the next Targeted Update in 2025 on R.15 progress and regulatory responses to emerging VA risks, together with an updated public table of jurisdictions with materially important VASP activity. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF will publish the next Targeted Update in 2025"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FATF will continue monitoring VA ecosystem updates, including development of Travel Rule tools and jurisdictions' Travel Rule implementation, and market developments (including DeFi and unhosted wallets/P2P) to keep the Standards relevant; it will gather practices on risk assessments, regulatory frameworks and supervisory and enforcement actions on stablecoins, DeFi and P2P, and serve as a platform to share findings and leading practices. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "continue to monitor market developments in order to ensure that FATF Standards remain relevant"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Where possible, the FATF and VACG will facilitate outreach and engagement focused on the R.15 elements commonly seen as challenging (for example risk assessment, licensing and registration, and Travel Rule implementation), giving priority to jurisdictions with materially important VASP activity. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "prioritising jurisdictions with materially important VASP activity"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: As far as possible, the FATF and VACG will organise forums, workshops or webinars so that jurisdictions can share experiences and build capacity, including by supporting wider efforts, where possible in collaboration with international organisations such as the IMF, World Bank and IOSCO that have resources, tools and experience in technical assistance for the VA sector. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Organising forums, workshops or webinars as possible to facilitate jurisdictions sharing their experiences and to build capacity."</sub>

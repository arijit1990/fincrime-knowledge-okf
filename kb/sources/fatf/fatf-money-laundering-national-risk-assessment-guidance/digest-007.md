## Annex A. Types of Risk Assessments that Complement the NRA

- **descriptive / definition**: A supranational risk assessment (SNRA) is analysis conducted at a regional or international level that identifies ML risks across multiple countries or member states in a regional or international organisation such as the European Union or an FSRB. It will normally be led by the supranational organisation so it considers cross-border threats and vulnerabilities affecting the bloc or region, rather than consolidating individual countries' assessments. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A supranational risk assessment (SNRA) is analysis conducted at a regional or international level"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supranational risk assessments should not replace the national risk assessments conducted by individual member countries, which remain best suited to identify, assess and determine how to mitigate their own risks; the SNRA results can however assist countries in conducting their own assessments. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Such risk assessments should not replace the national risk assessments to be conducted by the individual member countries"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Supranational risk assessments provide a comprehensive regional perspective, analysing risks that transcend national borders which cannot always be identified and mitigated through an individual country approach. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supranational risk assessments provide a comprehensive analysis of risks that transcend national borders"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Supranational assessments may promote standardisation of risk assessment methodologies, mitigating measures, risk ratings and regulatory frameworks across countries, giving a consistent and collaborative approach to high-risk areas in the region; for example, member countries conducting their NRA may consider assessing the risks of all relevant products and services assessed under the supranational assessment. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This approach may promote the standardisation of risk assessment methodologies, mitigating measures, risk ratings and regulatory frameworks"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Supranational assessments facilitate information sharing and collaboration between countries, agencies within countries and international organisations, enabling and encouraging sharing of good practices, intelligence and resources to combat ML regionally. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supranational risk assessments facilitate information sharing and collaboration between countries, agencies within countries and international organisations."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Because supranational assessments can use the resources and expertise of multiple countries, they enhance identification and assessment of emerging threats, vulnerabilities and cross-border risks that may not be apparent at national level. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "As supranational risk assessments can utilise the resources and expertise of multiple countries"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A supranational risk assessment may result in common recommendations for member countries, including public authorities and private sector operators, leading to a more robust and harmonised approach to mitigating identified risks. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A supranational risk assessment may result in the development of common recommendations for member countries"</sub>

## Box A.1. Supranational Risk Assessments

- **descriptive / case example**: The European Commission published SNRAs in 2017, 2019 and 2022, with a fourth to be published in 2025. The 2022 SNRA gives an overview of ML/TF risks in the EU and recommendations for countering them. (p. 69)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The European Commission has already published three supranational risk assessments (SNRA), in 2017, 2019 and in 2022."</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Article 6(1) of the Anti-Money Laundering Directive requires the EC to update the SNRA in principle every two years, reassessing all sectors covered in previous iterations and fine-tuning information on sectors where risks have not changed; where risks have changed, the EC recalculates the risk levels. (p. 69)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Article 6(1) of the Anti-Money Laundering Directive requires the EC to update the SNRA in principle every two years"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The 2022 EU SNRA analyses current ML/TF risks and assesses how well countries implemented the previous iterations' risk-mitigation recommendations, evaluating residual risk levels. The EC consulted widely with EU-level organisations, national experts and academia, and considered member countries' NRAs; many EU member states reported consulting the SNRA in their own NRA process. (p. 69)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "assesses how well countries have implemented the previous iterations’ recommendations for risk mitigation, evaluating residual risk levels"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Under the new EU AML/CFT framework, an AML/CFT risk assessment at Union level (Union RA) will be published in 2028 (Article 7 Directive (EU) 2024/1640) and updated every four years; the European Commission may update parts of the report more frequently, if appropriate. (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "will be published in the year 2028 (Article 7 Directive (EU) 2024/1640) and updated every four years"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The Union RA report shall cover at least the areas and sectors of the internal market exposed to ML/TF risks; the nature and level of risks for each; the most widespread means used to launder illicit proceeds, including where available those used in transactions between Member States and third countries; and an assessment of ML/TF risks associated with legal persons and legal arrangements, including exposure to risks from foreign legal persons and arrangements and risks of non-implementation and evasion of targeted financial sanctions. (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This report shall cover at least the areas and sectors of the internal market that are exposed"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The new EU AML/CFT framework also includes a four-year update requirement for NRAs ("at least every 4 years", Article 8(1) Directive (EU) 2024/1640). (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is also a four-year update requirement for NRAs"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In 2020-2022 the Eurasian Group (EAG) conducted an ML/TF risk assessment of the Eurasian region, identifying regional risks, threats and vulnerabilities and sub-regional risks (for specific groups of member states or supranational entities). It produced an Action Plan (Roadmap) including organisational and supranational measures, such as regional mechanisms and tools to address technical compliance deficiencies in national legislation and improve effectiveness of national AML/CFT/CPF systems. (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In 2020-2022, the EAG conducted a risk assessment of ML/TF in the Eurasian region"</sub>
- **recommended / case example**: EAG members are encouraged to use the results of the supranational risk assessment to inform their NRAs, and many EAG members reported consulting it as part of their national risk assessment process. (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "EAG members are encouraged to use the results of this supranational risk assessment"</sub>

## Annex A. Types of Risk Assessments that Complement the NRA

- **descriptive / context**: Sub-national assessments give a granular understanding of ML threats and vulnerabilities in specific areas such as cities, regions or districts, considering factors that differ from other parts of the country (e.g., demographics, proximity to a border, economic development, levels of crime), enabling targeted analysis of local contributing factors. (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Sub-national ML risk assessments provide a granular understanding of ML threats and vulnerabilities within specific geographic areas"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: By focusing on local contexts, sub-national assessments enable tailored interventions and risk mitigation strategies addressing the specific needs and challenges of communities, industries or sectors in the area. (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "sub-national assessments enable the development of tailored interventions and risk mitigation strategies"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Sub-national assessments involve local government, LEAs, businesses and community groups to foster collaboration and engagement in the risk assessment process, and may lead to early detection of emerging ML trends and typologies in specific areas so authorities can act and mitigate risks before they escalate to national level. (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This approach involves local government, LEAs, businesses and community groups"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: A sub-national risk assessment examines the ML risks particular to one geographic area; it is commonly used in countries with a federal system or with wide geographic reach, including overseas territories. (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A sub-national risk assessment looks at ML risks specific to a particular geographic area"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Where a country carries out several sub-national risk assessments, it should also consider consolidating their findings in an NRA. (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "If a country undertakes multiple sub-national risk assessments, it should also consider consolidating these findings in an NRA."</sub>

## Box A.2. Sub-national Risk Assessment

- **descriptive / case example**: Germany's NRA builds on other assessments and sources, including the EU SNRA, sectoral or issue-specific assessments by federal authorities and the Länder, and input from the private sector and academia. (p. 71)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Germany’s NRA is based on and builds on other risk assessments and sources"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: After the NRA, Germany's 16 Länder were tasked with conducting their own independent risk assessments, which identified regional differences and sometimes different risk conclusions from the national NRA. For example, the NRA rated traders of goods, including art and antiques, as medium-high ML risk, while Bavaria's assessment deemed the risk in the region low, considering market size, customer base and transaction types; some Länder assessments offered more nuanced views of DNFBP risk. Germany plans to bring these findings together for a national understanding of relative risks across sectors and regions. (p. 71)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "while the NRA rated traders of goods, including art and antiques, as having a medium-high ML risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Coordination challenge: all risk assessments may face coordination challenges, particularly supranational and sub-national ones, as stakeholders may have no existing relationship. The entity leading the process should ensure adequate support and coordination, and collaboration may improve relationships across borders. (p. 71)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is important that the entity leading the process ensures adequate support and coordination as part of the process"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Loss of local specificity (supranational) or national-level breadth (sub-national): supranational assessments may focus less on national-level risks, as they aim to understand global and cross-border risks affecting the group of countries, and may lack granularity and country-specific contextual factors. The SNRA does not typically replace the requirement for a national risk assessment, where the granularity needed for detailed risk understanding is achieved. (p. 72)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the SNRA does not typically replace the requirement to conduct a risk assessment at a national level"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Dependency on external risk assessments (supranational): relying solely on external information and expertise may lead a country to adopt those methodologies, frameworks and risk indicators, which does not allow it to adjust its approach to its own risk and context. (p. 72)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Relying solely on external sources of information and expertise for risk assessments may result in a country adopting those methodologies"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: "Ownership" of process: some countries may see supranational assessments as encroaching on their right to manage their own risks and not feel ownership of a process managed by a supranational organisation, so early and continuous engagement is needed. Sub-national assessments may struggle to engage local authorities and promote the necessity of risk understanding, particularly where resources, capacity and risk-awareness are limited. (p. 73)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Some countries may see supranational risk assessments as encroaching on their right to manage their own risks"</sub>

## Annex B. High-level Summary of Responses to Public Consultation

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Consultation Q1 (how the private sector, civil society and academia can best contribute to the NRA): the most useful ways were regular forums and discussions, open surveys and consultations, participation in specialised working groups, collaboration with academic institutions on specific in-depth research, and provision of technical expertise and specialised advice. (p. 73)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "regular forums and discussions, open surveys and consultations, participation in specialised working groups"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Other Q1 responses identified multi-sector collaboration between civil society, private organisations and academia to identify and assess emerging risks, and early private sector engagement so that risk categories correspond to industry practices, allowing granular and appropriate application of risk weightings. (p. 73)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "early engagement of the private sector at an early stage in the process would ensure that risk categories"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Consultation Q2 (most effective ways for countries to engage these stakeholders): educational workshops and seminars and specific training sessions for the sectors concerned; digital platforms for feedback and reporting; collaboration with chambers of commerce and professional associations and their active participation in policy review; and all agreed on a structured, continuous approach to collecting and responding to feedback. (p. 73)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the setting up of educational workshops and seminars, and the planning of specific training sessions for the sectors concerned"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Consultation Q3 (how stakeholders use NRA outputs): NRA outputs enable compliance and risk management policies, facilitate improved training and awareness programs, and enable adjustments to obliged entities' operational strategies based on identified risks. (p. 73)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "NRA outputs enable compliance and risk management policies, facilitate the improvement of training and awareness programs"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Further Q3 responses: NRA outputs encourage new initiatives to mitigate risks, ongoing monitoring and evaluation of risks, and learning programs based on identified risks, and form the basis of policy-update recommendations and updates to compliance and risk management policies based on recommended best practice. (p. 73)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "NRA outputs also form the basis of recommendations for policy updates"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Consultation Q4 (designing NRA process and outputs for utility): the private sector recommends designing the process to encourage close collaboration with all stakeholders from the outset, enabling clear and specific compliance guidelines. All stakeholders identified investing in capacity-building and practical risk management tools and resources, and all called for transparency and access to NRA data and results to facilitate research and better understanding of risks. (p. 74)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The private sector recommends that countries design the NRA process in such a way as to encourage close collaboration"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Consultation Q5 (how NRA findings improved stakeholders' own risk management): responses were largely consistent with Q3. For a majority, findings were used to update compliance policies, aligning practices with the latest risk management recommendations, and to apply NRA findings strictly in internal risk assessments. (p. 74)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to update compliance policies by ensuring that practices are aligned with the latest risk management recommendations"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q5 responses also reported that NRA findings led to strengthened internal controls and audit procedures, with adoption of best practices recommended by the NRA and implementation of training programs based on identified risks as recurring points. (p. 74)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "NRA findings have also led to the strengthening of internal controls and audit procedures"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Consultation Q6 (best mechanism for stakeholder feedback on the NRA): stakeholders recommend dedicated online portals for submitting feedback and regular consultation meetings, and encouraged publication of relevant information and training for all those involved in the NRA. (p. 75)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Stakeholders recommend the creation of dedicated online portals for submitting feedback and the organization of regular consultation meetings."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The document (updated August 2025) states that to establish a strong national AML framework countries must prioritise a deep understanding of ML risks, and that the guidance supports countries in conducting a money laundering NRA, drawing on insights from over 90 countries within the FATF Global Network.[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "drawing on insights from over 90 countries within the FATF Global Network"</sub>

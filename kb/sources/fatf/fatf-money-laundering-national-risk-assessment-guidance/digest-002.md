## Section 1: NRA Preparation and Set-up

- **descriptive / context**: The FATF Standards require countries to identify, assess and understand ML risks at country level by designating an authority or mechanism to coordinate risk assessment actions and apply resources so that risks are effectively mitigated. The FATF allows flexibility based on context and does not require the creation of a permanent body to develop the NRA. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "does not require the creation of a permanent body to develop the NRA"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Whatever mechanism is chosen should promote good cooperation, coordination and participation of a wide range of national authorities to secure a comprehensive risk assessment. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "However, any chosen mechanism should promote good cooperation, coordination and participation of a wide range of national authorities"</sub>

## Table 1. Factors to Consider: Permanent and Temporary Mechanism

- **descriptive / context**: Table 1 lists benefits of a permanent NRA mechanism: it promotes consistency and replicability across iterations, fosters inter-agency coordination through longer-term relationships and information sharing, lets participants build experience in the NRA process and methodology, and builds institutional memory and expertise. Its drawback is ongoing overhead and cost. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Promotes consistency and replicability across different iterations."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Table 1 lists benefits of a temporary NRA mechanism: more flexibility in risk-based resource allocation (especially important for lower-capacity countries), maintaining existing good inter-agency coordination without a permanent body, and bringing in other perspectives so the process does not stagnate. A drawback is possible loss of expertise and disconnect with past risk assessments, especially if agency staff turnover is high. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Can result in loss of expertise and disconnect with past risk assessments"</sub>

## Box 2. Mixture of Permanent and Temporary

- **descriptive / case example**: In Italy, the Financial Security Committee (FSC), made up of representatives of all relevant public authorities and administrations, maintains both a permanent coordination mechanism (for ongoing information exchange and monitoring) and a temporary dedicated team led by the Ministry of Economy and Finance that conducts risk assessments during NRA updates, combining benefits of both approaches. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "maintains both a permanent coordination mechanism and a temporary dedicated team led by the Ministry of Economy and Finance"</sub>

## An Inclusive National Mechanism to Assess Risk

- **descriptive / context**: Countries may choose a top-down or bottom-up approach to the risk assessment, but a combination of the two has often been observed as the most effective way to develop a comprehensive NRA. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Countries may choose to take a top-down or bottom-up approach to conducting the risk assessment"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In a top-down approach, the assessment starts from a high-level perspective and identifies broad categories of factors affecting national ML risk; it should inform high-level strategies and actions such as budget allocation. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In a top-down approach, risk assessment begins with a high-level perspective"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A bottom-up approach relies more on industry-level, sectoral and thematic risk assessment findings combined with the experience of subject matter experts and operational staff of competent authorities. It can help identify the likelihood of risk events, support operational-level changes to procedures and controls, and identify sector-specific vulnerabilities. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A bottom-up approach relies more on industry level, sectoral and thematic risk assessment findings"</sub>

## NRA Leadership

- **expected / expectation**: Whatever approach is taken, countries should establish who will lead the NRA process, whether one agency or working group or multiple agencies, and should invest in finding leaders with the necessary expertise in the FATF Standards, ML risk and the risk assessment process. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Irrespective of the approach taken, countries should establish who will lead the process"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Most countries responding to the questionnaire named their National AML/CFT Committee (or a working group subset) as the dedicated coordination authority for risk assessment; some cited the governing ministry or agency, such as the FIU, Ministry of Finance or Ministry of Justice. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identified their National AML/CFT Committee (or working group sub-set) as being the dedicated coordination authority"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Where several agencies lead the NRA, the arrangement requires strong coordination between agencies and a clear designation of roles and responsibilities, including procedures for decision making. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This requires strong coordination between agencies and a clear designation of roles and responsibilities"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: NRA leaders need a cross-AML-regime perspective, the ability to work with different competent authorities and the private sector, and adequate seniority to inform senior policy makers of the process and its outcomes. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should have adequate seniority to inform senior policy makers of the process and its outcomes"</sub>

## Involvement of all Relevant National Stakeholders

- **expected / expectation**: A comprehensive ML risk assessment will require involvement from key authorities across operational, policy and supervisory functions. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A comprehensive assessment of ML risks will require involvement from key authorities across operational, policy and supervisory functions."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Key authorities include intelligence and security agencies, police, border security, prosecution authorities, the FIU, anti-drug and anti-corruption authorities, customs, supervisory and regulatory authorities and foreign counterparts. Regional authorities may be involved depending on country size, and other bodies such as tax authorities, statistics departments and trade and commerce regulators may hold relevant information. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Some key authorities include intelligence and security agencies, police, border security, prosecution authorities, the FIU"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Depending on the country context, involvement of financial inclusion policy makers should also be considered. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Depending on the country context, involvement of financial inclusion policy makers should also be considered."</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Several countries have set up an interdepartmental, multi-agency NRA Committee to coordinate and integrate all relevant national, sectoral and industry risk assessments. It could be beneficial to establish a defined decision-making forum with clear terms of reference setting out the roles and responsibilities of lead and participating agencies, acting as a central body overseeing the NRA process. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it could be beneficial for a country to establish a defined decision-making forum, which has clear terms of reference"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The decision-making forum should set out the decision-making hierarchy for various types of decisions, specifying which require escalation for consensus-building or to address divergent views. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the forum should delineate the decision-making hierarchy for various types of decisions"</sub>

## Involvement of Non-governmental Stakeholders

- **recommended / recommendation**: Although an ML risk assessment is an overall government assessment, countries should be encouraged to involve non-governmental stakeholders with subject matter expertise where appropriate, including FIs, DNFBPs, VASPs, civil society and academia. Some countries also involve risk-assessment experts from outside the AML world and scientific or data experts. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries should be encouraged to involve other non-governmental stakeholders with subject matter expertise, where appropriate"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Non-governmental stakeholders can supplement or validate information from law enforcement and supervisors about what occurs in practice, add private sector expertise on transactions, legitimate use and misuse of products and services, industry practices and emerging threats, and help avoid over- or under-generalisation of risk and unintended consequences. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Involving non-governmental stakeholders can supplement or validate information from LEA and supervisors"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Non-governmental stakeholders can be engaged at any stage; involving FIs, DNFBPs, VASPs and civil society early and continuously can make the product more impactful, and strong working relationships and trust support honest feedback and willingness to share information. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Involving FIs, DNFBPs, VASPs, and civil society early in the process and on a continuous basis"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Engaging a representative sample of each sector (different services and entity sizes), including through the relevant supervisor, is important. Outreach could be tailored to the size and maturity of each sector, and countries could work with non-AML industry bodies to reassure firms that responses will not be used against them in supervisory assessments. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Engaging with a representative sample from the sector"</sub>
- **recommended / method**: Countries may seek institutional risk assessments or other material from reporting entities, or send surveys or questionnaires; using a standardised template to facilitate analysis is a good practice. Input can also come through interviews, roundtables or meetings, and public-private partnerships or other private sector forums. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is a good practice to have a standardised template to facilitate the analysis of the information received."</sub>

## Box 3. Involvement of Private Sector in NRA

- **descriptive / case example**: In the United Kingdom, between NRAs public-private threat groups on priority areas produce public-private threat alerts. When the NRA formally begins, questionnaires on how risks have changed since the last NRA go to regulated-sector firms via industry bodies rather than AML supervisors, so firms can respond honestly without risk of supervisory intervention. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These are circulated via industry bodies, not AML supervisors, to give firms confidence"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The UK also holds NRA workshops with each sector, inviting a diverse group of firms (for example differing in size and sub-sector) to discuss key themes from questionnaire responses, including where private sector views diverge from those of supervisors or law enforcement. Questionnaire and workshop summaries are among the data sources used to draft NRA findings. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Private sector contribution is also sought through NRA workshops with each sector."</sub>

## Ensuring the NRA Reflects Current Risks

- **expected / requirement**: The FATF Standards require countries to maintain an up-to-date understanding of risks, allowing flexibility in how and how often updates occur, in line with risks, context and capacity. Countries should take an RBA to the schedule and scope of updates and can prioritise high-risk themes or sectors for targeted updates. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF Standards require countries to maintain an up-to-date understanding of risks"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Updates should be communicated in the same way as the NRA to ensure a comprehensive and up-to-date understanding among stakeholders. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Updates should be communicated in the same way as the NRA"</sub>

## Involvement of Non-governmental Stakeholders

- **recommended / recommendation**: Although an ML risk assessment is an overall government assessment, countries should be encouraged to involve non-governmental stakeholders with subject matter expertise, where appropriate. Such stakeholders may include, but are not limited to, FIs, DNFBPs, VASPs, civil society and academia. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries should be encouraged to involve other non-governmental stakeholders with subject matter expertise, where appropriate"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Some countries also bring in experts on risk assessment from outside the AML world, and scientific or data experts, to strengthen their approach to the NRA. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Some countries also include experts in risk assessment from outside of the AML world"</sub>

## Ensuring the NRA Reflects Current Risks

- **expected / expectation**: The NRA should not be regarded as a static document but as a continuous process; the formal NRA document is a snapshot of the country's risk situation at a specific time. Countries should manage the NRA as a dynamic process, constantly identifying emerging threats, managing residual risks and assessing the effectiveness of mitigation measures. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The NRA should not be regarded simply as a static document, but as a continuous process."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: There is no requirement to use data collected over a particular length of time, but data should span a sufficient period and be current enough to give an up-to-date, accurate and comprehensive view of risks, which varies with how often the country updates its NRA and conducts targeted updates. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is no requirement for countries to use data collected over a particular length of time in their NRA"</sub>

## Box 4. Statistics on Timeliness of NRAs

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The majority of responding countries (53%) update their NRA every 2-3 years and 27% update when the risk landscape changes. The World Bank found the average time between NRA updates in the countries it studied was 3.5 years. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "update their NRA every 2-3 years, and 27% update"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The World Bank found that the average length of time between NRA updates in the countries studied was 3.5 years. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the average length of time between NRA updates in the countries they studied was 3.5 years"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Some countries have legislation setting how often the risk assessment must be updated, often with a supplementary requirement to update if the risk landscape changes. The key is a mechanism allowing an agile approach with quick adjustments, plus ongoing national discussion of risk outside any legislated schedule, since risk assessment is a continuous process. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Some countries have legislation dictating the frequency at which they must update their risk assessment"</sub>

## Box 5. Statistics on Timeliness of NRAs

- **descriptive / finding or result**: World Bank research found that completing the NRA too close to the mutual evaluation onsite visit can limit the time available to introduce risk-based policies. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "completing the NRA too close to the onsite visit for the ME can limit the time available"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In the World Bank study, of 26 countries where the gap between completing the NRA and the onsite visit was three months or less, 17 had deficiencies in their risk-based national policies and strategies, stemming from insufficient time to implement updated risk-based mitigation measures. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the 26 countries where this time span between completing the NRA and the onsite visit is three months or less"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The timing of the NRA and the stage of implementation of the risk mitigating measures based on its findings materially affect the effectiveness of a country's AML framework. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The timing of the NRA and stage of the implementation of the risk mitigating measures"</sub>

## Scoping and Objective Setting

- **expected / expectation**: All parties involved should agree on the purpose and scope of the NRA before it begins, clarifying why it is conducted, the questions it should answer, the criteria used to answer them and the decisions it will inform. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "All parties involved in the NRA process should agree on the purpose and scope of the NRA"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Countries should establish the target audience(s) early in the process and consider that ML risk assessments should serve as input to national AML strategy or strategies as part of domestic AML coordination. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Countries should establish the target audience(s) early in the process"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The NRA scope should be clearly defined and referred to throughout, and should cover all relevant sectors, geographic areas and illicit activities in line with the objectives. Scoping also involves identifying key stakeholders and appropriate methodologies and data sources, and focuses resources efficiently. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The scope should be clearly defined and referred to throughout the process"</sub>

## Actions to Take Before Starting NRA

- **recommended / recommendation**: For countries with existing NRAs, a first step is to analyse the previous NRA and other risk assessment materials, either immediately after publication or as a handover note for future coordinators, to make future NRAs more robust. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "For countries with existing NRAs, a first step is to analyse the previous NRA and other risk assessment materials"</sub>
- **recommended / method**: On process, the review of the previous NRA should ask whether methodology and scope were appropriate, whether data collection issues can be resolved, whether all relevant authorities and stakeholders were involved, whether there were difficulties building private sector risk understanding or selecting and implementing mitigation measures, and whether further alignment with national AML strategies is needed. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Were the methodology and scope appropriate?"</sub>
- **recommended / method**: On content and findings, the review should ask whether the risk landscape has changed with new threats and vulnerabilities, which vulnerable areas have seen risk mitigating measures introduced, and what data was lacking that could help draw conclusions on risk. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Has the risk landscape changed and are there new threats and vulnerabilities to include?"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: A good practice is to do a stocktake of available information and a gap analysis to see where data may be lacking, and stakeholder mapping is useful for obtaining national and international data; it is advisable to do this on an ongoing basis. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A good practice is to do a stocktake of available information and a gap analysis"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Steps that countries reported as yielding positive results include studying NRAs of neighbouring countries or supranational risk assessments, consulting experts from countries rated highly or substantially effective on IO.1 in their MER, and consulting reports from international organisations, news media and civil society, especially to identify new and emerging threats. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Consulted experts from other countries that were rated highly or substantially effective on IO.1 in their MER."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: To keep the NRA on track, countries should create a project plan with timelines, key milestones and deadlines; base timelines on actual time spent in previous exercises; allocate tasks to specific people or departments; and have an ongoing project monitoring mechanism. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries should create a project plan with timelines, key milestones and deadlines throughout the process"</sub>

## Different Types of Risk Assessments and Their Interactions

- **descriptive / context**: The NRA should give a baseline understanding of ML risks and help identify policy focus areas. Countries can supplement it with sectoral and thematic risk assessments or other research (such as FIU strategic analysis, law enforcement or intelligence threat actor assessments, IFI and research institute work, and supervisory studies), which can take fewer resources, allow a deep dive into sectors or themes, and support a progressive approach in resource-limited situations. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Countries can also conduct sectoral risk assessments and thematic risk assessments"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Bodies such as the EU and FSRBs have conducted supranational risk assessments across multiple countries, which can help with region-specific and cross-border risks. Larger jurisdictions or those with overseas territories may conduct sub-national risk assessments, useful for border regions or areas with characteristics making them more vulnerable to ML. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Some international bodies such as the European Union (EU) and FSRBs have conducted supranational risk assessments"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Countries should also consider findings of institutional-level risk assessments by FIs and DNFBPs and other risk-related work by national authorities, such as trends and typologies reports. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Countries should also consider the findings of risk assessment work done on an institutional level by FIs and DNFBPs"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Countries should aim for complementary risk assessment activities, taking an RBA by prioritising highest-risk areas for sectoral or thematic assessments, and conduct them as part of the broader AML strategy because they become less beneficial when done in isolation. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "They should also take an RBA, i.e., by prioritising highest-risk areas for sectoral/thematic risk assessments."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: To incorporate other assessments into the NRA, countries should standardise methodologies and risk ratings so assessments are comparable and easily integrated. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Countries should take steps to standardise methodologies and risk ratings"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Compiling and integrating data from the various risk assessments into a centralised national-level database gives a holistic view of ML data across sectors, themes, countries and levels of administration. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Compiling and integrating data collected in the development of the various risk assessments into a centralised, national level database"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Cross-referencing risks: using a variety of risk assessments involving different stakeholders lets authorities cross-check their understanding of risk and address discrepancies, for example checking whether industry-level findings correspond with government sectoral findings and, if not, investigating why (such as lack of private sector involvement or a change in the risk landscape). (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Doing a variety of risk assessments which involve different stakeholders allows the authorities to cross-check their understanding of risk"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Keeping sensitive information confidential throughout the NRA process is important for maintaining stakeholder trust. To address confidentiality concerns about involving non-governmental stakeholders, countries can develop legal frameworks and procedures on use and transfer of sensitive information and/or require participants to sign non-disclosure agreements. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Ensuring that sensitive information is kept confidential throughout the NRA process is important"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Countries should have systems in place to protect NRA data from unauthorised access, for example encrypted databases and communication channels. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Countries should also have systems in place to protect data from unauthorised access"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Where there is concern that non-governmental stakeholders may downplay risk or vulnerabilities to obtain lower levels of regulation, it is important to contextualise their information with other sources such as law enforcement, supervisors, intelligence information and the FIU. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is important to contextualise the information that is provided with other sources of information"</sub>

## Collection of Information and Data

- **descriptive / context**: Data collection plays a role in every step of the NRA process and forms the foundation for analysis; methods depend on country size, quality of national coordination and information-sharing mechanisms, and capacity and resources dedicated. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Data collection plays a role in every step of the NRA process"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Quantitative data is numerical, measurable information, such as statistics on STRs received and disseminated, investigations, prosecutions, convictions, confiscated proceeds, offenders and supervisory enforcement. It provides a good evidence base and ability to monitor trends but lacks the context needed to interpret underlying causes and implications. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Quantitative data refers to numerical, measurable information that can be expressed in terms of quantities or amounts."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Qualitative data is non-numerical, descriptive information, such as interviews with law enforcement, supervisors and supervised entities, case studies, expert opinions, observations, and existing typologies or trend analysis. It gives insight into stakeholders' perceptions and experiences, helps identify gaps and weaknesses, and can explain trends in quantitative data. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Qualitative data refers to non-numerical, descriptive information."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Both quantitative and qualitative data should be considered for an accurate picture of risk. For example, if a sector has reported no STRs it may appear low risk, but qualitative information may show it does not understand reporting obligations or has only recently begun reporting, so risk may be higher than the quantitative data suggests. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "if no STRs have been reported by a sector it may appear as though that sector is low risk"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: As data comes from many sources, it is recommended that overlapping information be cross-checked to create the most accurate picture possible. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is recommended that information that overlaps is cross-checked to create the most accurate picture possible"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Countries should consider their level of confidence in the reliability of each data source and avoid taking data at face value, which can help establish potential data gaps. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Countries should also consider the level of confidence they have in the reliability of each data source"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Countries should ensure data are comparable and uniform over time periods, for example always using calendar year rather than fiscal year. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Countries should ensure that the data they use is comparable and uniform over time periods"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: A lack of data does not necessarily mean a lack of risk and could indicate heightened risk if it reflects a vulnerability within the country's AML/CFT framework. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A lack of data does not necessarily equate to a lack of risk"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Countries should track data gaps and procedural issues and improve them with each NRA. Developing a robust data collection process takes time and should not be rushed, as that could result in poor quality and misleading data. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Developing a robust data collection process takes time and should not be rushed"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Eighty percent of countries responding to the project questionnaires reported some challenges with data collection in the NRA process. Investing in a consistent, ongoing approach can save resources over time as the process gets easier and quicker with each iteration. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Eighty percent of countries that responded to the project questionnaires indicated that they had experienced some challenges with data collection"</sub>

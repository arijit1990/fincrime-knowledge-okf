## INTRODUCTION

- **descriptive / data or feature**: EUROPOL has released an annual public regional terrorism threat assessment since 2007, drawing on member states and non-EU countries. It covers jihadist, ethno-nationalist and separatist, and right and left-wing terrorism and uses information such as failed, foiled and completed attacks, arrests, convictions and penalties (Eurojust), EUROPOL counter-terrorism activities, outgoing and returning FTFs, social media use for terrorism purposes, and legislative amendments across EU member states. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Since 2007 EUROPOL has been releasing an annual public assessment of regional terrorism threats"</sub>

## Considerations for jurisdictions with no or very few known (or suspected) terrorism or TF cases

- **descriptive / expectation**: It is important that countries assess and continue to monitor TF risks regardless of the absence of known threats. Absence of known or suspected terrorism and TF cases does not necessarily mean low TF risk, and does not eliminate the potential for funds or other assets to be raised and used domestically (for a purpose other than a terrorist attack) or transferred abroad. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The absence of known or suspected terrorism and TF cases does not necessarily mean that a jurisdiction"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Jurisdictions may rely on techniques such as scenario building (likelihood and credibility of common TF typologies for the domestic context) and structured interviews and focus groups with domestic or regional operational experts. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "scenario building (i.e. likelihood and credibility of common TF typologies for domestic context)"</sub>

## Box 2.4. Assessing and managing low TF risk - the Papua New Guinea (PNG) and Vanuatu experience

- **descriptive / case example**: PNG's 2017 ML/TF NRA found the likelihood of TF low because of absence of domestic terrorist activity and supportive communities, but recognised that this could change rapidly and that even a small amount of terrorism funding could have potentially catastrophic consequences, necessitating vigilance and a strategy. PNG's major TF threat is being used as a conduit for funds, given its location between high TF risk jurisdictions. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the likelihood of TF in PNG is low due to absence of domestic terrorist activity"</sub>

## Considerations for jurisdictions with no or very few known (or suspected) terrorism or TF cases

- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 34: a jurisdiction with no TF or terrorism cases still needs to weigh how likely it is that terrorist funds are raised domestically (including via donors who give willingly or are deceived), that funds and assets pass through or leave the country to support terrorism, and that funds are used for purposes other than a domestic attack. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Jurisdictions without TF and terrorism cases will still need to consider the likelihood of terrorist funds being raised domestically"</sub>

## Box 2.4. Assessing and managing low TF risk - the Papua New Guinea (PNG) and Vanuatu experience

- **descriptive / case example**: Vanuatu's 2017 TF risk assessment found no evidence that Vanuatu has been a source or conduit of TF in domestic or offshore sectors, but recognised that absence of domestic terrorism does not mean its financial system cannot be used to raise funds abroad or transfer funds between jurisdictions, particularly given its role as an Offshore Financial Centre where terrorist financiers could use international companies, legal arrangements and other structures; there was no evidence offshore entities had been used for TF. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "absence of domestic terrorism does not mean that Vanuatu’s financial system and institutions cannot be used"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: To test CTF systems despite low identified risk, both countries adopted comprehensive standard operating procedures (SOPs) and ran practical training exercises applying legislation and SOPs to hypothetical scenarios. Vanuatu held a hypothetical desktop exercise where officers from relevant agencies worked through hypothetical cases, including relevant processes, forms and templates; PNG intends to run a similar exercise. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "both countries adopted comprehensive standard operating procedures (SOPs) and conducted practical training exercises"</sub>

## TF Vulnerabilities

- **descriptive / expectation**: Assessment of TF vulnerabilities is inherently linked to a jurisdiction's context and identified TF threats, but all countries will need a comprehensive TF vulnerability assessment regardless of the nature of identified threats, because TF threats may exploit loopholes in the domestic CFT regime at any point; the assessment may draw to some extent on known ML vulnerabilities. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "all countries will need to carry out a comprehensive assessment of their TF vulnerabilities regardless of the nature"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Financial and non-financial supervisors and non-government stakeholders will be important participants in assessing TF vulnerabilities because they can offer a unique perspective on how products and services may be exploited. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "can offer a unique perspective on how certain products and services may be exploited"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Jurisdictions would typically consider structural elements as TF vulnerability factors, e.g. rule of law, national counter terrorism and TF strategies and activities (including de-radicalisation/extremism prevention programmes), and relationships with regional partners. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Structural elements (e.g. rule of law, national counter terrorism and TF strategies and activities"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Materiality factors typically considered: the extent to which the jurisdiction is a financial or trade hub, relative importance of parts of the economy, how cash-based or unregulated the economy is, the importance of sectors highlighted in international typologies as higher risk for TF, and cultural links and demographics. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "extent to which the economy is cash based/unregulated"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Other vulnerability factors typically considered: compliance with FATF Standards relevant to ML/TF (TF offence and targeted financial sanctions legal framework, preventive measures, cross-border controls, LEA powers and expertise, TF information exchange), and effectiveness of the AML/CFT regime (authorities' capacity to identify and prevent TF, effectiveness of TF-related STR reporting, monitoring and analysis, quality of intelligence, international CFT cooperation, human resources, and timely access to beneficial ownership information). (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Compliance with FATF Standards relevant to ML/TF (e.g. legal framework linked to TF offence and Targeted Financial Sanctions (TFS)"</sub>

## Box 2.5. Belgium 2018 National TF Risk Assessment – Relationship between TF threats and vulnerabilities

- **descriptive / case example**: In Belgium's TF NRA one identified threat was use of cash in preparing terrorist attacks, so the vulnerability assessment examined measures to supervise cross-border or domestic cash transportation and to limit cash use. Another threat was use of social benefit allowances to finance terrorists, so it examined measures to stop illicit or unlawful payments of such allowances to Belgian FTFs; vulnerability identification went beyond weaknesses in sectors subject to AML/CFT measures (reporting entities). (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the use of social benefit allowances to finance terrorists or a terrorist attack"</sub>

## TF Vulnerabilities

- **expected / data or feature**: When assessing sector or product-specific TF vulnerabilities, the guidance says the assessment should include consideration of how far products or services have featured in known domestic or international typologies, how aware of TF and compliant the sectors are, and how complex and far-reaching money movement is in sub-sectors or firms that may carry higher TF risk. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the extent to which products or services have been misused in known domestic or international typologies"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: A footnote observes that terrorists are known to have misused public services to raise money for travel to take part in terrorist acts, so jurisdictions may also need to consider TF vulnerabilities of public services. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions may also need to consider TF vulnerabilities of public services"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Depending on its profile, a jurisdiction may need to collect further information to assess vulnerabilities. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "certain jurisdictions may need to collect further information in order to assess vulnerabilities"</sub>

## Jurisdiction is a global or regional financial or trade centre

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: International finance and trade centres may be vulnerable to misuse for moving or managing funds or assets linked to terrorist activity because of the high volume and cross-border nature of assets. Cases show terrorist organisations misusing land, sea and air trade to move funds or other assets (e.g. weapons or vehicles) using techniques such as under/over-invoicing or falsification of trade documents, which can be hard to identify as terrorists rely on complex legal structures to hide the beneficial owner. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Common techniques include: under/over-invoicing, or falsification of trade documents"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Financial and trade centres may also consider the extent of trade linkages with such jurisdictions and intelligence and open source information on links between the trade centre and funds or assets (including cargo) linked to designated individuals or entities and associates; and the extent of business relationships and one-off transactions with parties in or linked to target jurisdictions (including FIs' role as service provider or correspondents for customers or FIs in high-risk jurisdictions) and their characteristics. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the extent of trade linkages with jurisdictions with active terrorist organisations and/or conflict zones"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: They may further consider the extent to which financial or administration services are provided from the centre for import/export or other trading activity usable for terrorism; the extent of use by foreign PEPs (given the possible link with state-sponsored terrorism); levels of TF awareness and trade expertise in the private and public sectors and ability to detect suspicious behaviour; and anomalies in data (e.g. differences between reported exports from jurisdiction A to B and reported imports by B from A). (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the extent to which the financial or trade center is used by foreign PEPs"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Paragraph 40: to spot potential TF vulnerabilities, financial and trade centres may consider how extensive their financial links are with jurisdictions hosting active terrorist organisations and/or conflict zones, for example using fund inflow/outflow data by jurisdiction, including, when available, data on bank deposits, correspondent banking, investments, ATM withdrawals abroad, incoming and outgoing wire transfers, and prepaid card loads and spends. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the extent of financial linkages with jurisdictions with active terrorist organisations and/or conflict zones"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Assessing TF risk within the trade sector will often require a multi-agency approach with inputs from customs, police, the FIU, tax authorities, public registrars and intelligence agencies; experience highlights the importance of international trade expertise and adequate IT tools to handle complex, large volumes of trade data. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "will often require a multi-agency approach with inputs required from customs authorities, the police"</sub>

## Jurisdiction has a large informal or cash based economy and/or limited state infrastructure

- **descriptive / finding or result**: A number of FATF reports identify cash as a common means by which terrorist financiers raise, move and use funds (including physical transportation by FTFs). Lower capacity jurisdictions with large cash-based economies and informal/unregulated activities can therefore face additional TF vulnerabilities. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "use of cash as a common means through which terrorist financiers raise, move and use funds"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: When assessing TF risks in the informal sector, countries may rely on credible research on the scale and scope of the informal sector, interviews with specific communities, other intelligence gathering (including undercover operations), and information on porous borders and identified smuggling networks. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "findings from credible research studies on the scale and scope of the jurisdiction’s informal sector"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: For jurisdictions with isolated communities due to limited infrastructure or government presence, it is vital for competent authorities to engage and seek support from such communities to assess and combat TF risk; country experience highlights that engaging via a trusted third party may facilitate more open dialogue. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is vital for competent authorities to engage and seek support from such communities"</sub>

## Jurisdiction is within close geographic proximity to an active terrorist threat

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Jurisdictions bordering a conflict zone or close to jurisdictions with active terrorist organisations often face additional cross-border TF threats; cases show TF facilitators in neighbouring jurisdictions helping transport funds and goods (including FTFs) into or out of conflict zones. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Jurisdictions bordering a conflict zone or within close proximity to jurisdictions with active terrorist organisations"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: For such jurisdictions, a TF risk assessment will likely consider the strength of border controls, customs capacity to identify smuggling linked to TF, the general criminal environment at the border (e.g. smuggling networks), TF awareness and compliance of local FIs, DNFBPs or other vulnerable sectors, and the extent of engagement and information sharing with neighbouring authorities. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "strength of border controls, capacity of customs authorities to identify smuggling linked to TF"</sub>

## Box 2.7. Lebanon: 2019 Terrorist Financing Risk Assessment

- **descriptive / case example**: Lebanon's 2019 ML/TF NRA, building on its 2014 NRA, was led by its FIU (the Special Investigation Commission) with input from private sector stakeholders and the two national AML and CFT committees, backed by high-level political commitment, using qualitative and quantitative data. Vulnerabilities assessed went beyond sector and product misuse to PESTEL factors (Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental/Geographical and Legislative), including law enforcement resources, large refugee populations with ties to jurisdictions witnessing terrorism/instability, proximity to the ISIL conflict, and ISIL and Al-Nusra Front elements entrenched in local mountainous border areas. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental/Geographical & Legislative factors (PESTEL)"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Lebanon reviewed cases handled (STRs, requests for assistance, spontaneous disclosures, TF typologies including NPO misuse). A review of TF convictions revealed cash was primarily used and that cross-border smuggling cases did occur. Input came from multiple law enforcement agencies, not just customs, since border monitoring involved other authorities including the army, and expert law enforcement opinions on FTFs who left Lebanon and returnees were factored in. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A review of TF convictions revealed that cash was primarily used and that cases of cross-border smuggling did occur."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Lebanon identified cross-border TF risks arising from terrorist groups targeting Lebanon from strongholds abroad, at times enabled by sympathisers and extremists taking refuge or cover in large refugee communities. Its TF risk mitigation measures were found commensurate with risk, with room for additional measures that were commissioned. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "sympathizers and extremists taking refuge or cover within the large refugees communities"</sub>

## Jurisdiction with strong communal links to active terrorist zones

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Terrorist financiers have been known to use local diaspora communities, ethnic links and family ties to raise and move funds and other assets. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "utilise local diaspora communities, ethnic links and family ties to raise and move funds and other assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Jurisdictions with strong communal links to active terrorist areas will typically consider the potential for sympathetic views among local community members (e.g. open source information and intelligence on radicalisation), the level of economic activity flowing to and from the community and regions of active terrorist activity (e.g. family support remittances), and may consider the prevalence of NPOs targeting local ethnic communities for donations to such regions. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the level of economic activity flowing to and from the local community and regions of active terrorist activity"</sub>

## Analysis of Terrorist Financing Threats and Vulnerabilities

- **descriptive / method**: After identifying TF threats and vulnerabilities, the next step is to consider how they interact to form risks, including how threats may exploit vulnerabilities based on known cases or typologies. Approaches include hypothetical TF risk events (Annex C) and/or combined quantitative and qualitative judgement based on experience. TF risk events are hypothetical scenarios derived from identified threats, vulnerabilities and consequences that may help prioritise between risks. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the next step is to consider how these interact to form risks"</sub>

## Sources, channels, direction and use of terrorist funds and other assets

- **descriptive / method**: The analysis stage is likely to consider the different sources, channels, destinations and origins of terrorist funds and assets, as TF assessment should holistically cover raising, moving and using funds and other assets. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a holistic consideration of all stages of TF: raising, moving, and using funds and other assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Direction/use of funds: funds or assets may be generated in the home jurisdiction but used for operations elsewhere or vice versa, or may transit the jurisdiction. It is important that jurisdictions establish the direction and use of terrorism-related fund and asset flows, as this is relevant to determining which controls need to be adopted or strengthened. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Fund or other assets might be generated by terrorist financiers in the home jurisdiction, but used by terrorists"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Sources of TF are likely to differ between terrorist actors (e.g. funding sources for FTFs likely differ from those for large terrorist organisations); considering different sources enables countries to identify where mitigation controls need to be placed. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the value and sources of funding for foreign terrorist fighters is likely to differ from the sources used"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Channels: terrorist financiers use different channels to move funds and assets, including banking, money service business (MSB) sector, cash smuggling and informal remittances. It is important to consider which channels may be higher risk for TF in order to identify the severity of vulnerabilities of specific sectors or products. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Terrorist financiers use different channels to move funds and assets, including through the banking sector"</sub>

## Likelihood and consequences

- **descriptive / method**: In the analysis stage it is important to prioritise between identified risks, which may consider likelihood and consequences of specific TF risks. Likelihood and consequence may be assessed or scored with descriptive words or number scales; the key is to use these concepts to differentiate risk levels of diverse types of TF and help prioritise mitigation. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Likelihood and consequence may be assessed or scored using descriptive words or number scales."</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Given the challenges in calculating consequences, jurisdictions need not take a scientific approach and may want to start with the presumption that TF consequences will be severe (domestically or elsewhere) and consider whether any factors would alter that conclusion. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions need not take a scientific approach, and instead may want to start with the presumption"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Consequences of TF may differ by source/channel or intended recipient; channels enabling high volumes of funds may have higher consequences, but given the relatively low volume of funds needed to launch an attack, jurisdictions need to be careful when determining TF consequence on the basis of volumes alone. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions need to be careful when determining the consequence of TF on the basis of volumes alone"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Consequences for many TF risks are likely to be more severe than for ML or other financial crime (e.g. tax fraud); considering consequences helps jurisdictions identify the resources to dedicate and the mitigating measures to implement. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Consequences for many TF risks are likely to be more severe than for ML or other types of financial crime"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Paragraph 48: jurisdictions usually judge likelihood by looking at how common known cases are, intelligence and typologies, the capabilities and intent of terrorist organisations, individuals and supporters, and how strong CFT controls are. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Jurisdictions typically assess likelihood by considering: the prevalence of known cases, intelligence, and typologies"</sub>

## Box 2.8. Argentina's 2017-2019 TF-P NRA – Scoring TF Risks

- **descriptive / case example**: Argentina's AML/CTF National Coordination (NC) developed two formulas and matrices to score risks of generation of funds and of placement, movement and use of funds, assessing likelihood and harm/consequences. Lacking domestic TF cases to score likelihood, the NC developed an 'overall likelihood' combining a 'local incidence sub-score' with a 'global prominence sub-score', which checks against under-rating risks that evaded local detection or under-weighting emerging risks. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "combines a “local incidence sub-score” with a “global prominence sub-score”"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Because harm from terrorism and TF is always high, Argentina developed a 'marginal harm' concept (harm beyond the common base level of physical harm). As recent attacks show funds raised do not directly correlate to harm inflicted, the marginal harm score has no sub-score for amount of funds raised, instead drawing on the FATF's Crime and Terrorism Harm Framework and assessing social, environmental, financial or economic, and institutional or structural harms. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the amount of funds raised does not directly correlate to the extent of harm inflicted"</sub>

## Evaluation

- **descriptive / method**: Good approaches highlight the particular importance of qualitative judgement and intelligence sources in reaching conclusions on relative TF risk levels, and a common understanding of risk levels is important for prioritising mitigation. Countries facing disparate risks across regions or sectors may need to evaluate TF risk at regional or sector level. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the particular importance of using qualitative judgment and intelligence sources in reaching conclusions"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: Experience highlights the benefits of validating findings with government and non-government stakeholders to avoid confirmation bias and group think, and acknowledging uncertainties or gaps in available information where applicable. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "validating findings with different government and non-government stakeholders to avoid confirmation bias and group think"</sub>

## Box 2.9. Malaysia's 2017 National TF Risk Assessment – Evaluating TF Risk

- **descriptive / case example**: Malaysia's third-iteration 2017 NRA was completed and endorsed by the National Coordination Committee to Counter Money Laundering (NCC) in July 2018. In evaluation, subject matter experts from public and private sectors (current and retired law enforcement and government officials, supervisors, regulators, academics, NPO representatives, journalists) gave feedback in focus groups on a preliminary risk rating based primarily on quantitative inputs such as number of TF cases; the NCC Working Group moderated the risks, compared the preliminary rating with the qualitative assessment for the final rating, and the NCC then deliberated and endorsed it. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "subject matter experts were engaged in focus group discussions to provide feedback on the preliminary risk rating"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Malaysia's assessment found inherent TF risk had increased from medium to medium high since 2013 due to increasing threats from the Islamic State (IS) and foreign terrorist fighters in Malaysia and the ASEAN region, but that these threats had been largely contained by authorities with considerable capacity to identify and combat TF domestically. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the inherent risk had increased from medium to medium high since 2013"</sub>

## PART 3: ASSESSING CROSS-BORDER AND SECTOR-SPECIFIC TERRORIST FINANCING RISKS

- **descriptive / context**: An assessment of TF risk requires consideration of both cross-border risks and risks to specific sectors; how far sector risks need to be assessed depends partly on materiality, TF awareness and geographic reach of sectors. The guidance presents information sources for cross-border TF risks, banking and MVTS sector TF risks, and TF linked to exploitation of natural resources, selected for their prevalence in international typologies, though areas of increased focus differ between jurisdictions. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The extent to which countries will need to assess the TF risks posed to specific sectors will depend"</sub>

## Cross-border Terrorist Financing Risks

- **descriptive / context**: Cross-border TF risk assessment is typically integrated throughout the TF risk assessment rather than a standalone exercise. Cross-border TF risks may relate to physical transportation of funds or assets into or out of a country to support terrorism (e.g. cross-border smuggling), flows of funds or goods through the financial or trade sector, or cross-border provision of material support (recruitment, training and facilitation). (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An assessment of cross-border TF risks is typically integrated throughout the assessment of TF risk"</sub>

## Box 3.1. Potential Use of Financial Intelligence to Assess Cross-border TF Risks

- **descriptive / limitation**: The low volume of funds terrorists often use makes detection in a threshold-based declaration system challenging, but countries have found that information on general CBNI inflows/outflows may still provide useful information on potential TF vulnerabilities posed by different borders. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may still provide useful information on the potential TF vulnerabilities posed by different borders"</sub>

## Cross-border Terrorist Financing Risks

- **descriptive / data or feature**: Paragraph 52: for assessing cross-border TF risks, jurisdictions would typically consult the cross-border aspects of existing TF information or intelligence, customs and border experience and confiscations, any available analysis of cross-border declarations/disclosures or cross-border wire transfers, and information on international cooperation on terrorism and TF. Further relevant sources are data on flows of funds, goods and people, intelligence on smuggling networks, and the capacity of cross-border controls. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "analysis of cross-border declarations/disclosures or cross-border wire transfers (if available)"</sub>

## Box 3.1. Potential Use of Financial Intelligence to Assess Cross-border TF Risks

- **mandatory / requirement**: Under FATF Recommendation 32, the FATF Standards require every country to run a declaration or disclosure system for currency and bearer negotiable instruments (CBNIs) moving into or out of the country across borders, with a maximum threshold of USD/EUR 15 000. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF Standards require that all countries implement a declaration or disclosure system for incoming and outgoing cross-border transportation"</sub>

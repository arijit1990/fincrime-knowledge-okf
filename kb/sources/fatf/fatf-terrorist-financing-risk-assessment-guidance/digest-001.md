## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

- **descriptive / context**: The FATF's July 2019 Terrorist Financing Risk Assessment Guidance states that terrorists regularly adapt how and where they raise and move funds and other assets to circumvent jurisdictions' safeguards, and that identifying, assessing and understanding TF risk is an essential part of dismantling and disrupting terrorist networks. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Terrorists regularly adapt how and where they raise and move funds and other assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Countries often face particular challenges assessing TF risks because of the low value of funds or other assets used in many instances and the wide variety of sectors misused; the cross-border nature of TF adds challenges, and attack operational needs can include routine transactional activity (e.g. car rental, purchasing a kitchen knife). (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "due to the low value of funds or other assets used in many instances, and the wide variety of sectors"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Lower capacity jurisdictions often face further challenges from a lack of TF expertise or personnel and information gaps on unregulated or unsupervised activities. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Lower capacity jurisdictions often face further challenges due to a lack of TF expertise or personnel"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The report builds on the FATF's 2013 Guidance on National Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing Risk Assessments and offers good approaches, information sources and practical examples for assessing TF risk at jurisdiction level, drawing on inputs from over 35 jurisdictions across the FATF Global Network. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this report provides good approaches, relevant information sources and practical examples for practitioners"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report covers scope and governance considerations for a TF risk assessment, practical examples to overcome information sharing challenges, information sources for identifying TF threats and vulnerabilities, considerations for different country contexts (e.g. financial and trade centres, lower capacity jurisdictions, jurisdictions bordering a conflict zone), information sources for TF risks in the banking and Money or Value Transfer (MVTS) sectors, and good approaches for assessing TF risks facing NPOs within the FATF definition. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "considerations for different country contexts (e.g. financial and trade centres, lower capacity jurisdictions, jurisdictions bordering a conflict zone"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The FATF guidance says that an understanding of TF risk should also feed into national counter terrorist financing (CFT) strategies, and that it helps effective implementation of a risk-based approach (RBA) to CFT measures. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An understanding of TF risk should also inform national counter terrorist financing (CFT) strategies"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The guidance states that all countries should have a holistic understanding of all stages of TF, namely the raising, moving and use of funds or other assets. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "all countries should have a holistic understanding of all stages of TF (raising, moving and use of funds"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report recognises that no single approach fits every jurisdiction when assessing TF risk; each jurisdiction will need to extract the parts of the Guidance most relevant to its own context and threat profile. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this report recognises that there is no one-size fits all approach when assessing TF risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report states that a risk assessment presents a snapshot in time and highlights the importance of regular mechanisms to monitor TF risk on an ongoing basis, taking into account current terrorism and TF threats and developments. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "While a risk assessment presents a snapshot in time"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Given the cross-border nature of TF, jurisdictions facing a low domestic terrorism risk may still face significant TF risks; even countries assessing their TF risk as low will still need to regularly monitor and review their understanding and stay vigilant to changes in TF threats and trends. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions that face a low domestic terrorism risk may still face significant TF risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report highlights the importance of continuing to critically review the approach used to assess TF risk and to identify blind spots and areas needing further information; for some jurisdictions it may be necessary to take a phased approach and prioritise a mechanism for gathering relevant quantitative and qualitative information. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it may be necessary to take a phased approach to assessing TF risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report concludes with areas for further focus: enhanced information sharing on TF risks among jurisdictions with similar threat profiles, continued development of multiagency information sharing initiatives, and use of information technology tools to manage "big data." (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "enhanced information sharing on TF risks among jurisdictions with similar threat profiles"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: In FATF's definition, an NPO is a legal person, arrangement or organisation whose main activity is raising or paying out funds for charitable, religious, cultural, educational, social or fraternal aims, or for carrying out other kinds of 'good works'. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "primarily engages in raising or disbursing funds for purposes such as charitable, religious, cultural, educational, social or fraternal purposes"</sub>

## Purpose, Scope & Objectives

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The low value of funds used, the wide variety of sectors misused, limited domestic TF or terrorism information and few criminal/intelligence cases make TF vulnerabilities and threats hard to identify; lower capacity countries also face lack of terrorism and TF expertise and limited information on unregulated or unsupervised activities. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "limited availability of TF or terrorism information domestically"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Because of such challenges, TF risk often gets limited attention in National Risk Assessments (NRAs) and is sometimes not differentiated from terrorism risk; in developing risk methodologies, jurisdictions sometimes fail to account for the unique threats posed by terrorist financiers and sympathisers as opposed to criminals. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "TF risk is often given limited attention in National Risk Assessments (NRAs) and is sometimes not differentiated"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FATF Standards provide flexibility in how jurisdictions assess TF risks and do not prescribe a particular methodology. The report covers TF risk assessments within broader NRAs and more specific assessments supporting NRAs (e.g. sectoral, agency-specific, high-risk corridor financial flow assessments). (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "do not proscribe a particular risk assessment methodology"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report aims to support jurisdictions in improving the mechanisms used to understand TF threats and vulnerabilities rather than to re-analyse them; it touches on follow-up actions but does not extend to implementing measures to address identified risks. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this report does not extend to the implementation of measures to address identified risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report builds on the 2013 FATF Guidance on national ML and TF risk assessment and on jurisdictions' experience since then, focusing on the unique challenges of assessing TF risk as opposed to ML risk. It is a reference for States assessing their TF risk as requested by UNSCR 2462 (adopted March 2019), and takes account of the 2018 UNODC Guidance Manual for Member States on Terrorist Financing Risk Assessments and the OSCE Handbook on Data Collection in Supporting ML and TF National Risk Assessments. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This report constitutes a reference for States undertaking to assess their TF risk as requested by UNSCR Resolution 2462"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The guidance says there should not be a one-size-fits-all approach to assessing TF risks: scope, focus and objectives vary with a jurisdiction's threat profile, national context and wider counter terrorism and CFT strategies, and the country examples are included for reference without prejudging alternative approaches. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there should not be a one-size-fits-all approach in assessing TF risks"</sub>

## Structure

- **descriptive / context**: The report has six sections: Part 1 on governance, scoping and national coordination; Part 2 on TF risk methodologies, drawing on methodologies of over 35 jurisdictions; Part 3 on cross-border and sector-specific TF risks (banking, MVTS/remittance, exploitation of natural or environmental resources); Part 4 on NPOs and TF risk; Part 5 on follow up and maintaining an up-to-date assessment; and a Conclusion. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Assessing Cross-border and Sector-specific Terrorist Financing Risks"</sub>

## Methodology

- **descriptive / method**: The report incorporates inputs from over 35 FATF and FSRB members who submitted information and case studies on assessing TF risk at sectoral, national and regional levels, and draws partly on a horizontal review of completed Fourth Round FATF and FSRB Mutual Evaluations (MEs). (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "horizontal review of completed Fourth Round FATF and FSRB Mutual Evaluations"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: A FATF/MONEYVAL experts' workshop was held in Tel Aviv, Israel in March 2019 to gather inputs, and the project team held a targeted consultation with civil society representatives on the sidelines of the FATF Private Sector Consultative Forum in May 2019. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "held in Tel Aviv, Israel in March 2019 to gather inputs for the report"</sub>

## Key Concepts and Terms Relevant to Assessing Terrorist Financing Risk

- **descriptive / definition**: The guidance defines TF risk as a function of three factors: threat, vulnerability and consequence. It involves the risk that funds or other assets intended for a terrorist or terrorist organisation are raised, moved, stored or used in or through a jurisdiction, as legitimate or illegitimate funds or other assets. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A TF risk can be seen as a function of three factors: threat, vulnerability and consequence."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: A TF threat is a person or group of people (natural or legal) with the potential to cause harm by raising, moving, storing or using funds and other assets, from legitimate or illegitimate sources, for terrorist purposes. Threats may include domestic or international terrorist organisations and their facilitators, their funds, past, present and future TF activities, and individuals and populations sympathetic to terrorist organisations. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A TF threat is a person or group of people16 with the potential to cause harm"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: TF vulnerability comprises things that can be exploited by the threat or that may support or facilitate its activities, including features of a sector, financial product or service that make them attractive for TF; weaknesses in CFT-specific measures or in wider AML/CFT systems or controls; and contextual features of a jurisdiction (e.g. large informal economy, porous borders). There may be overlap in the vulnerabilities exploited for ML and TF. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The concept of TF vulnerability comprises those things that can be exploited by the threat"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: In the TF context, consequence is the impact or harm a TF threat may cause if it eventuates, including the effect of the underlying terrorist activity on financial systems and institutions, the economy and society. Consequences of TF are likely to be more severe than for ML or other financial crime (e.g. tax fraud), which affects how countries respond, and are likely to differ between countries and TF channels or sources. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "consequences for TF are likely to be more severe than for ML or other types of financial crime"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Given the challenges in assessing consequences, countries need not take a scientific approach and may want to start with a presumption that TF consequences will be severe (domestically or elsewhere) and consider whether any factors would alter that conclusion. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries need not take a scientific approach when considering consequences"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The guidance defines a terrorist as any natural person who commits or attempts to commit terrorist acts by any means, directly or indirectly, unlawfully and wilfully; participates as an accomplice; organises or directs others to commit terrorist acts; or contributes to the commission of terrorist acts by a group acting with a common purpose, intentionally and with the aim of furthering the act or with knowledge of the group's intention. A terrorist organisation is any group of terrorists meeting those same criteria. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The term terrorist refers to any natural person who: (i) commits, or attempts to commit, terrorist acts by any means"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF Recommendations 5 and 6 set out the specific requirements to criminalise TF and implement targeted financial sanctions based on the 1999 International Convention for the Suppression of the Financing of Terrorism and relevant UN Security Council Resolutions. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "set out in detail the specific requirements to criminalise TF and implement targeted financial sanctions"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The guidance describes a TF risk assessment as a product or process built on a methodology agreed by the parties involved, which tries to identify, analyse and understand TF risk and acts as a first step towards addressing it. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A TF risk assessment is a product or process based on a methodology, agreed by those parties involved"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Although assessments can take different forms, the guidance says a TF risk assessment should generally cover every aspect of raising, moving, storing and using funds or other assets (including goods, vehicles, weapons etc.) to meet the needs of a terrorist or terrorist organisation. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a TF risk assessment should generally cover all aspects of raising, moving, storing and using funds or other assets"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The guidance says a TF risk assessment should extend past the revenue raising aspects to cover terrorist procurement and terrorist facilitation networks, including Foreign Terrorist Fighters (FTFs). (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This should go beyond the revenue raising aspects and address terrorist procurement and terrorist facilitation networks"</sub>

## How is terrorist financing risk different from terrorism risk?

- **descriptive / finding or result**: TF risk and terrorism risk are often, but not always, interlinked: assessing TF risk requires considering domestic and foreign terrorist threats, and active terrorist organisations operating domestically or regionally will likely increase the probability of TF. Because TF is cross-border, a jurisdiction with low terrorism risk may still face significant TF risk, as actors may exploit vulnerabilities to raise or store funds or assets domestically or move them through the jurisdiction. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "TF risk and terrorism risk are often, but not always, interlinked."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Factors associated with TF risk are distinct from those for ML risk: laundered funds come from proceeds of illegal activities, whereas funds financing terrorism may come from both legitimate and illegitimate sources. For ML, generating funds is often an end in itself, while in TF the end is to support terrorist acts, individuals and organisations, so funds must for the most part ultimately be transferred to persons connected with terrorism. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "funds used to finance terrorism may come from both legitimate and illegitimate sources"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: While identification of ML risk is often enforcement-led, TF risk by the nature of the threat will need to be more intelligence-led. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "TF risk by the nature of the threat will need to be more intelligenceled"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Although criminals and terrorists may misuse overlapping vulnerabilities, their motive, and so the threat and risk indicators, differs. A low volume of funds transferred may be lower risk for ML but a higher TF risk indicator when considered with other factors (e.g. reporting thresholds or the limited funds needed to carry out terrorist acts); terrorist financiers have been known to use low-limit prepaid cards despite their being considered lower risk for ML (FATF Report on Emerging TF Threats, pages 36-37). (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "terrorist financiers have been known to use low-limit prepaid cards for TF purposes despite being considered lower risk for ML"</sub>

## Core FATF Obligations Regarding Assessing Terrorist Financing Risk at the Jurisdiction-level

- **descriptive / context**: The guidance notes that the risk-based approach is a central element of the Fourth Round FATF Standards, so a jurisdiction's risk and context bear heavily on assessing technical compliance with Recommendation 1 and effectiveness across a number of Immediate Outcomes. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the risk-based approach is a key component of the Fourth Round FATF Standards"</sub>
- **descriptive / requirement**: Recommendation 1 sets basic principles on TF risk and calls on jurisdictions to "identify, assess and understand" the TF risks they face, including by designating an authority or mechanism to coordinate risk assessment actions. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It calls on jurisdictions to “identify, assess and understand” the TF risks they face"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Under Recommendation 1, once the TF risks are assessed, jurisdictions should apply a risk-based approach (RBA) so that measures to prevent or mitigate TF match the risks identified. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions should apply a risk-based approach (RBA) to ensure that measures to prevent or mitigate TF"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: The Interpretative Note to Recommendation 1 (INR.1) clarifies that jurisdictions should take steps to identify and assess their TF risks on an "ongoing basis", so as to inform possible changes to the AML/CFT regime (including laws and regulations) and to help competent authorities allocate and prioritise AML/CFT resources. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions should take steps to identify and assess their TF risks on an “ongoing basis”"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: INR.1 says jurisdictions should have a mechanism for giving the results of the risk assessment(s) to all relevant competent authorities and self-regulatory bodies (SRBs), financial institutions, and designated non-financial businesses and professions (DNFBPs). (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Jurisdictions should have a mechanism to provide information on the results of the risk assessment(s)"</sub>

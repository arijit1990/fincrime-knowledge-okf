## Box 4.2. U.K: 2017 Domestic Review of NPO sector

- **descriptive / case example**: The U.K.'s 2017 Domestic Sector Review of its NPO sector had three primary components: identifying and examining the size, scope and composition of the entire NPO sector (published data augmented with information requested from government departments and agencies that register or regulate NPOs); evaluating NPO structures and oversight (what information each regulatory body collected, to assess transparency and oversight); and identifying the subset of NPOs within the FATF definition that may be at greatest risk of TF abuse and so subject to Recommendation 8. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Evaluating NPO structures and oversight: To understand the legal structures and reporting requirements"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In identifying the at-risk NPO subset, the U.K. analysis took into account the FATF report Risk of Terrorist Abuse in Non-Profit Organisations and the U.K.'s 2015 National Risk Assessment, and considered work for the second NRA under way at the time. The 2017 review considered recent TF case studies involving NPOs, the stated purposes of relevant NPOs, areas of operation and U.K. geographical location, and conducted a trend analysis of historic cases. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the review conducted a trend analysis of historic cases"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Regulators and government departments overseeing NPOs separate from those registered or regulated by the U.K.'s charity regulators were surveyed about NPOs under their purview, with responses providing information on investigations or evidence or allegations of NPO TF abuse. Enquiries were also made with law enforcement agencies, and published studies by the NPO sector and others on its composition were reviewed. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "were approached via a survey enquiring about the NPOs under their purview"</sub>

## Identifying the nature and threat posed by terrorist organisations to NPOs deemed to be at risk

- **descriptive / method**: Paragraph 69: in assessing TF threats to NPOs found vulnerable to TF misuse, jurisdictions will typically look at the general terrorism and TF threat environment, how much domestic intelligence exists on the TF threat to NPOs, existing regional and international typologies (and how far they fit the domestic context), and credible open source information linking domestic NPOs to terrorist individuals or organisations. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "prevalence of domestic intelligence on the TF threat posed to NPOs"</sub>

## Reviewing the adequacy of measures, including laws and regulations

- **permissive / method**: Paragraph 70: when reviewing the adequacy of measures for NPOs assessed as more at risk of TF misuse, jurisdictions may also consider self-regulatory governance and transparency measures (sector and organisational level), government policy measures (including outreach to the sector), and national CFT capacity more generally. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "self-regulatory governance and transparency measures (at the sector and organisational level)"</sub>

## Box 4.3 . U.K.: Review of Adequacy of Measures Including Laws and Regulations

- **descriptive / case example**: In reviewing measures for NPOs vulnerable to TF, the U.K. considered relevant legislation and regulations, self-regulatory measures, and the adequacy of outreach and guidance to the sector. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "as well as self-regulatory measures and the adequacy of relevant outreach and guidance to the sector"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Following a 2014/15 review by the Charity Commission of England and Wales (CCEW), the Charities Act 2011 was amended in 2016 to enhance or introduce CCEW powers and to expand automatic disqualification from being a charity trustee, including individuals convicted of terrorism offences and those subject to financial sanctions. This responded to identified gaps in CCEW's legislation to address abuse of the charity sector, including for terrorist purposes. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "expands the automatic disqualification of certain individuals from holding the position of charity trustee"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Section 16 of the Charities Act 2016 mandates that a review is conducted within three years of the Act coming into effect and that a report of the review is published within four years. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "mandates that a review is conducted within three years of the Charities Act, 2016 coming into effect"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Competent authorities actively seek feedback on the adequacy and relevance of their published guidance. In 2018 a survey was conducted of charities operating internationally (identified as the subset of higher-risk NPOs) to obtain feedback on the guidance and on where resources and tools for trustees could be improved. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "conducted a survey of charities operating internationally"</sub>

## Engaging relevant competent authorities, the NPO sector and other non-government stakeholders

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 71: national coordination can pose particular challenges for NPO risk assessment because relevant information is often spread across several ministries and agencies. Government agencies overseeing part of the NPO sector (regulators, supervisors or self-regulatory bodies) will need to play a central role in assessing TF risk. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "relevant information is often spread across a number of ministries and agencies"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Tax authorities are among those jurisdictions would typically consult: in many jurisdictions NPOs subject to tax exemptions must file annual financial statements and statements of purpose with tax authorities, which may provide important contextual information. R.8 does not require jurisdictions to consider NPOs as reporting entities. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "R.8 does not require jurisdictions to consider NPOs as reporting entities"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FIUs may provide valuable financial intelligence for identifying TF risks to NPOs, through access to suspicious transaction reports or wire transfers and/or information on common TF typologies and trends. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "either through access to suspicious transaction reports, or wire transfers"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Law enforcement agencies (including customs authorities) are an important source of information on the general terrorism and TF threat profile and the criminal environment facing NPOs identified as vulnerable to TF. Intelligence agencies are also an important source on the threat environment, including information from foreign counterparts; the text refers to Chapter 1 for approaches to confidentiality-related information sharing challenges. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "LEAs are an important source of information on the general threat profile for terrorism and TF"</sub>

## Box 4.4. Australia's 2017 National NPO Risk Assessment Approach: Data collection and stakeholder engagement

- **descriptive / case example**: AUSTRAC led collation of an NPO high-risk dataset (later analysed to identify key risk indicators) comprising 28 Suspicious Matter Reports (SMRs), case studies, investigations and intelligence holdings. ACNC and the ATO led a review of regulatory landscapes to identify sector vulnerabilities in laws, reporting requirements and governance. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "comprised of 28 Suspicious Matter Reports (SMRs), case studies, investigations and intelligence holdings"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The second stage was stakeholder engagement: a formal request for information was sent to 23 agencies including all Commonwealth, state and territory law enforcement bodies and NPO regulators; ACNC and AUSTRAC convened round-table meetings with NPO members and peak bodies; and they developed and distributed a TF risk perceptions survey gathering views from government, industry, NPO peak bodies and experts on threat, sector and organisational vulnerabilities and overall risk. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A formal request for information was sent to 23 agencies including all Commonwealth, state and territory law enforcement bodies"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Box 4.4: Australia split the information collection for its 2017 National NPO risk assessment into two stages. The first was identifying and gathering existing documents and data holdings, including open source documents, classified data, financial intelligence and details of criminal/national security investigations. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the collection of information was divided into two stages"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: AUSTRAC used some quantitative data for data-match activities to help identify higher-risk NPOs, for example matching the list of NPO names against national security intelligence holdings. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "matching the list of NPO names against national security intelligence holdings"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: To ensure accuracy, the Australian assessment was developed in consultation with NPO sector members, including by providing the final risk ratings for review. Structured consultations were also held with key government stakeholders and terrorism financing experts to capture intelligence, policy and supervisory perspectives and to evaluate findings and judgements. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This included providing the final risk ratings for review"</sub>

## Engaging relevant competent authorities, the NPO sector and other non-government stakeholders

- **expected / recommendation**: Where there may be a lack of prior engagement with the NPO sector, jurisdictions should consider outreach via trusted representatives and should ensure engagement with a representative sample (for example umbrella organisations and service NPOs). Experience also highlights open online surveys and questionnaires as a good approach to ensure broader feedback from NPOs. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions should consider outreach via trusted representatives"</sub>

## Box 4.5. Kosovo's 2017 Sector Specific Risk Assessment on Terrorism Financing in the NPO Sector

- **descriptive / case example**: Given perceived sensitivities about engaging NPOs in a government information-gathering exercise, Kosovo's Working Group engaged another NPO as an 'agent' to carry out face-to-face interviews with a selected cross-section of NPOs and help them complete the questionnaire. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "another NPO would be engaged as an ‘agent’ to carry out face-to-face interviews"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The agent compiled a list of 150 NPOs, based on the Ministry of Public Administration's official list and municipalities' lists of active NPOs and agreed by the Working Group; a further 21 organisations were added for resilience in case selected NPOs refused. The agent ultimately interviewed 150 NPOs from a cross-section of organisation types, and Kosovo Islamic Society (BIK) contributed strongly to the working group. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a further 21 organisations were placed onto the list"</sub>

  <sub>anchor: "Ongoing engagement with the NPO sector is important in the success of any efforts to identify and assess TF risks"</sub>

## Box 4.6. Kyrgyzstan – NPO Engagement During the TF Risk Assessment

- **descriptive / case example**: In the first half of 2019, NPOs in Kyrgyzstan were included in the government-led working group on the NPO sector risk assessment. The FIU issued a public call for civil society representatives to become formal members, and three NPOs were appointed. NPO representatives worked with government to identify and adapt a methodology developed by an international consultancy. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "with three NPOs appointed to the group"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The methodology adopted in Kyrgyzstan requires active engagement of the NPO sector to increase accuracy of the data collected, increase awareness of potential risks and protective measures, build trust among the sectors and enhance buy-in for the recommendations and results. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to increase accuracy of the data collected, increase awareness about the potential risks and protective measures"</sub>

## PART 5: FOLLOW UP AND MAINTAINING AN UP-TO-DATE ASSESSMENT OF TERRORIST FINANCING RISKS

- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 75: jurisdictions should ensure that the findings of the TF risk assessment are endorsed by senior officials and that all key stakeholders share a common understanding of the outcomes and the relative measures of risk (for example 'low' or 'high' across types of TF risk and/or compared to other domestic crimes). (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Jurisdictions should ensure that the findings of the TF risk assessment are endorsed by senior officials"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: Given the sensitivity of terrorism and TF information, experience highlights the benefits of disseminating a sanitized version of the report and holding closed briefings with key stakeholders to ensure a common understanding of outcomes. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "disseminating a sanitized version of the report, and holding closed briefings with key stakeholders"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Paragraph 77: the FATF Standards require countries to maintain an up to date assessment of their TF risks. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF Standards requires countries to maintain an up to date assessment of their TF risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: An important part of updating a TF risk assessment is critically reviewing the approach taken and identifying areas for improvement next time (for example blind spots and areas needing further information), recognising some jurisdictions may need a phased approach. Risk updates may focus on specific threats or sectors and/or the development of risk indicators. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to critically review the approach taken, and to identify areas for improving the approach the next time"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Jurisdiction experience highlights benefits of embedding a culture of ongoing risk or threat assessment, having ongoing mechanisms to collect relevant TF risk information, and conducting more targeted TF risk assessments that allow enhanced stakeholder engagement (for example focusing on specific sectors or threats, or developing risk indicators). (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "embedding a culture of ongoing risk or threat assessment"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Paragraph 75: the FATF Standards require jurisdictions to have a mechanism ensuring that competent authorities and the relevant financial institutions, DNFBPs and other sectors know the results of the national TF risk assessment(s). (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF Standards require that jurisdictions have a mechanism to ensure that competent authorities"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 76: a TF risk assessment should lead to clear, practical follow-up actions. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An assessment of TF risk should result in clear and practical follow-up actions"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Paragraph 76: follow-up actions may include, without being limited to, amending CFT legislation and policies to fix identified deficiencies, allocating resources or training to key authorities, building platforms or mechanisms for sharing TF information, stepping up engagement with sectors or institutions found vulnerable to TF, and/or putting in place a more systemic mechanism for collecting and maintaining TF or terrorism related information. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "amendments in CFT legislation and policies to address identified deficiencies, allocation of resources or training to key authorities"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 78: although a risk assessment is a snapshot in time, TF risk assessment should be an ongoing, evolving process, with key competent authorities updating their analysis continually in light of current terrorism and TF threats and developments. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an assessment of TF risk should be an ongoing and evolving process"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 78: even a jurisdiction that rates its domestic TF risk as low should update its assessment regularly and stay alert to changes in its terrorism and TF threat profile. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "even jurisdictions that assess their domestic TF risk to be low should regularly update their assessment"</sub>

## Box 5.1. The U.K.s updated 2018 ML/TF Risk Assessment

- **descriptive / case example**: In 2018 the U.K. completed an update of its 2015 ML/TF NRA. The first stage identified evidence that had emerged since 2015, and data gathering mainly extracted data from existing information or assessments from past and ongoing work rather than new data gathering exercises. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The first stage of the 2018 ML/TF risk assessment focused on identifying evidence which had emerged since the last NRA"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Authorities identified the importance of embedding a culture of ongoing risk assessment: both U.K. NRAs benefitted from a wide range of ongoing risk and threat assessments used to inform operations, policy and resourcing, allowing the NRAs to tie these together and analyse findings in a cross-cutting way. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "both UK NRAs benefitted from the wide range of ongoing risk and threat assessments"</sub>

## Box 5.2. Australia's Ongoing Approach to Assessing TF Risks

- **descriptive / case example**: Since Australia's last NRA in 2014, the country has moved away from a broad-scope 'all-in' model to sectoral-based or product-based assessment of TF and ML risk. Since 2016 AUSTRAC has completed five sectoral risk assessments (superannuation, financial planning, securities and derivatives, NPOs and on-course bookmakers), two product-based assessments (stored value cards and travellers cheques) and one issue-specific assessment (remittance corridors between Australia and Pacific island countries). (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "moved away from the broad-scope, “all-in”, model towards a sectoral-based or product-based assessment"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Further assessments to be produced shortly include customer-owned banking (credit unions and building societies) and the broader banking sector. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Customer-owned banking (credit unions and building societies) as well as the broader banking sector"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: AUSTRAC found the sectoral/product-based model requires more time and investment than a one-off assessment, but enables deeper, more focused analysis of each sector, product or issue and deeper industry engagement, helping tailor the assessment so it stays useful. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it enables a deeper and more focused analysis on each sector, product or issue"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Additional benefits of Australia's approach include increased TF awareness and engagement by sectors (including enhanced SMR reporting), identification of blind spots by industry and awareness of where entities sit in the sector, and unique intelligence for partner agencies to inform national criminal threat reports. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "increased TF awareness and engagement by the sectors (including enhanced SMR reporting)"</sub>

## CONCLUSIONS

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 79: the changing nature of TF threats and vulnerabilities means the information sources countries need to consult when assessing TF risk will change somewhat over time. The report recognises that lower capacity jurisdictions often face additional challenges in assessing TF risk, despite terrorist organisations often targeting isolated communities within them for support. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "lower capacity jurisdictions often face additional challenges in assessing TF risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: For lower capacity jurisdictions the report says it is vital that efforts to assess TF risk include community engagement and consider broader criminal networks and activities that terrorist organisations often draw on to raise and move funds or other assets. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is vital that efforts to assess TF risk include community engagement"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Regional information sharing initiatives on TF are described as vital to deepening understanding of TF risk in certain regions, and there is a need for enhanced information sharing on TF risk within regions facing similar TF threat profiles. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is a need for enhanced information sharing on TF risk within regions which face similar TF threat profiles"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Understanding TF risks linked to larger terrorist organisations as well as individual perpetrators often requires close analysis of a large amount of financial data. For developed countries with large financial and trade flows, smart solutions to cope with 'big data' and continued development of multi-agency information sharing mechanisms will likely be important in identifying and assessing TF risk. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "often requires a close analysis of a large amount of financial data"</sub>

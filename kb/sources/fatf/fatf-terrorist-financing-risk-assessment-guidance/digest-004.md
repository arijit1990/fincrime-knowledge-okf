## Box 3.2. Cross-border TF Risk: Lessons Learned from an Australian Perspective

- **descriptive / method**: AUSTRAC matched arrested persons and persons of interest (POI) lists to financial transactions; the results highlighted POI touchpoints with reporting entities and could identify which financial sectors, products, services and delivery channels POIs may have been using. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Matching arrested persons and persons of interest (POI) lists to financial transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: AUSTRAC matched immigration data with Threshold Transaction Reports (cash transactions of AUD 10,000 or more) and Cross-Border Movement of cash reports, which identified individuals who entered Australia and made a large cash deposit but failed to submit a Cross-Border Movement of cash report to border authorities. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Matching immigration data with Threshold Transaction Reports (cash transactions valued at AUD 10,000 or more)"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: AUSTRAC matched Cross-Border Movement of cash customers to SMR customers, which could identify entities that had legitimately declared cash on entry to Australia and had previously been reported by reporting entities for suspicious behaviour or transactions. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Matching Cross-Border Movement of cash customers to SMR customers"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: AUSTRAC leveraged partner agency covert investigation powers to proactively collect intelligence, and leveraged Customs and Border authorities to conduct operations to detect undeclared Cross-Border Movement of cash. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Leveraging partner agency covert investigation powers to proactively collect intelligence"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: AUSTRAC designed a labelling app to label partner agency investigations and intelligence reports, recording a wide range of details relevant to TF threat and vulnerabilities; a footnote says the app allows a quantitative measure of qualitative data. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AUSTRAC designed a labelling app to record a wide range of details relevant to TF threat and vulnerabilities"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: AUSTRAC created a dynamic matrix to measure threat, which let the assessor assign a quantitative risk rating even when inputs were primarily qualitative. AUSTRAC found this type of rating system resonated with industry stakeholders and removed ambiguity in the rating process. (p. 37)[^original]

## Sector-specific Terrorist Financing Risks

## Banking sector

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 55, citing the 2015 FATF Report on Emerging Terrorist Financing Risks: the banking sector is attractive to terrorist organisations moving funds globally because of the speed and ease of moving funds internationally. The low value of funds often used by terrorist financiers and the sheer size and scope of financial flows give them the opportunity to blend in with normal financial activity. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the banking sector is an attractive means for terrorist organisations seeking to move funds globally"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: For many jurisdictions the banking sector is subject to the most robust AML/CFT requirements relative to other financial institutions. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the banking sector is subject to the most robust AML/CFT requirements"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: When assessing TF risk facing the banking sector, jurisdictions would typically collect information on the types of banking institutions and lines of business or services offered, the types of customers served, the nature of TF threats posed to the sector, and AML/CFT compliance and awareness within the sector. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions would typically collect information on: the types of banking institutions and the lines of businesses or services offered"</sub>

## Box 3.5. Potential Considerations When Assessing TF Risk in the Banking Sector Based on Jurisdiction Experience

- **descriptive / method**: On the TF threat posed to the banking sector, authorities would consider information on how terrorist organisations or individual terrorists accessed or misused the jurisdiction's banking system, and open source information on links between domestic banking institutions and foreign legal or natural persons (including financial institutions) with links to TF. A footnote says some jurisdictions found that supporters of certain terrorist organisations used the banking system to transfer funds more frequently than those of others. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "how terrorist organisations or individual terrorists accessed or misused the banking system in the jurisdiction"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: On types of banking institutions and lines of business, authorities would consider the materiality of the sector, the services offered (retail, wealth management, corporate/commercial or correspondent banking), and the volume and location of branches. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "information on the materiality of the sector, including the types of services are offered"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Certain factors may make services vulnerable to TF: past FATF reports have identified the use of pre-paid cards for TF; retail banking services allowing person-to-person funds transfers can be misused by terrorist supporters to quickly move funds overseas; and banks providing correspondent services (particularly nested accounts) may lack full information about the ultimate originator and beneficiary of cross-border transfers. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "banks providing correspondent services (particularly nested accounts) may lack full information about the ultimate originator and beneficiary"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: On customers served by banks, jurisdictions should also consider whether certain types of corporate or individual customers may be more closely associated with TF, including corporate customers identified as higher-risk for TF and individual customers identified, through contextual information, as potentially associated with terrorism or TF. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions should also consider whether certain types of corporate or individual customers may be more closely associated with TF"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Any gap in a jurisdiction's AML/CFT legal framework can be a vulnerability, but weaknesses in these areas may be more closely linked to TF vulnerabilities for banks: STR filing requirements for TF (none, or few filings); lack of authority or ability to share information with the private sector; and weak implementation of targeted financial sanctions, or of customer due diligence obligations or internal controls (especially for clients in high risk areas or lines of business). (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "no authority or ability to share information with the private sector"</sub>

## Money Value Transfer Services (MTVS)/Remittance sector

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 56, citing the 2008 FATF Typologies Report on Terrorist Financing and the 2015 Emerging Terrorist Financing Risks report, says the MVTS/remittance sector has been used to move illicit funds and is vulnerable to TF. Where banking access is limited and terrorist organisations operate in conflict-affected jurisdictions, remittance providers can be the main financial institution consumers use for cross-border transfers. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "remittance providers may be the primary financial institution"</sub>

- **descriptive / method**: Paragraph 57: when assessing TF risks in the MVTS/remittances sector, jurisdictions would typically consult information on the nature of the sector and services offered (including prevalence of services favouring anonymity), the scope of unregulated actors, the nature of TF threat, and AML/CTF compliance and awareness within the sector. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "prevalence of services which favour anonymity"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Experience highlights that engagement with diaspora communities is important to identify how such communities transfer money into or out of the jurisdiction, particularly for local or foreign populations that may be more sympathetic to foreign terrorist organisations and actors. ## Box 3.6. Potential Considerations When Assessing TF Risk in the MVTS/Remittance Sector Based on Country Experience

- **descriptive / method**: On TF threat to the MVTS sector, authorities ask whether local diaspora populations may be sympathetic to regional or international terrorist actors, how such communities transfer money abroad, and how terrorist organisations or terrorists have accessed or misused MVTS/remittance providers domestically and in common international typologies. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Are there local diaspora populations that may be sympathetic to regional or international terrorist actors?"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: On types of MVTS and lines of business, authorities consider the size and scope of the sector (small remitters dealing directly with customers or large MVTS providers operating through established banks), the scale of services favouring anonymity for sender or receiver, including non-face-to-face interactions and/or quick cross-border transfers, and the scope of unlicensed MVTS providers operating domestically. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the scope of unlicensed MVTS providers operating domestically"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The following customer characteristics may indicate a higher TF vulnerability for MVTS/remitters: transactions indicating a customer operates a cash-based business that appears to be a front or shell company or is intermingling illicit and licit proceeds; a customer who knows little or is reluctant to disclose details about the payee (address/contact information); or a customer involved in transactions with no apparent ties to the destination country and no reasonable explanation. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a customer knows little or is reluctant to disclose details about the payee"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: On agents, authorities ask what share of remitters in the jurisdiction rely on agents or other third parties to undertake customer due diligence, what type of relationship agents have with remitters, whether agents are regulated and supervised, and what fit and proper checks they undergo. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "What share of remitters in the jurisdiction rely on agents or other third parties to undertake customer due diligence?"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: A footnote says jurisdictions should also consider whether some of their MVTS providers serve corridors that include jurisdictions or areas identified as funding or supporting terrorist activities, or in which designated terrorist organisations operate. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Jurisdictions should also consider whether a segment of their MVTS providers serve corridors"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: For MVTS, weaknesses in the following areas may be more closely tied to TF vulnerabilities: STR filing requirements for TF (no filing requirement or low number of filings); no authority or ability to share information with the private sector; weak implementation of targeted financial sanctions or customer due diligence obligations or internal controls; and weak onsite supervision or enforcement. If MVTS providers are not subject to licensing or registration, that would constitute an important vulnerability. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "if MVTS providers are not subject to licensing or registration, that would constitute an important vulnerability"</sub>

## Unregulated MVTS Providers and Hawala

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 58: limited banking access in some jurisdictions leads legitimate customers to use unregulated MVTS, and there are multiple examples of terrorists and their financiers using Hawala or other similar service providers (HOSSPs) to transfer funds. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there are multiple examples of terrorists and their financiers using Hawala or other similar service providers"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: In addition to assessing their regulated MVTS/remittance sector, jurisdictions should attempt to assess the scale of activity undertaken by unregulated or unlicensed MVTS providers, considering actions taken to identify and sanction unregulated MVTS providers and the country's compliance with FATF Recommendation 14. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions should also attempt to assess the scale of activity undertaken by unregulated or unlicensed MVTS providers"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Identifying unregulated actors will typically involve engagement with regulated MVTS. Other relevant information sources may include bilateral remittance data and the role of informal remittances in TF investigations and STRs. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the identification of unregulated actors will typically involve engagement with regulated MVTS"</sub>

## PART 4: NON-PROFIT ORGANISATIONS AND ASSESSING TERRORIST FINANCING RISK

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 61: recognising the requirements under Recommendation 8 to assess TF risks facing NPOs within the FATF definition, Part 4 provides considerations and good approaches from jurisdiction experience. General guidance elsewhere in the report is also relevant, in particular national coordination and stakeholder engagement (chapter 1) and maintaining an up-to-date understanding of risk (chapter 5). (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Recognising the requirements under Recommendation 8 (R.8) to assess TF risks facing those NPOs"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In June 2016 the FATF revised R.8 so its implementation is in line with the risk-based approach and does not disrupt or discourage legitimate non-profit activities. The revisions clarified that not all NPOs represent the same level of TF risk and that some represent little or no risk at all. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "not all NPOs represent the same level of TF risk, and that some NPOs represent little"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Jurisdictions continue to face challenges in assessing TF risk for NPOs due partly to the large and often diverse nature of the sector, a lack of identification or understanding of NPOs within the FATF definition, and limited availability of relevant quantitative information or cases. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a lack of identification or understanding of those NPOs falling within the FATF definition"</sub>

## FATF Requirements on Identifying and Assessing TF Risk Facing NPOs

- **mandatory / requirement**: Paragraph 63: FATF R.8 requires jurisdictions to carry out a domestic review of their NPO sector, or to be able to obtain timely information on its activities, size and other relevant features, so as to identify the subset of NPOs falling within the FATF definition. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "R.8 requires jurisdictions to undertake a domestic review of their NPO sector"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Paragraph 63: in doing that review, jurisdictions are required to draw on all available information sources to identify the features and types of NPOs that, because of their activities or characteristics, are at risk of being misused for TF. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions are required to use all available sources of information in order to identify features and types of NPOs"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: FATF defines an NPO as a legal person, arrangement or organisation whose main activity is raising or paying out funds for charitable, religious, cultural, educational, social or fraternal purposes, or for other types of 'good works'. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a legal person or arrangement or organisation that primarily engages in raising or disbursing funds"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The guidance describes the FATF NPO definition as functional: it rests on the activities and characteristics that expose an organisation to TF risk, not on the mere fact that it operates on a non-profit basis. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This functional definition is based on those activities and characteristics of an organization that puts it at risk of TF"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: When assessing risk, R.8 requires jurisdictions to identify the nature of threats posed by terrorist organisations to NPOs deemed at risk and how terrorist actors abuse those NPOs. R.8 also requires jurisdictions to periodically reassess the NPO sector by reviewing new information on the sector's TF vulnerabilities. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "R.8 requires jurisdictions to identify the nature of threats posed by terrorist organisations to NPOs deemed to be at risk"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Jurisdictions should review the adequacy of measures, including laws and regulations, relating to the subset of the NPO sector that may be abused for TF so they can take proportionate and effective actions. The FATF Standards highlight that the review should go beyond laws and regulations, and that existing measures may already sufficiently address identified risks. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions should review the adequacy of measures, including laws and regulations"</sub>

## Understanding the sector

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 66: a comprehensive understanding of the features, nature and activities of the sector is a vital pre-requisite to understanding TF risks to some NPOs, and the domestic review is a critical starting point for determining which NPOs fall within the FATF definition.   <sub>anchor: "A comprehensive understanding of the features, nature and activities of the sector is a vital pre-requisite"</sub>

  <sub>anchor: "there may be entities which meet the FATF definition of an NPO which do not fall within national NPO legislation"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: Paragraph 67: a jurisdiction's domestic sector review could include the types of organisation and the purposes for which they were established, location of activities, services provided, donor base, value of sector assets, movement of funds, means of payments, and cash intensity of the sector.   <sub>anchor: "the types of organisation(s) and the purpose(s) for which they were established"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: Paragraph 68: in identifying types and features of NPOs vulnerable to TF misuse, jurisdictions may also want to consider domestic and foreign intelligence on NPO misuse, investigations and suspicious activity, domestic or international NPO-related TF typologies, and inputs from civil society (including sector or organisational self-risk assessments). This will likely involve both sectoral and organisational vulnerabilities. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions may also want to consider: domestic and foreign intelligence on misuse of NPOs"</sub>

(p. 47)[^original]

## Box 4.1. Malaysia 2017 NPO Risk Assessment

- **descriptive / case example**: In 2017, separate from its ML/TF NRA, Malaysia carried out a TF risk assessment of NPOs within the FATF definition. Its first part was a comprehensive domestic review of the NPO landscape (overall NPO population, distribution of services and expressive NPOs, value of NPO assets, movement of NPO funds) plus an assessment of the legal and regulatory regime to identify NPOs within the FATF definition. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Malaysia carried out a TF risk assessment of those NPOs that fall within the FATF definition in 2017"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The second part of Malaysia's 2017 NPO assessment aimed to identify inherent TF risks facing NPOs identified as vulnerable and the control measures in place. The findings were validated by 31 regulators, LEAs, NPOs and academics before being finalised for deliberation and adoption by the National Coordination Committee to Counter Money Laundering (NCC). (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "involving 31 regulators, LEAs, NPOs and academics, to ensure the robustness of the assessment"</sub>

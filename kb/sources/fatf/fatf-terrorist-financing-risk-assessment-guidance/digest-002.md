## INTRODUCTION

- **descriptive / requirement**: Recommendation 8 contains the TF requirements applicable to NPOs that fall within the FATF definition; its requirements focus exclusively on TF and do not extend to ML, and the risk-based approach is an essential component of R.8. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The requirements under R.8 are exclusively focused on TF and do not extend to ML."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A jurisdiction's TF risk assessment and understanding is considered under FATF Immediate Outcome 1 (IO.1) and affects how effectively it meets objectives under other Immediate Outcomes, notably identifying and investigating TF cases (IO.9), depriving terrorists of assets and preventing NPO abuse (IO.10), and guiding reporting entities on TF risk (IO.3 and IO.4). (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A jurisdiction’s TF risk assessment and understanding is also considered under FATF Immediate Outcome 1 (IO.1)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: IO.1 considers the involvement of all relevant competent authorities: the extent to which competent authorities and relevant stakeholders (including financial institutions and DNFBPs) are involved in risk assessment, how and at what stage they give inputs, and whether adequate resources and expertise were involved. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "adequate resources and expertise were involved"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: IO.1 also considers engagement with non-government stakeholders (the extent to which financial institutions, DNFBPs and other sectors are aware of relevant national TF risk assessment results, e.g. through briefings and guidance, and input to risk assessments), the quality of information sources (comprehensiveness of methods, tools and information), and the reasonableness and timeliness of the TF risk assessment, including consistency with the jurisdiction's TF threats and vulnerabilities and consideration of risks identified by other credible sources. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the extent to which the risk assessment is reasonable and consistent with the TF threats, vulnerabilities and specificities"</sub>

## Preliminary Scoping and Objective Setting

- **expected / recommendation**: The objectives of the TF risk assessment should tie into broader national CTF objectives and activities and build on existing domestic and regional threat and risk assessments. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The objectives of the risk assessment should tie into broader national CTF objectives and activities"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The required scope of a TF risk assessment varies and may be affected by the national and regional TF threat profile, the importance and materiality of sectors, and the jurisdiction's geographic location and demographics. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "(ii) the importance and materiality of different sectors, and (iii) the jurisdiction’s geographic location and demographics"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: For a jurisdiction facing a known threat from a specific terrorist group, it may be beneficial to carry out a targeted risk assessment of that threat (see Sweden's FTF assessment). Where regions share common TF issues, it may be beneficial to conduct a regional risk or threat assessment, which should feed into the national risk assessment. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it may be beneficial to carry out a targeted risk assessment of that specific threat"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: For a large and decentralised jurisdiction with varying risks across domestic regions, a series of regional risk assessments may be preferable or even constitutionally necessary; in such cases federal authorities will need to play a coordinating and advisory role to ensure consistent and comparable methodologies and results. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a series of regional risk assessments may be preferable, or even constitutionally necessary"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: Jurisdiction experience highlights the benefits of a scoping exercise before assessing TF risk, which may consider potential methodologies and their applicability, identifying a lead agency or body and other stakeholders, and the availability of and gaps in information and data. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdiction experience highlights the benefits of carrying out a scoping exercise prior to commencing an assessment of TF risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Box 1.1 (Philippines/regional): because of common TF threats facing South East Asia and Australia, jurisdictions carried out joint regional assessment initiatives feeding national risk assessments. At the August 2016 CTF Summit in Bali members established the Financial Intelligence Consultative Group (FICG), which aims to develop a mechanism for regional financial intelligence analyst exchanges among ASEAN and close partner FIUs. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "members established the Financial Intelligence Consultative Group (FICG)"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Under FICG's South East Asian Counter-Terrorism Financing Working Group, jurisdictions conducted a joint study of funding methods of ISIL-affiliated groups in four parts: ISIL's financing in SEA (regional environment, led by the Australian FIU); external funding to ISIL SEA (Indonesian FIU); hawala dealers financing ISIL and other high threat terrorist organisations in SEA (Malaysian FIU); and self-funding of ISIL SEA (Philippines FIU). Participants found such regional initiatives an effective basis for regional cooperation that helped ensure continuity and relevance of assessments. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Self-Funding of ISIL SEA (led by the Philippines FIU)"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The Dutch context analysis found that the Netherlands' open economy, internationally oriented financial sector and the scale of criminal income from fraud and drug-related crime may potentially affect the country's vulnerability to TF. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "open economy, internationally oriented financial sector, and the scale of criminal income from fraud and drug-related crime"</sub>

## Involvement of All Relevant Competent Authorities

- **descriptive / context**: A comprehensive TF risk assessment requires involvement of many authorities across operational, policy and supervisory functions. Key authorities may typically include intelligence and security agencies, police and border security (LEAs), prosecutors, the FIU, customs, the national authority implementing TF targeted financial sanctions, supervisory and regulatory authorities, and foreign counterparts. Experience highlights the particular importance of involving intelligence and security services. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "experience highlights the particular importance of involving intelligence and security services when assessing TF risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Other competent authorities may hold relevant information, including tax authorities, social welfare administrations and civil aviation authorities (Annex B gives examples). (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "tax authorities, social welfare administrations, and"</sub>

## Preliminary Scoping and Objective Setting

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 1.2 (Dutch 2017 ML/TF NRA): before starting their first NRA, Dutch authorities carried out an explanatory study (drawing on NRA methodologies applied in five countries) and a contextual analysis. The study concluded that, because quantitative data was lacking, the first NRA should adopt a growth model and should also identify blind spots, incompleteness and vulnerabilities in the data, TF methods and other information, in addition to analysing TF risk. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the first NRA should adopt a growth model"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Box 1.2 (Dutch 2017 ML/TF NRA): the Dutch first NRA was planned to concentrate on the ten most significant TF risks, relying mainly on qualitative inputs, and later risk assessments might add quantitative inputs to these findings. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the first NRA would focus on the ten most significant TF risks based primarily on qualitative inputs"</sub>

## Involvement of All Relevant Competent Authorities

- **descriptive / context**: Effective national coordination takes different forms depending on domestic CFT authority structure. Experience highlights the importance of high-level commitment (Prime Minister, Government and Parliament) to support the process, drawing on existing CFT information-sharing platforms (e.g. multi-agency task forces, inter-agency centres for strategic threat analysis, shared platforms, suspicious transaction review teams), and engaging key authorities including LEAs and intelligence agencies early. High-level commitment can also help resolve differing agency priorities. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "ensuring high-level commitment (Prime Minister, Government and Parliament) to support the risk assessment process"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Box 1.3 (Belgium 2018 TF NRA): two separate committees coordinate the fight against ML (Committee Coordinating the Fight against Money of Illicit Origin) and TF (National Security Council). The TF committee includes the federal police, FIU (CTIF-CFI), civil and military intelligence, Customs and Excise, the Federal Public Service Treasury (responsible for UN and EU sanction lists), the Federal Prosecutor's Office and the Coordination Unit for Threat Analysis. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "two separate committees coordinate the fight against ML and TF"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Because of the two committees, Belgium conducted separate ML and TF risk assessments, and kept the committees separate because of the sensitivity of information exchanged in the National Security Council on terrorism and TF; the CTIF-CFI provided the information sharing mechanism between the two committees. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "because of the sensitivity of the information exchanged in the “National Security Council” on terrorism and TF"</sub>

## Engagement with Non-Government Stakeholders – use of multi-stakeholder working groups and public-private collaboration to assess terrorist financing risks

- **descriptive / expectation**: An assessment of TF risk will require engagement with non-government stakeholders, which may include (not exclusively) financial institutions, DNFBPs and NPOs. Since TF risk assessment should be an ongoing process, experience highlights benefits of ongoing engagement, including through supervisors/regulators, which also builds trust and open dialogue. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an assessment of TF risk will require engagement with non-government stakeholders"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Experience highlights engaging a representative sample of the sector (e.g. small and large entities and different services), engaging non-government stakeholders at both the preliminary and validation stages, and communicating the assessment's objectives and purpose clearly and early. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "engagement with a representative sample from the sector (e.g. both small and large entities"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: Engagement may be facilitated through open or closed online surveys, direct consultation, and existing umbrella organisations, facilitators or interlocutors. Countries may also need multi-stakeholder consultations, as some sectors hold vital information for assessing TF risk in other sectors (e.g. the banking sector will likely hold information relevant across a number of sectors). (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Engagement may be facilitated through open or closed online surveys, direct consultation"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Box 1.4 (Malaysia): a 2016 survey identified characteristics of financial transactions of all individuals designated by the Ministry of Home Affairs (Malaysia Domestic List, UNSCR 1373) for terrorism links. It involved 20 reporting institutions (banks, development financial institutions and the pilgrims fund board), which analysed each designated person's account over a 24-month review period before designation; scope was expanded to wire transfers received and conducted during 2014 to 2016 to assess external funding, with information also obtained from banks and licensed remittance MSBs. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The survey involved 20 reporting institutions (including banks, development financial institutions (DFIs) and pilgrims fund board)"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Box 1.5 (UK): the Joint Money Laundering Intelligence Taskforce (JMLIT), established in 2015, enables tactical and strategic intelligence sharing between law enforcement and leading financial institutions; it brings together law enforcement, the regulator and over 30 UK and international financial institutions, and uses the NCA's legal gateway so private sector institutions can share information multilaterally. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Joint Money Laundering Intelligence Taskforce (JMLIT) was established in 2015"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In the Malaysian surveys of banking and remittance transactions, the most frequently used way of raising terrorism funds proved to be self-funding from legitimate sources, notably for travel to conflict zones or use there. Authorities then used the findings to build TF red flags and typologies, which were sent to reporting institutions in March 2018. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "self-funding from legitimate sources is the most commonly used method for raising terrorism funds"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: On the strategic side JMLIT has an Experts Working Group on CFT, chaired by the National Terrorist Financing Investigation Unit (NTFIU) and the Home Office Office for Security and Counter Terrorism, meeting approximately every 6 weeks so public and private experts can share emerging CFT threats and typologies. Current projects include typologies on financial indicators of home grown/lone wolf terrorists, review of financial indicators common to individuals convicted of CT offences, and TF risks affecting corporate and investment banks to inform CDD/RBA. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It acts as a regular forum (approx. every 6 weeks) at which experts from the public and private sectors"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: On the tactical side the JMLIT Ops Group supports NTFIU and the CT network in proactive or reactive CFT investigation enquiries, including an out-of-hours 'critical incident' function; it has provided a quick and efficient means to obtain tactical information, which in several instances proved highly significant to investigations. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "JMLIT Ops Group is available to the NTFIU and CT network"</sub>

## Approaches Taken to Overcome Information-Sharing Challenges

- **descriptive / context**: Effective inter-agency information sharing is critical to a holistic and credible TF risk assessment, but the necessarily confidential nature of terrorism and TF information can create sharing challenges among competent authorities and with non-government stakeholders. Experience shows the importance of enabling policies and mechanisms permitting information sharing where possible. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Effective inter-agency information sharing is critical to ensure a holistic and credible assessment of TF risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FATF RTMG published a confidential report on inter-agency information sharing, including good approaches and practical techniques, disseminated to relevant competent authorities. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF RTMG published a confidential report on inter-agency information sharing"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: Good approach: establish procedures and mechanisms to handle exchange of sensitive information from an early stage of the risk assessment (e.g. engaging intelligence agencies so safeguards are in place from the beginning). (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Establishing procedures and mechanisms to handle the exchange of sensitive information from an early stage"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: Good approach: ensure the lead agency can access and facilitate sharing of sensitive information and, where necessary, segregate handling of sensitive and non-sensitive information (e.g. distinct working groups whose participants have appropriate security clearance). (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Ensuring that the lead agency is able to access and facilitate the sharing of sensitive information"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: Good approach: explore innovative ways to share information with competent authorities and non-government stakeholders where possible, e.g. redacted/sanitised reports, case extracts, 'closed' briefings, anonymised/aggregated statistics, and proxy organisations/competent authorities to validate information. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Exploring innovative ways to share information with competent authorities and non-government stakeholders"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Box 1.6 (Philippines 2015-16 NRA): intelligence from the FIU (AMLC) and law enforcement/intelligence agencies was used to identify typologies on source, use and channels of funds, shared through focus group discussions. Existing coordination mechanisms (National Law Enforcement Coordinating Committee and Joint Terrorist Financing Investigation Group) were used to identify participating agencies, all already members, so they could share information freely. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "intelligence information from both the FIU (AMLC) and law enforcement/intelligence agencies was used to identify and provide typologies"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The Philippines' full report was validated and given to agencies with appropriate security clearance, while a sanitised version was validated by participating agencies and published on the AMLC website. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A sanitized version of the report was also validated by the participating agencies"</sub>

## PART 2: TERRORIST FINANCING RISK METHODOLOGIES – Good Approaches and Considerations

- **descriptive / context**: The FATF Standards do not prescribe a risk assessment methodology and there is no one-size-fits-all approach; ideally a methodology should be flexible, practical and reflect the jurisdiction's features. The analysis highlights elements, similarities and differences of methodologies rather than providing a strict formula. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Ideally, a risk methodology should be flexible, practical and take into consideration specific features and characteristics of the jurisdiction."</sub>

## Information Collection

- **descriptive / method**: A TF risk assessment requires a wide range of quantitative and qualitative information on the general criminal environment, TF and terrorism threats, TF vulnerabilities of specific sectors and products, and the jurisdiction's general CFT capacity. Collection techniques include aggregate statistics or government information (classified or unclassified), domestic and regional questionnaires, online surveys, interviews, working groups and seminars, and open source information. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "collection techniques may include: gathering of aggregate statistics or information from government sources"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Box 2.1 (2016 South-East Asia and Australia Regional TF Risk Assessment): co-led by Indonesia's PPATK and Australia's AUSTRAC, it collected inputs from competent authorities of six jurisdictions (Australia, Indonesia, Malaysia, Philippines, Singapore, Thailand) via a questionnaire on the general TF environment and vulnerabilities and a TF assessment package of mainly qualitative inputs on threats, vulnerabilities, consequences and cases, sent to FIUs. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "collecting inputs from competent authorities of six jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The regional assessment ran two regional in-jurisdiction workshops (Medan, Indonesia and Manila, Philippines) to ensure analytical rigour and accuracy of findings; stakeholders identified the benefits of targeted information collection tools to prioritise specific questions and improve response timeliness. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "benefits of using targeted information collection tools, to prioritise specific questions and enhance the timeliness of responses"</sub>

## TF Threat

- **expected / expectation**: Because of the preventative nature of CFT, domestic and foreign intelligence should be a key input when identifying terrorism and TF threats. Threat identification should not be limited to perpetrators of attacks but include individuals who travel to conflict areas and individuals and organisations engaged in recruitment, training and facilitation (including fundraising). (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "domestic and foreign intelligence should be a key input when identifying both terrorism and TF threats"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Jurisdictions will need a holistic approach to terrorism threats, as TF risk may be linked to terrorism in jurisdictions not in close proximity, so they will likely need information on domestic and international terrorism threats. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "TF risk may be linked to terrorism occurring in jurisdictions that are not within close proximity"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: When assessing terrorism threat, jurisdictions would normally collect information on known domestic terrorist organisations and individuals (including whether nationals are on UN designation lists), known regional and international terrorist actors and the extent to which the jurisdiction is a high-priority target, and the nature, ideological motives and organisational structure of active organisations. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "known domestic terrorist organisations and individuals (including whether nationals are on UN designation lists)"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Further terrorism-threat information normally collected: volume and location of known attacks (committed/attempted) domestically or regionally; domestic or foreign intelligence on global and national terrorism threats (including MLA/extradition requests and informal requests sent/received); and parts of the local population that may be sympathetic to active terrorist organisations (drawing on local population information from countries with active groups or conflict zones and intelligence agency information on specific communities). (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "volume and location of known terrorist attacks (committed/attempted) domestically or in regional jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Also normally collected: intelligence on the volume and characteristics of citizens suspected of travelling overseas for terrorist purposes (Foreign Terrorist Fighters, FTFs); expert views from law enforcement, the FIU and security agencies on the domestic terrorism threat; and open source information on radicalisation among domestic populations (e.g. via social media or community engagement). (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "intelligence on the volume and characteristics of citizens suspected of travelling overseas for terrorist purposes"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnoted sources for threat information include outreach to foreign counterparts and open sources (e.g. the Global Terrorism Database) for regional incidents; FATF internal reporting on ISIL and affiliates since 2015; and open sources such as World Bank bilateral migrant stock and remittance estimates and World Tourism Organisation statistics. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF has been monitoring evolving TF risks associated with the Islamic State in Iraq and the Levant (ISIL)"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: When identifying TF threats specifically, jurisdictions typically gathered information on funding needs and capacity of known terrorist organisations (e.g. level of sophistication) and the extent of communities with links to them; number and types of known domestic TF cases (including assets frozen under UNSCRs); and intelligence on potential domestic TF activity (e.g. STRs, intelligence from domestic or foreign intelligence agencies). (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "funding needs and capacity of known domestic or international terrorist organisations"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Jurisdictions also typically gathered intelligence on source, movement and use of funds by suspected FTFs; financial and trade linkages (including investment flows) with countries with active terrorist organisations and/or conflict zones; volume of active domestic NPOs operating in a conflict zone and known links to terrorist groups or individuals; and expert views from LEAs, the FIU, security agencies, supervisors and other non-government stakeholders on the domestic TF threat. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "financial and trade linkages with countries with active terrorist organisations operating within them and/or conflict zones"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnoted open sources for financial and trade linkages include the IMF Consolidated Portfolio Investment Survey, UN Foreign Direct Investment statistics, central bank and statistics department data, and the World Development Indicators Database. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "IMF Consolidated Portfolio Investment Survey, UN Foreign Direct Investment statistics"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Terrorist organisations and their facilitators are known to have used methods similar to criminals to raise and move funds and other assets, and some are known to have collaborated with local and regional criminal networks (including smuggling networks) in some regions. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "terrorist organisations and their facilitators are known to have collaborated with local and regional criminal networks"</sub>
- **permissive / expectation**: For this reason jurisdictions may also need to collect information on broader illicit finance risks not directly related to terrorism (e.g. the organised crime profile for relevant criminal activities); how much depends partly on known links between TF and criminal networks. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions may also need to collect information on the broader illicit finance risks that are not directly related to terrorism"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Box 2.2 (Sweden): in 2017 Sweden completed a targeted risk assessment of funding of FTFs from Sweden and Denmark between 2013 and 2016, to determine the conditions in place to identify such activities in future, following its 2015 red flag indicator report. According to the Swedish Security Service, approximately 300 people travelled to Syria and Iraq since 2012 to join the Islamic State and Jabhat Fatah al-Sham (JFS, previously Jabhat al-Nusra). (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "approximately 300 people have travelled to Syria and Iraq since 2012 to join the Islamic State"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Indicators linked to FTFs identified in Sweden's literature review: applications for different types of loans (micro loans, SMS loans, student loans); applications for credit cards usable for cash withdrawal and cash withdrawals in areas bordering the conflict area; fund-raising of smaller amounts from many different accounts and unusually high account activity; and purchase of tickets to travel to or through border zones, pre-paid SIM cards or pre-paid mobile subscriptions, outdoor equipment, and rental and leasing of specific vehicle models. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "applications for different types of loans (micro loans, SMS loans, student loans)"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Sweden also reviewed the financial activities of Swedish FTFs using public documents (preliminary inquiry reports, judgements, fiscal re-examination decisions) and information from competent authorities (police, tax authority, secret service, social insurance office, economic crime agency), and gathered information about Danish FTFs over the same period to identify similarities and differences and Danish characteristics that may appear in Sweden. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "reviewed the financial activities of Swedish FTFs based on information from public documents"</sub>

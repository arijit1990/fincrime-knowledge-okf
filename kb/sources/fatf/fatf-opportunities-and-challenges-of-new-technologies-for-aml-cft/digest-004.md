## Box 13. API in practice

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 13 (Tunisia): the Tunisian FIU, CTAF, launched the "Hannibal Platform" RegTech in January 2021 to permanently monitor physical cross-border transportation of currency. It results from cooperation between law enforcement (Ministry of Interior and Customs), banks, the Post Office and exchange offices under FIU leadership, and aims to understand, identify and assess national ML/TF risks related to such transportation. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "launched in January 2021 a Regtech named “Hannibal Platform” that permanently monitors the physical CrossBorder Transportation of currency"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: The Hannibal Platform was designed using blockchain technology, said to guarantee information transparency and enhance safety from hacking, and relies on APIs connecting the databases of the stakeholders (Ministry of Interior, Customs, banks, Post Office, exchange offices and the FIU). (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The platform also relies on APIs that connect databases of the stakeholders"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Through the APIs, Tunisian authorities obtain real-time data on foreign currency imports, banking operations in foreign currency and seizures by law enforcement; they can monitor the final destination of currency declared to Customs, run intersections to get immediate warnings based on programmed parameters, and turn information into intelligence, allowing measures to mitigate national ML/TF risks from physical cross-border currency transportation. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "real-time data on the volume of importation of foreign currencies and all banking operations related to foreign currencies"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Box 13 (India, Account Aggregators): IndiaStack is a set of APIs allowing governments, businesses, start-ups and developers to use a digital infrastructure for presence-less, paperless and cashless service delivery. It provides four layers: a universal biometric digital identity, a single interface for all bank accounts, a secure way to share data, and digital ID records that can move freely; it comprises Aadhaar, eKYC, eSign, DigiLocker and UPI, facilitating open banking. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "India Stack provides four distinct technology layers including a universal biometric digital identity"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Box 13 (US SSA CBSV): the Consent Based Social Security Number Verification service uses an API node that qualified financial institutions or their authorised service providers (permitted entities) can use to verify, with the individual's consent and for statutorily specified purposes, whether the name, SSN and date of birth submitted match SSA records. It returns a "yes" or "no" match, plus a death indicator if SSA records show the SSN holder is deceased, and does not verify an individual's identity. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "CBSV returns a match verification of “yes” or “no.”"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: CBSV is typically used by companies providing banking and mortgage services, credit checks, background checks and licensing requirements, and has a one-time $5,000 initial enrolment fee plus a fee per-SSN verification transaction. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "CBSV has a one-time $5,000 initial enrolment fee, and a fee per-SSN verification transaction."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 100 states that, in addition to facilitating internal procedures, APIs facilitate communication between actors. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In addition to facilitating internal procedures, APIs facilitate communication between actors."</sub>
- **permissive / finding or result**: The use of APIs by supervisors combined with AI-driven analytics could increase the efficiency of mandated reporting and the quality of risk-based supervision, letting supervisors process historical data together with onsite inspection data and contextual factors and generate automated reports to consider and define action. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could increase the efficiency of mandated reporting practices and the quality of the riskbased supervision"</sub>
- **permissive / finding or result**: Automated analysis offers the possibility of giving supervised entities more immediate and detailed feedback on the supervisory process and expectations. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "more immediate and detailed feedback of the supervisory process and expectations"</sub>

## Box 14. Mexico

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 14 (Mexico): the problem described is inefficiencies in AML data architecture, combined with many financial institutions categorised as high-medium risk, causing inadequate deep insights from data informing onsite visits and delayed, unproductive auditing. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Inefficiencies in AML data architecture_ combined with many financial institutions categorized as high-medium risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Mexico's SupTech solution is an API-based AML data architecture with an AI-driven analytics tool: a centralised platform generating standardised automated requests to supervised entities, with raw data received by push or pull submission into a data lake; an API providing secure machine-to-machine transmission into a processing engine that instantly runs validation tests on quality, content and structure of reports and feeds a consolidated, single, access-controlled data architecture. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An API to establish secure, direct line of machine-to-machine data transmission feeding the data into a processing engine"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The Mexican analytics component detects suspicious transactions using predictive analysis and ML techniques (clustering, neural networks, logistic regression, random forests) and recommends AML alerts using ML based on FIs' underlying risk exposures; dashboards and a watchlist tracker show the AML risk landscape, and a second phase is described as including an AI-driven analytics tool using the same techniques. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Dashboards and watchlist tracker provide a view of the AML risk landscape."</sub>

## 4. The Challenges of Implementation of New Technologies for AML/CFT

- **descriptive / context**: The report states that adopting and implementing new technologies in AML/CFT frameworks is not without challenges, and that core challenges are either regulatory or operational. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Core challenges are either regulatory or operational."</sub>

## 4.1. Regulatory challenges

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Data collected suggest a need for clear support from FATF and national competent authorities for AML/CFT innovation. A few experts wanted "technology-active supervisors" willing to engage with technology developers rather than technology-neutral approaches; respondents believe lack of express support by authorities and FATF has diminished interest, investment and trust in new technologies. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "need for clear support from FATF and national competent authorities for innovation in AML/CFT"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The interpretability and explainability of new technologies to supervisors is described as key to securing support for these tools. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The interpretability and explainability19 of new technologies to supervisors is key to securing support for these tools."</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The report says regulated entities must be able to explain, and remain responsible for, the principles and technical details of innovative solutions before deploying these new technologies. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Regulated entities must be able to explain, and remain responsible for, the principles and technical details"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The report says supervisors must be able to understand the models used by AI tools so that they can determine the tools' accuracy and relevance to the identified risks. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisors must be able to understand the models used by AI tools in order to determine their accuracy"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A few respondents stated that most supervisors lack the expertise or resources needed to understand and adequately supervise new technologies. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a few respondents stated that most supervisors do not have the expertise or resources"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Respondents said even the most technologically literate supervisors are often slow to adjust regulatory practices; some jurisdictions promote adoption through innovation events and regulatory support, but this does not always translate into supervisory acceptance of new procedures and compliance practices. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these efforts do not always translate into supervisory acceptance of new procedures and compliance practices"</sub>

## Box 15. Levering public infrastructure to facilitate digital CDDprocedures

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 15 (Denmark): the Danish FSA issued a public consultation, "project AML/TEK", on technological initiatives supporting obliged entities against financial crime. It presents pros and cons of seven initiatives to strengthen the first line of defence, raises trade-offs between fighting financial crime and data protection/privacy, and notes most initiatives have legal implications and raise questions on the legal basis for accessing and sharing data. Three could support further digitalisation of CDD. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The analysis presents pros and cons of seven initiatives that have the potential to strengthen the first line of defence"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Box 15 (Denmark, public registers): a central barrier to digitalising CDD is the absence of verified digital customer information. The analysis examines increased access to registers such as those of the Danish Business Authority, Tax Agency, passport and driving licence registers and Immigration Service. Danish Business Register data is provided by the obliged entities themselves, so although accessible by API and subject to a comprehensive control environment, identification of all faulty or misleading registrations is not certain, compromising its use for CDD; the analysis proposes examining whether lawyers and approved auditors could verify the registered data. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A central barrier for obliged entities to digitalise their CDD procedures is the absence of verified digital customer information"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Box 15 (Denmark, PEP screening): screening PEPs and their relationships is a resource-heavy manual process requiring personal data on customers. In Denmark such relationships could largely be mapped through public registers, though this raises serious data protection concerns; the analysis examines a public PEP-screening solution that could improve quality and reduce cost through digitisation while minimising personal information collection. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Screening of PEPs and their relationships is a resource-heavy manual process for obliged entities"</sub>

## 4.1. Regulatory challenges

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report states AML/CFT technology can only truly be effective if systems rest on standardised data that is easier for developers to integrate, easy to explain to non-experts, and easy to communicate to counterparts and authorities. It also shows the importance of FIUs and other public authorities giving reliable feedback to reporting entities on suspicious activity and ML cases usable for training. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the importance of public authorities, particularly FIUs, providing reliable feedback to reporting entities"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Training a machine learning system on real cases positively verified as involving ML or TF, if available, would offer a significantly better hit rate than training an AI to replicate human compliance officers' decisions on whether the suspicion threshold is met. Automated FIU feedback on which reports are most useful would also help FIs train internal teams and systems. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "would offer a significantly better hit rate than training an AI to replicate the decisions of a human compliance officer"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Lack of data harmonisation was cited as an obstacle because costs of investing in new technologies and expertise increase exponentially if systems need adjustment to different jurisdictional requirements and formats; harmonisation would let actors converge on goals such as common transaction monitoring, feedback to the private sector and risk assessments. Data quality, a concern of 45% of respondents to the digital transformation questionnaire, is seen as an obstacle to adoption. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Ensuring data quality - a concern shared by 45% of respondents to the digital transformation questionnaire"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Real or perceived interpretability issues have limited trustworthy relationships between technology providers and users and trust that data processed through new technologies is robust. An increasing number of actors register data at scale, which has meant a greater ability to match different sets of complex data. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The real or perceived issues of interpretability have also led to constraints"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: 60% of respondents to the digital transformation questionnaire deemed the role of third parties as providers of new technologies sufficiently clear, but private sector respondents requested additional guidance on interpreting current regulations in the digital era. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "was deemed to be sufficiently clear by 60% of respondents to the digital transformation questionnaire"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: The private sector called for clarification on accountability, transparency and supervision of entities using new technologies. The report states supervisors should reflect on what tools regulated entities are adopting and whether vendors of those tools should face additional scrutiny, for example as service providers to regulated entities or via separate regulation and supervision. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supervisors should reflect on what kind of tools regulated entities are adopting"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Respondents said a balance must be struck between integrating technologies and retaining a forward-looking, human-based supervisory process; most available tools still include human input and review as a key component, evidence that they enhance rather than replace current systems. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the majority of available tools still include human input and review as a key component"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Authorities might also consider whether innovative AML/CFT technology used by regulated entities and/or regulatory authorities could be leveraged more effectively through new forms of collaboration, such as public-private partnerships or wider access for regulated entities to government databases. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Authorities might also consider whether innovative AML/CFT technology used by regulated entities"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The report states that the use of innovative solutions should not call into question the ultimate responsibility that lies with regulated entities. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should not call into question the ultimate responsibility lying with regulated entities"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Human input and capacity building were identified as continuing to have an essential role in supporting adoption of new technologies for AML/CFT, in particular regarding three things: elements that technology still cannot overcome, regional inequalities, and expertise on emerging issues. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "in particular regarding elements that technology still cannot overcome, regional inequalities or expertise on emerging issues"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report identifies numerous successful AML/CFT collaborations that were technology aided but relied essentially on dialogue and commitment between the actors; public-private collaborations such as identifying ML/TF red flags showed immediate benefits of technology without being completely dependent on the tools for effectiveness. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "relied essentially on dialogue and commitment between actors to achieve success"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Systems based on state-issued digital identity tools appear to allow greater success in the uptake of digital ID systems and collaborative platforms than systems that rely on collecting data from multiple sources. Data validation may be one aspect where human authority continues to take precedence. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "state-issued digital identity tools appear to allow for greater success in the uptake of digital ID systems"</sub>
- **mandatory / expectation**: The report says that as use of new technologies becomes more widespread, actors must also consider the degree to which machine error becomes, or may not be, acceptable. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "actors must also consider the degree to which"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: AML/CFT effectiveness is limited, among other reasons, by regulated entities' inability to share information with counterparts and across borders; fully understanding suspicious transactions requires access to their full pathway, often beyond borders or held by other entities. New technologies may offer significant value in overcoming this, as discussed in the FATF Stocktake on Data Pooling, Collaborative Analysis and Data Protection report. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "actors require access to their full pathway which is often beyond borders or held by other entities"</sub>

## 4.2. Operational Challenges

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Operational challenges mostly concern adapting practices to new and sometimes untested systems; respondents raised costs, the ability to understand and train staff to implement technologies, and replacement of legacy systems as core issues. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Operational challenges mostly relate to to adapting practices to new and sometimes untested systems"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Adoption of new technologies by supervisors lags behind private sector levels, and respondents stressed the need for supervisors to update their own systems and strategies to interpret and supervise AML/CFT in the digital age. Supervisors identified the cost of replacing legacy systems, availability of quality reported AML/CFT data, and availability of specialised resources and skilled staff as their greatest difficulties. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisors identified the costs associated with the replacement of legacy systems"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Regulated entities may be reluctant to invest in technology that may be hard to integrate with legacy systems or beyond their technical capacity; may become outdated; may not meet regulatory expectations or satisfy an examiner lacking capacity to evaluate it; or may present risks including privacy violations and AML/CFT compliance failures. Smaller financial institutions often lack internal capacity or confidence to evaluate vendors' solutions, judge fit to risk profile, customer base and activities, or implement models and manage model risk. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Smaller financial institutions in particular often lack internal capacity or confidence to evaluate the effectiveness"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Respondents generally agreed some supervisors are less engaged than the private and technology sectors on new trends and digital solutions; their lack of specialist skills, resources and knowledge increases the interpretability challenge and mostly limits the potential for AML/CFT effectiveness. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "some supervisors are not as engaged as the private sector"</sub>

## 4.1. Regulatory challenges

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Security and protection from criminal interference did not rank high among the challenges identified in private sector responses, though it may be more significant from a public policy and law enforcement perspective. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "security and protection from criminal interference did not come up high on the list of identified challenges"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The growing number of criminal cases associated with the use of technologies, for example identity fraud or operations using money mules, should be taken into account in assessing the impact of new technologies on regulated entities' operations and on criminal activity generally. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "growing number of criminal cases associated with the use of technologies"</sub>

## 4.2. Operational Challenges

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Procurement processes for updating legacy systems are described as overly complex and lengthy, and often not targeted at the right actors. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Procurement processes for updating legacy systems, for example, are overly complex, lengthy"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Some respondents said lack of harmonisation could make technology use at scale impossible, preventing cost-effectiveness and hampering development; using big data most efficiently needs it to be available across multiple entities, and without scalability some tools may not be financially feasible. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "technology use at scale might be impossible"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Inability to scale technologies widens gaps in uptake between large and smaller entities and between regions. Respondents agreed wider implementation will only be possible with stronger incentives, either mandated use or a greater trust environment, supporting investment and justifying reform of smaller financial operations and other non-financial obliged entities. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "wider implementation of technology will only be possible if there are more significant incentives"</sub>
- **permissive / finding or result**: Automating initial data input through natural language processing tools could improve data quality by minimising errors by customers or staff registering the data. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could also improve data quality by minimising the errors of customers or staff registering the data"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Consumer appetite for new technologies in financial services was identified as one of the least significant drivers of adoption, though consumer perspective may become more relevant as CDD and other individual-focused digital solutions become more prominent. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "consumer appetite for new technologies in financial services was identified as one of the least significant drivers"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Among the most frequently cited risks of digitalisation are abuse of the system by criminals and increased vulnerability and financial exclusion of certain segments of society, i.e. the elderly and rural or distant (less connected or remote) communities. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "abuse of the system by criminals and its contribution to increasing the vulnerability and financial exclusion"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: New technologies have improved data quality but will continue to rely on human input and manual review. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "New technologies have improved data quality but will continue to rely on human input and manual review."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Machine learning tools depend on existing systems and their manual updating, so "bad data" may harm the models; this includes training data, so any false positives or other errors in it will be built into the machine learning system, although some margin for error is still needed for human bias or unidentified errors. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "If the training data includes false positives or other errors"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: As actors overcome the identified regulatory and operational challenges, the report says it may be worth considering customer response to traditional and new CDD and monitoring approaches and their impact on data protection and privacy. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it may be worth considering the customer response to traditional CDD and monitoring procedures"</sub>
- **mandatory / expectation**: The report says that the risks and unintended consequences of digitalisation must be taken into account in the adoption and implementation of new AML/CFT tools. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there are risks and unintended consequences to digitalisation which must be taken into account"</sub>

## Box 16. Overcoming operational challenges

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 16 (Hong Kong): the HKMA took steps to identify common operational challenges banks face adopting new technologies, starting with an AML/CFT Regtech Forum in November 2019 and conversations with about 40 banks through 2020 in three working groups by technology adoption maturity. In January 2021 it published "AML/CFT Regtech: Case Studies and Insights" (HKMA/Deloitte, 2021) to build awareness and lower real and perceived barriers. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Conversations took place with about 40 banks throughout 2020 across three working groups"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The HKMA report shares case studies of different approaches (e.g. use-case-led versus solution-led), early adopters' insights, technology spotlights and guidance on key operational challenges such as data and process readiness, stakeholder buy-in and executive support, and working with third party vendors. It is structured for banks at different maturity levels, with follow-up industry sharing and interactive lab sessions in progress. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "data and process readiness, stakeholder buy-in and executive support, and considerations when working with third party vendors"</sub>

## 4.3. Unintended Consequences and Potential for Abuse

- **descriptive / context**: Innovative technology in the financial sector brings transformative benefits but also risks of unintended consequences, potential conflict with competing objectives (privacy, inclusion, equitable outcomes) and vulnerability to witting abuse. AI's growing use has raised ethical and legal concerns prompting calls and workstreams for government and private sector standards and safeguards. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "risks of unintended consequences, potential conflict with competing objectives, such as privacy, inclusion, equitable outcomes"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: AI/ML solutions vary greatly and may present significant risks: lack of explainability and transparency can undermine the ability to assess accuracy in identifying suspicious transactions, so effectiveness as an AML/CFT tool cannot be established. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Potential lack of explainability and transparency can undermine the ability to assess an AI/ML solution"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Researchers are finding that many AI algorithms replicate developers' conscious and unconscious biases and apply them at scale, unfairly targeting the financial activities of certain individuals or entities as suspicious, or producing risk profiles and decisions that deny them access to financial products and services. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "many AI algorithms replicate program developers’ conscious and unconscious biases and apply them at scale"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Digital identity solutions lacking adequate risk-based technical assurance and appropriate governance present operational risks and potential unintended consequences and are open to deliberate abuse, although trustworthy digital ID can strengthen identification/verification at onboarding, support other CDD measures, help combat fraud and cybercrime and facilitate financial inclusion. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "do not provide adequate risk-based technical assurance and appropriate governance present operational risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: When digital identification solutions are adopted without regard to the risk based approach or proportionality, they may add to exclusion of underserved communities; for example asylum seekers may be unable to provide initial documentation some digital ID providers require. There are further potential unintended consequences, in particular potential disclosure of personal information. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "asylum seekers may not be able to provide initial documentation that providers of digital ID sometimes require"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Operational risks and mitigants for digital ID, including unintended exclusion and privacy risks, are discussed in Section V of the FATF Guidance on Digital Identity, and stakeholders are encouraged to consult it. The World Bank's updated Principles on Identification for Sustainable Development (2021) provide principles for design, governance and use of digital identity systems to be inclusive, consent-based, privacy-protecting, fair and accountable. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Stakeholders are encouraged to consult this document."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: When used for financial services, the amount of personal data required from customers is elevated, because a high level of assurance about individuals' real identity is necessary for CDD and AML regulation. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the amount of personal data required from costumers is elevated"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: To properly implement the financial inclusion objective, Digital ID tools should be inclusive in their design and operation. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Digital ID tools should be inclusive in their design and operation."</sub>

## Box 17. Challenges posed by biometrics data

- **descriptive / limitation**: Box 17: biometric digital identity tools can raise conflicts with human rights, mostly the rights to privacy (e.g. UDHR Article 12) and freedom from discrimination (e.g. UDHR Article 7). The modernised Council of Europe Convention 108 (108+) and the EU GDPR treat biometric data as a special category needing higher protection, and concerns exist that the broad scope and rapid multi-purpose use of biometrics may put human rights at risk (CoE, 2011). (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "‘biometric data’ as a special category of data requiring a higher level of protection"</sub>

## 4.4. Assessing AML/CFT effectiveness of technology solutions and how to address residual risks

- **descriptive / recommendation**: The report states it is important for regulated entities to continually examine the effectiveness of new technologies in detecting and combating ML/TF risks; effectiveness measurements encourage outcome orientation and help ensure adoption is fit for purpose and performs adequately over its life cycle. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is important for regulated entities to continually examine the effectiveness of these new technologies"</sub>

## Box 17. Challenges posed by biometrics data

- **descriptive / finding or result**: If biometrically-based digital identity solutions were made mandatory, they would have the potential to become a pervasive means of identification, tracking or control, negatively affecting the right to privacy. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they would have the potential to become a pervasive means of identification, tracking or control"</sub>

## 4.4. Assessing AML/CFT effectiveness of technology solutions and how to address residual risks

- **descriptive / context**: Effectiveness measurements will serve as a feedback loop for the public and private sectors to re-calibrate technology-based solutions that do not fulfil their purpose, and clear measurements will help supervisors assess new technologies used by regulated entities. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "serve as a feedback loop for both public and private sector to re-calibrate their technology-based solutions"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Developing effectiveness indicators and determining the acceptable level of effectiveness or residual risk has been identified as challenging, and there is scope for sharing best practices and/or guidance. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it has been identified to be challenging to develop such effectiveness indicators"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The report says all actors should assess whether residual risks may arise from the use of new technologies, or where key human elements cannot be fully replaced by them. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "all actors should assess whether there are residual risks that may arise with the use of new technologies"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The report says it is essential to ensure there is no over-reliance on new technologies, and that where residual risks are identified, regulated entities should demonstrate awareness of them and the ability to manage or respond to them when needed. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is essential to ensure that there is no over-reliance on new technologies"</sub>

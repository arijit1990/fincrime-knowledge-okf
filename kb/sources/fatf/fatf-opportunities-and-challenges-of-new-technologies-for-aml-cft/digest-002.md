## 2. New Technologies for AML/CFT: towards a more effective implementation of FATF Standards

- **descriptive / context**: Inability to adequately identify, assess and mitigate ML/TF risk, including fundamental risk identification elements (customer identification/verification and transaction monitoring), obstructs AML/CFT effectiveness; this is where new technologies can provide the most added value. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This is where new technologies can provide the most added value."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Most current risk assessment and management efforts combine automated but static analyses of a pre-determined set of risk factors with human judgement. Legacy systems are updated with new algorithms and manually inputted information, generating matrices for risk interpretation, but very rarely offer a real-time overview of customer transactional or institutional risks. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "automated but static analyses of a pre-determined set of risk factors, together with human judgement"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: For the paper, "legacy systems" refers to systems and practices that rely on low-tech (manual submissions and databases) processes for data collection and analysis. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "refers to the systems and practices that rely on _low-tech (manual submissions and databases)_ processes"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Traditional risk assessment tools based on spreadsheets (such as Excel) or static reporting platforms do not allow data to be analysed at large scale, limiting correlations and fine-grained risk pictures; the quality of data from legacy systems varies and may lack the accuracy and detail needed to comply with AML/CFT standards. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "do not allow data to be analysed at a large scale, limiting the potential for correlations"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In the private sector, poor risk assessment can lead to defensive box-ticking application of the AML/CFT framework, which is inefficient, burdensome and does not reflect real ML/TF threats. It potentially causes two problems: insufficient attention to mitigating new or emerging risks (allowing ML and TF), and over-application of risk mitigation in low-risk situations where simplified measures may be appropriate (causing unnecessary costs and customer friction, including financial exclusion). (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "over-application of risk mitigation measures in low-risk situations where simplified measures may be appropriate"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: New technologies in identifying, assessing and managing ML/TF risks allow risk analysis to be more dynamic, provide network analysis and operate at customer, institutional, jurisdictional and cross-border levels. Optimal use requires a regulatory and policy environment that frames adequate data pooling and sharing or collaborative analytics, and appropriate access by supervisors and law enforcement. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "allows risk analysis to be more dynamic, provide network analysis, and operate at customer, institutional, jurisdictional and cross-border levels"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 34 names weak understanding of ML/TF threats and risks as a key obstacle to effective AML/CFT implementation. Decisions resting on inadequate risk assessments are at times inaccurate and irrelevant, leaning heavily on human input and on defensive box-ticking rather than a truly risk-based approach. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "One of the main challenges hindering the effective implementation of AML/CFT measures is poor understanding of ML/TF threats and risks."</sub>

## Box 1 Dynamic risk assessment tool for FIs

- **descriptive / case example**: A multinational FI is building a Dynamic Risk Assessment tool to use deeper, richer data updated dynamically with the latest investigative insights, identify financial crime risk faster with fewer unproductive alerts, and create a more accurate and sophisticated customer risk assessment. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Identify financial crime risk at a faster pace and with less unproductive alerts."</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The tool uses cloud capabilities to centralise and process data at scale, plus machine learning, to identify financial crime risk by incorporating existing knowledge on typologies and suspicious activity; examining an entity's transactional and social links to entities with suspicious or confirmed adverse characteristics; quantifying abnormal behaviour relative to peer groups of similar characteristics; and quantifying abnormal behaviour relative to the entity's own history. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Looking at an entity’s transactional and social links to other entities with suspicious or confirmed adverse characteristics."</sub>

## 2. New Technologies for AML/CFT: towards a more effective implementation of FATF Standards

- **descriptive / finding or result**: An analysis of 4th Round FATF Mutual Evaluation Reports (MERs) showed many supervisors are still unable to properly assess risks of supervised entities by sector or institution. The MERs suggest many supervisors lack the ability to collect and process data because of resource and tool shortages, and some supervisors' risk assessments lack adequate updating and the critical basis needed for the risk-based approach and for feedback to supervised entities. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "many supervisors are still unable to carry out proper risk assessments of the supervised entities by sector"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: While digital identity and AML/CFT transaction monitoring and reporting solutions are increasing and RegTech firms have proliferated, respondents confirmed a significant gap remains in supervisors' and regulators' capacity and adoption of these technologies. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is still a significant gap in supervisors’ and regulators’ capacity and adoption of these technologies"</sub>

## Box 2. Dynamic risk assessment tool for Supervisors: a digital solution for risk assessment

- **descriptive / case example**: A commercial-off-the-shelf (COTS) SupTech tool for FI or DNFBP supervisors automates the AML/CFT risk assessment process, usually performed annually, to inform supervisory engagement for a cycle, supporting a risk-based approach. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "automates the AML/CFT risk assessment process, usually performed on an annual basis"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The tool's risk model uses the machine learning concept of dimensionality reduction in the scoring algorithm, right-sizing the model for each entity by reducing variables (risk factors) to those reported with significant activity, which eliminates the 'water-down effect' and helps identify risky narrow business models and small-but-risky entities. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The scoring algorithm right-sizes the risk model for each entity by reducing model variables"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The solution is described as identifying risk with more relevance and precision, and producing residual risk results faster and at lower operating cost than non-automated alternatives. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "produces residual risk results faster and at a lower operating cost, than non-automated alternatives"</sub>

## 2.1. Implementing the risk-based approach

- **descriptive / context**: Quoting FATF (2014), the risk-based approach should be the cornerstone of an effective AML/CFT system and is essential to properly managing risks. Despite FATF guidance, the FATF Strategic Review of the 4th Round of Mutual Evaluations concluded many jurisdictions continue to apply largely rule-based systems, and the private sector continues to struggle to adopt the risk-based approach, preferring a costly and defensive approach. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "many jurisdictions continue to apply largely rule-based systems"</sub>

## Box 2. Dynamic risk assessment tool for Supervisors: a digital solution for risk assessment

- **descriptive / method**: The COTS supervisory tool is organised in three modules. One handles data collection, covering data quality assurance and survey management. A scoring module holds a risk model that ingests survey data, scores Inherent Risk and then factors in an assessment of Controls quality to produce institution-level Residual Risk ratings. A data analysis module offers supervisor-oriented analysis by sector, sub-sector, individual entity and individual risk factor. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "scores Inherent Risk, and combines it with an assessment on quality of Controls to generate Residual Risk ratings"</sub>

## 2.1. Implementing the risk-based approach

- **descriptive / context**: Robust knowledge and awareness of risks, allowing proportionate mitigation, is crucial to effective implementation of FATF Standards. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A robust knowledge and awareness of risks, which allows for the capacity to mitigate and address risks proportionately"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The traditional rule-based approach has led to defensive compliance rather than applying different mitigating measures to different levels of risk, and authorities' response to over-reporting relative to under-reporting has further contributed to defensive actions. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The traditional, rule-based approach has led to defensive compliance"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Defensive AML/CFT frameworks result from regulatory or operational uncertainty and/or lack of trust in the strategies and mechanisms applied; public and private sectors may lack trust in their own risk assessments because of incomplete understanding of reality, lack of information and data, and lack of resources and tools for solid, up-to-date, comprehensive assessments. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Defensive AML/CFT frameworks are the result of regulatory or operational uncertainty"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Greater capacity to collect, process and share data among stakeholders could offer significant advantages by promoting a more dynamic risk-based approach. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could offer significant advantages in this area, as it would promote a more dynamic risk-based approach"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Machine learning and other AI-based tools allowing real-time, quick and more accurate data analysis may offer a solution: they can partially or fully automate risk analysis, account for greater data volumes, identify emerging risks that do not correspond to already-understood profiles, and act as a semi-independent check on the conclusions of traditional risk analysis. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identify emerging risks which do not correspond to already-understood profiles"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Even where such tools reach the same conclusions as traditional analysis, the confirmation can reassure actors of completeness and accuracy, increasing confidence in applying risk-based measures and helping justify them to supervisors. Automated risk assessment tools may also be more readily auditable by supervisors and offer increased objectivity. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "machine learning can increase their degree of confidence when applying risk based measures"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Implementing new technologies to resolve these weaknesses requires technical work, but respondents reported that the primary obstacles are some existing supervisory practices and the difficulties some supervisors face in innovating; the Box 3 case study shows a desired culture shift is emerging, with some supervisors engaging the sector to encourage adoption. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the primary obstacles are some of the existing supervisory practices and the difficulties some supervisors face to innovate"</sub>

## Box 3. FinCEN and the Federal Banking Agencies

- **descriptive / case example**: In December 2018 the Federal Banking Agencies and FinCEN released a Joint Innovation Statement urging industry to consider, evaluate and, where appropriate, responsibly implement innovative approaches to AML/CFT obligations while still meeting Bank Secrecy Act (BSA)/AML compliance obligations. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "in December 2018, encouraging industry to consider, evaluate, and where appropriate, responsibly implement innovative approaches"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The Joint Innovation Statement centres on AML transaction monitoring compliance solutions but extends to innovative solutions for BSA/AML compliance more broadly, including innovative digital identity solutions. It recognises that responsible private sector innovation can help banks identify and report ML, TF and other illicit financial activity by making their BSA/AML compliance programs more effective and efficient. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Statement focuses on AML (transaction monitoring) compliance solutions, but also includes innovation solutions"</sub>

## 2.2. Financial Inclusion

- **descriptive / context**: Promoting financial inclusion is an important part of effective implementation of the FATF Standards and can reduce ML/TF risks overall, but mitigating financial exclusion continues to be a challenge. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Promoting financial inclusion is an important part of the effective implementation of the FATF Standards"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Around the world, one billion people struggle to provide adequate identification documents for opening bank accounts or maintaining access to financial services (Desai et al., 2018). Even where identification is possible, CDD along with strict, box-ticking risk management leads to financial exclusion of often the most fragile segments of society. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "one billion people struggle to provide adequate identification documents for opening bank accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The majority of respondents agreed that protecting individuals' rights to access financial services and ensuring financial inclusion are key elements of adequate AML/CFT implementation, and that mitigating and avoiding such unintended consequences should be a priority. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "protecting the rights of individuals to access financial services and ensuring financial inclusion are key elements"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF reiterated its commitment to proportionate risk-based adoption of its Standards to protect the most vulnerable. Its Guidance on AML/CFT measures and financial inclusion, with a supplement on customer due diligence, sought to raise awareness and encourage countries to use the flexibility of the FATF Recommendations to provide sound financial services to the financially excluded. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FATF has reiterated its commitment to the proportionate risk-based adoption of its Standards"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FATF Guidance on Digital Identity (FATF, 2020) includes detailed information on a risk-based approach to digital identity solutions to support financial inclusion; the G20 High Level Principles for Digital Financial Inclusion (2016) confirmed the need for a proportionate, risk-based approach to identification requirements aided by digital tools and financial literacy; and the UN promotes responsible use of biometric data for counter terrorism, to prevent unintended consequences and respect international law. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The more recent FATF Guidance on Digital Identity (FATF, 2020[8]) also includes detailed information"</sub>

## Box 3. FinCEN and the Federal Banking Agencies

- **descriptive / case example**: The Statement aims to reassure firms that AML pilot programs designed to test and validate responsible innovative approaches will not, by themselves, necessarily lead to any of three outcomes: supervisory criticism if the pilots ultimately prove unsuccessful; supervisory action if a pilot exposes gaps in an existing AML compliance program; or additional regulatory expectations if innovative approaches are implemented. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "will not, in and of themselves, necessarily result in:"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The Statement also made clear that FinCEN will use its exceptive relief authority in support of responsible AML/CFT innovation pilots that a specific regulatory prohibition or impediment might otherwise prevent. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FinCEN will use its exceptive relief authority to support responsible AML/CFT innovation pilots"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The Statement encourages the private sector to engage with the Agencies on innovative BSA/AML pilot programs, noting that early engagement can improve the Agencies' understanding of the approaches and allow supervisory expectations to be clarified as appropriate and as needed. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "highlighting early engagement can promote a better understanding of these approaches by the Agencies"</sub>

## 2.2. Financial Inclusion

- **descriptive / context**: A key element of securing financial inclusion is financial institutions implementing effective, risk-based AML/CFT approaches including CDD, which underpins assessment of individual customer risk in place of a rigid box-ticking approach and indiscriminate policies for broad customer categories. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "CDD underpins the assessment of the risk associated with individual customers in place of a rigid, box-ticking approach"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Technology-based solutions, both digital ID and AML transaction monitoring tools, can facilitate more accurate and up-to-date risk assessments at optimised cost and give greater confidence in conclusions, enabling greater use of simplified due diligence where appropriate. This could be a significant enabler of financial inclusion, which has been held back by unwillingness to use the flexibility of the risk-based approach fully and by profit-based business decisions of financial institutions. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "enabling greater use of simplified due diligence where appropriate"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Innovative technology-based solutions may contribute to financial inclusion provided they are implemented through a responsible, risk-based approach; they can reduce inconsistencies in human control measures, improve customer experience, generate cost savings and facilitate transaction monitoring. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "They can minimise weaknesses in inconsistencies related to human control measures, improve customer experience"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Traditional ID requirements may be the most obvious instrument for identifying customers, but they should not be the only tool used for that purpose. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may be the most obvious instrument to identify customers but should not be the only tool used for this purpose"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Natural language processing tools, biometrics and similar instruments may serve the CDD process better than requiring in-person production of physical ID documents, although the role and review of human analysts and experts remains key to prevent bias and other unintended consequences of over-reliance on technology. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "notwithstanding the role and review of human analysts and experts which remains key to prevent bias"</sub>

## Box .4. Benefits of digital ID for financial inclusion for regulated entities and individuals

- **descriptive / context**: Box 4, benefits for regulated entities: (1) lowering costs, since digital ID can support cheaper and more sophisticated onboarding and, with mobile access, cheaper automated CDD using wider data sets can let customers with no traditional credit record access financial services; (2) portability and interoperability, so verification burden is reduced to one onboarding instance (with particular advantages if the initial verification is government led); (3) reducing human error, since automated data collection and matching allows many more data points to be considered in a shorter time, although human input may still be required. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Portability and interoperability. Systems can be used across multiple institutions or transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Box 4, benefits for individuals: (4) better customer experience, as digital ID greatly reduces the burden of in-person ID and of carrying multiple physical documents; (5) multiple use, as systems allowing multiple use of verified ID simplify daily operations and give greater efficiency in interactions with service providers and authorities. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Digital ID greatly reduces the burden of in person ID"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Technology can enable financial inclusion through enhanced digital transaction monitoring. As set out in the financial inclusion guidance, enhanced ongoing monitoring can manage ML/TF risks tied to the trustworthiness of customer identification and verification data, so risk management relies less on CDD at onboarding. Where customers can provide only less reliable identity evidence, technological solutions such as behavioural analytics may support strengthened transaction and relationship monitoring, enabling customer take-on, and give a more robust ongoing monitoring process and better risk understanding. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "technological solutions, such as behavioural analytics, may support a strengthened and enhanced transaction and business relationship monitoring"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Technology-based solutions could facilitate "white labelled" transactions (e.g. salary, utilities and living expenses payments, government support payouts) and enhance limited accounts if and when customer risk assessment allows, giving more customers access to basic banking while mitigating institutions' risks. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The development of technology-based solutions in this context could facilitate"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Improvements in transaction monitoring may ease financial exclusion if they give banks greater confidence that other financial institutions, such as MVTS providers, run robust compliance programs. Better risk assessments, CDD and adequate monitoring tools could become part of more inclusive, safe financial systems that do not discriminate on means, social or regional context. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "if they give greater confidence to banks that other kinds of financial institutions, such as MVTS providers"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Digital solutions for financial inclusion, e.g. biometrics, have their own challenges: they can exacerbate exclusion for people without electronic devices, trust or awareness, especially with digital-only business models. Limited accounts may restrict expected account functions and lead to unsatisfactory experiences and exit from the formal system, and remote onboarding, account tiers and deferred identity proofs have sometimes led to difficulties in fully accessing services. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Remote onboarding, account tiers, and deferred identity proofs have also been identified as sometimes leading to additional difficulties"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Innovation can help mitigate unintended consequences of reliance on new technologies by offering alternatives for monitoring banking relationships; behavioural risk profiles, network analysis and psychometric data could, for example, inform underwriting and access to credit, complementing digital ID systems. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Behavioural risk profiles, network analysis and the use psychometric data could, for example, inform underwriting"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: It is important that such approaches also create a route toward full-service, unlimited access where possible; technology-enhanced ongoing monitoring over an extended period and behavioural analytics can give a more robust basis for customer risk profiling and improve enhanced due diligence related to untrustworthy identification and verification, potentially allowing limited accounts' functions to be extended. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "create a route towards fullservice and un-limited access to financial services, where possible"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The report states that any adoption of new technologies for AML/CFT purposes must follow a problem-solving approach that is equally aware of not creating additional burden or unintended consequences. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "must follow a problem-solving approach which is equally aware of not creating additional burden or unintended consequences"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: It is important to ensure that CDD at account opening supplies enough information to support effective customer monitoring, which affects how much information has to be collected. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is important to ensure that CDD, at account opening, provides sufficient information to inform effective customer monitoring"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Monitoring will not work as an effective control where an institution holds too little information about its customers and their expected use of the relevant financial products. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Monitoring will not be an effective control if an institution has too little information about its customers"</sub>

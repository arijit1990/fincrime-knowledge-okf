## 5. Creating an enabling environment for the use of new technologies in AML/CFT

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Respondents agreed that FATF and competent authorities need to do more to overcome regulatory and operational challenges to implementing new technologies for AML/CFT. The report cautions about unintended consequences of removing frictions in the system: for example, faster execution of transactions leaves less time to identify criminal activity and increases pressure on systems trying to detect and prevent financial crime. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "faster execution of transactions means there is less time to identify criminal activity"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report states that the opportunities and challenges of new technologies for AML/CFT may depend more on regulatory and policy responses than on further technological development, and that the case for their use is valid for public and private sectors alike, enhancing capabilities, data collection and visualisation, monitoring of criminal activity and efficient use of resources. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may depend more on regulatory and policy responses than additional technological development"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Citing BIS (2019), the report stresses senior management buy-in and securing interpretability and explainability as important for promoting adoption of SupTech and RegTech. Regulated entities' ability to demonstrate the benefits of new technologies to supervisors and internally is key to adequate adoption and supervision, and going forward the focus should be on using technology to address identified challenges and demonstrate progress in AML/CFT effectiveness. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the focus should be on using technology to address identified challenges and demonstrate progress"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Other examples of supervisory collaboration with industry on explainability include MAS (2018) guidance to industry on how to resolve the "black box" model. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "guidance to industry on how to resolve the “black box” model."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Some jurisdictions and mostly large financial sector entities have begun using new technologies in regular compliance, but emphasise that their true added value will only be achieved when adopted at scale by the majority of actors globally. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "will only be achieved when these are adopted in scale and by the majority of actors"</sub>

## Box 18. Personal Account on the Rosfinmonitoring website

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 18 (Russian Federation): Rosfinmonitoring develops a Personal Account (PA) on its website as a communication mechanism with the private sector, combining SupTech and RegTech functions. It was initially designed to file STRs and circulate the list of designated persons; after the pilot ended in 2018 it became mandatory for all reporting entities, now about 80 thousand including 60 thousand DNFBPs who use it regularly, and has proven an effective risk mitigation tool for the private sector. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In 2018 after the “pilot” mode was over, PA became mandatory for all reporting entities."</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: The PA conveys information from Rosfinmonitoring's automated remote monitoring system (ARMS), used to calculate risk assessment for supervisory goals. Each reporting entity can receive information on deficiencies in all aspects of internal control (filing STRs, risk management, use of the list of designated persons, etc.) and mitigate them remotely; about 2 thousand DNFBPs annually succeed in mitigating deficiencies using this information. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Each reporting entity can receive information on deficiencies in its activities concerning all aspects of internal control"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: The PA works as an STR feedback mechanism, giving financial institutions an information flow quality index with criteria defining effectiveness of STR reporting by obliged entities. It also lets the FIU exchange information on ML/TF risks and typologies and disseminate national and sectoral risk assessment results. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "information flow quality index, which includes number of criteria defining effectiveness of STR reporting"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: The PA also raises private sector awareness of legislative requirements through distance e-learning, with courses developed by the International Training and Methodology Centre of Financial Monitoring (specific courses on PEPs and beneficial owner risk management to be introduced soon); a PA for supervisors launched in 2018 supports operational risk exchange between Rosfinmonitoring and supervisory bodies. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In 2018, PA for supervisors was launched."</sub>

## 5. Creating an enabling environment for the use of new technologies in AML/CFT

- **descriptive / finding or result**: A favourable regulatory environment, competitive costs, expertise (training) and scale were identified as key preconditions to adoption of new technologies (Figure 4). (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A favourable regulatory environment, competitive costs, expertise (training) and scale were identified"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 148 states supervisors should take a proactive approach to technology, which would promote the preconditions enabling adoption and help members implement AML/CFT Standards more effectively. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisors should take a proactive approach to technology."</sub>

## 5.1. Technologically-Active Supervisors

- **descriptive / finding or result**: More active support for new technologies from supervisors and FATF would help address outstanding risk and trust concerns of regulated entities. Support already takes the form of tech-sprints, accelerators, innovation hubs and other collaborative initiatives where the private sector can develop, present and test tools and get feedback on applicability to AML/CFT frameworks. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "tech-sprints, accelerators, innovation hubs, and other collaborative initiatives"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Neither the FATF nor individual supervisors should take positions on individual technologies or providers, and responsibility for compliance with AML/CFT requirements remains with regulated entities. The role of FATF and national authorities should be to enable innovation and new approaches, allowing the market to support trustworthy and proven technologies within appropriate regulation and supervision and respecting public policy objectives set by national governments. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Neither the FATF nor individual supervisors should take positions on individual technologies or providers."</sub>
- **expected / context**: A footnote clarifies that "technologically-active" supervision is not to be confused with endorsing specific technologies or digital solutions; FATF and supervisors should remain technology neutral. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FATF and supervisors should remain technology neutral."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Respondents believe collaboration must go beyond specific events to ongoing exchanges and cooperation between supervisors and supervised entities. Overcoming fear of regulatory penalties or sanctions requires more constant interaction, for example a full regulatory strategy reform adjusting to the digital era or specific implementation guidance (as suggested in Box 20). (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Overcoming the fear of regulatory penalties or sanctions requires a more constant interaction"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A report to the European Commission, "Thirty Recommendations on Regulation, Innovation and Finance" (EC, 2019), supports this perception, with many findings corroborated by this report, including the need to clarify explainability and interpretability of AI and associated technologies, promote digital ID and remove default paper requirements, promote technology-driven financial services, and develop measures to support RegTech and SupTech. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "clarify the explainability and interpretability of AI and associated technologies, promote the use of digital ID"</sub>

## Box 19. Innovation hubs, Tech-sprint and Sandbox examples

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 19 (BaFin, Germany): in 2020 BaFin initiated "TechBridge", establishing institutionalised exchange formats for innovators including on AML/CTF issues. The core component is confidential individual workshops between an innovator and selected BaFin experts, possible as early as the research and development phase. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "confidential individual workshops attended by an innovator and a group of selected BaFin experts"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: BaFin's TechBridge selection criteria: the new tools must potentially raise new supervisory and/or regulatory issues, and further criteria include whether they could have a major impact on the financial market and potentially entail high risks. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the new tools must potentially raise new supervisory and/or regulatory issues"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Box 19 (FCA, UK): the FCA regulatory sandbox lets regulated entities test innovative products, services and business models in a live market with appropriate safeguards. It opened for applications from June 2016 with six complete cohorts, in which entities tested AML solutions for transaction monitoring and identity verification. Main interventions are early steers on applying AML regulation, enabling entities to iterate business models, and guiding them through regulatory processes critical to launch. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there have been six complete cohorts of the sandbox"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Box 19 (FCA, UK): in July 2017 the FCA published a report commissioned from PA Consulting on how new technologies are used to streamline AML compliance, and gave clear messages in speeches encouraging experimentation and deployment; Megan Butler spoke of the FCA's view that, used to the right ends, such technologies can be gamechangers against financial crime. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In July 2017 the FCA published a report it had commissioned from PA Consulting"</sub>

## Box 20. Monetary Authority of Singapore

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 20 (Singapore): with the financial industry, MAS developed the FEAT principles (Fairness, Ethics, Accountability and Transparency) on the use of AI and data analytics in the financial sector, guiding FIs on responsible use and strengthening internal governance around data management and use. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a set of principles to promote Fairness, Ethics, Accountability and Transparency (FEAT)"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Box 20: in 2019 MAS collaborated with FIs through Singapore's AML/CFT Industry Partnership (ACIP) on data analytics issues. MAS and industry agreed three key principles for responsible adoption of new technologies: governance, model explainability and model performance. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "three key principles to encourage the responsible adoption of new technologies"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Box 20: there was consensus that there should be no compromise on robust governance as FIs adopt more innovative approaches to financial crime, and that explainability should be a design priority for the system to be effective and should be considered at the onset of system development. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there should be no compromise on robust governance"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In the space of virtual asset transactions and blockchain-based finance, one promising direction is to explore ways of ensuring that protocols and computer code are developed to facilitate AML/CFT compliance while keeping the benefits of innovation. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "one promising direction is to explore the ways to ensure development of protocol and computer codes"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Because developers, protocol designers and third party providers are not explicitly subject to AML/CFT obligations under the FATF Recommendations, the report says the FATF should consider whether further discussion is needed with other stakeholders, for example on the role of technology providers and growing blockchain use in finance, to keep FATF standards relevant and effective in the mid-long term. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FATF should consider whether additional discussion is needed with other stakeholders"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Innovative approaches and collaborative supervision have been identified in the most emerging areas of new technologies; DLT is identified as particularly important in the supervision of virtual assets, and various global initiatives support these technologies and create environments for stakeholder dialogue. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Distributed Ledger Technology has been identified as having particular importance in the supervision of virtual assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: VA transactions based on DLT are often conducted without intermediaries or other obliged entities and face obstacles to achieving regulatory objectives, especially AML/CFT, because tracing and monitoring is difficult. As virtual assets become more widespread, risk mitigation through intermediaries may become challenging over the medium to long term. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the risk mitigation through the use of intermediaries may become challenging over the medium to long term"</sub>

## Box 21. Supervisors and DLT

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 21 (JFSA, Japan): the Blockchain Governance Initiative Network (BGIN) was launched in March 2020 and JFSA has contributed proactively. It tackles challenges of the decentralised financial system underpinned by blockchain through a multi-stakeholder approach, advocated by the FSB (2019) and welcomed by the G20 under Japan's 2019 Presidency, forming common understanding through dialogue on an equal footing among regulators, developers, obliged entities and academia, given the limits of unilateral regulator-to-entity communication. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Blockchain Governance Initiative Network “BGIN” initiative was launched in March 2020"</sub>

## Box 20. Monetary Authority of Singapore

- **descriptive / context**: The FATF has identified Suggested Actions to Support the Use of Technology in AML/CFT (see Annex B), which advance the 2017 San Jose Principle to pursue positive and responsible innovation. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Suggested Actions to Support the Use of Technology in AML/CFT"</sub>
- **mandatory / expectation**: These Suggested Actions note that new technologies for AML/CFT must be developed and implemented in a way that reflects threats as well as opportunities, ensuring their use is compatible with international standards of data protection and privacy, and cybersecurity. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "must be developed and implemented in a way that reflects threats as well as opportunities"</sub>

## Box 21. Supervisors and DLT

- **descriptive / data or feature**: The Blockchain Governance Initiative Network (BGIN) tentatively focuses on three things: an open, global and neutral platform for multi-stakeholder dialogue; a common language and understanding among stakeholders with diverse perspectives; and academic anchors built by continuously providing trustable documents and codes using an open source-style approach. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "creating an open, global and neutral platform for multi-stakeholder dialogue"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: BGIN deals with issues relevant to FATF, including identifying potential regulatory approaches to AML/CFT in DeFi. The box says it may be beneficial for FATF and its members to get involved to enhance dialogue with stakeholders including technology developers, who regulators usually find hard to access; continuing engagement would ensure AML/CFT compliance while avoiding stifling innovation. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It may be beneficial for FATF and its members to get involved in its activities"</sub>

## 5.2. Concluding remarks

- **descriptive / limitation**: The report is a high-level overview of opportunities and challenges, giving examples of existing best practices and/or specific challenges where possible; its findings are not all-encompassing and there is room for improvement in the relationship between FATF standards and digital transformation. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The findings of this report are not all encompassing"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Technological innovation offers great potential for AML/CFT effectiveness but may increase financial exclusion of certain segments (elderly, rural communities, etc.) and create societal challenges, particularly for human rights, democracy and rule of law. FATF is mindful that further challenges may emerge from irresponsible or misguided support for, and reliance on, new technologies. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "irresponsible or misguided support for, and reliance on, new technologies by actors"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: FATF encourages jurisdictions to work together and with private sector actors to consider a holistic approach to new technologies, taking into account their potential as well as their limitations. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FATF encourages jurisdictions to work together and with private sector actors"</sub>

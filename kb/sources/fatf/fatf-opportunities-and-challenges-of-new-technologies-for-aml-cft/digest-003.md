## 3.1. Artificial Intelligence (AI)

- **descriptive / definition**: The report defines AI as the science of mimicking human thinking abilities to perform tasks that typically need human intelligence, such as recognising patterns and making predictions, recommendations or decisions. AI uses advanced computational techniques on structured and unstructured data of different types, sources and quality to "autonomously" solve problems; several types of AI exist with different levels of autonomy, but in general AI systems combine intentionality, intelligence and adaptability. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI is the science of mimicking human thinking abilities to perform tasks that typically require human intelligence"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The report describes machine learning as a subset of AI that trains computer systems to learn from data, identify patterns and make decisions with minimal human intervention; it is a method of data analysis that automates analytical model building through experience and evolving pattern recognition algorithms. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Machine Learning_ is a type (subset) of AI that"</sub>

## 3.2. Natural Language Processing and soft computing techniques

## Box 6. Fuzzy logic applications

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 6 (Italy): Italy's Financial Intelligence Unit (UIF), with the Bank of Italy's Directorate General for Financial Supervision and Regulation, built a fuzzy logic application to construct AML indicators for non-banking financial intermediaries. The system, currently experimental, processes quantitative data (such as cross-border payments from/to higher risk countries) to support periodic AML/CFT risk assessment of such intermediaries. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "currently at an experimental stage - allows to elaborate quantitative data"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: In the Italian fuzzy logic application, indicator data come from the aggregate anti-money-laundering reports (S.AR.A.) database and Supervisory reports, and non-banking financial intermediaries are split into classes by typology (e.g. investment entities, asset management companies, payment and electronic money institutions, credit providers) and main activity (e.g. open funds, closed funds, money transfer, electronic money). (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The source of data used for computing the indicators is the aggregate anti-money-laundering reports"</sub>

## 3.3. Distributed Ledger Technology

- **permissive / finding or result**: DLT may improve cross-border and even global traceability of transactions, potentially making identity verification easier. Responsible and regulated use of DLT for data and process management may also speed up CDD, as consumers can authenticate themselves and may be automatically approved or denied through smart contracts that verify the data. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "DLT may improve traceability of transactions on a cross border basis"</sub>
- **permissive / finding or result**: Under appropriate safeguards and regulatory environment, transactions can potentially be managed via a single ledger shared among institutions across jurisdictions, or via interoperable ledgers, which would significantly increase monitoring possibilities. Contractual arrangements could be built into securities via smart contracts so that other shareholders are automatically notified and, depending on contract design, could become counterparties. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "transactions can potentially be managed via a single ledger shared among several institutions across jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: DLT may offer benefits for managing CDD requirements, including greater cost effectiveness for the private sector and a more accurate and quality-based data pool. In China, financial institutions use DLT to share watch lists or red flags within the scope of confidentiality that the system permits. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "in China, DLT is being used by financial institutions to share watch lists or red flags"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Despite its merits, DLT seems to continue to pose challenges and raise significant concern from an AML/CFT perspective, as seen in virtual asset regulation and supervision. Unlike transactions through conventional intermediaries, virtual asset transactions based on DLT are decentralised and enable un-intermediated peer-to-peer transactions without scrutiny, and they pose jurisdictional challenges if no single entity or clear location is responsible. This could pose potential challenges to traditional FATF standards that focus on regulating/supervising intermediaries. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "enable un-intermediated peer to peer transactions to take place without any scrutiny"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Given the AML/CFT challenges of DLT, the report says the use of this technology should be monitored and further considered by FATF members in detail. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The use of this technology should therefore be monitored and further considered by FATF members in detail"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: The report says authorities may also want to consider the carbon footprint of using DLT compared with traditional tools. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Authorities may also want to consider the carbon footprint of using DLT compared to traditional tools."</sub>

## Box 9. CDD and DLT

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 9: a collaborative initiative of nine large private companies from different industries, with support from local supervisors, promotes a user-controlled (self-sovereign identity) digital identity model on DLT. It follows European and Spanish standards for interoperability, lets the user control operations from a "wallet" to simplify ID and CDD exchanges with partner entities, was in pilot phase and expected to enter production in 2021. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A collaborative initiative with nine large private companies from different industries"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Customer identification/verification and monitoring is a key pillar of the AML/CFT framework but sometimes presents implementation and effectiveness challenges; when implemented on a non-risk basis, these efforts are believed to be costly and mostly inefficient, consuming resources and time without translating into accurate risk assessment or successful business relationships. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "When implemented on a non-risk basis these efforts are believed to be costly and mostly inefficient"</sub>

## 3.4. Digital Solutions for Customer Due Diligence

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Surveyed private sector parties described CDD and monitoring as extremely burdensome, with high uncertainty in data quality and difficulty updating and matching information. CDD is a main source of customer dissatisfaction because of repeated document requests and in-person periodic evidence submissions, and experts say CDD risk analysis is too rule-based rather than behavioural or contextual, leading to financial exclusion of unprivileged individuals or groups. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the risk analysis generated by CDD is too rule-based rather than behavioural or contextual"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: New technologies applied to CDD and monitoring may help through more streamlined onboarding adapted to risk, context and individual without compromising the integrity of the service provider or the financial system. Evidence suggests mixed approaches, where official IDs are provided in tandem with biometric identification, may offer more robust identification and verification. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "mixed approaches, where official ID’s are provided in tandem with biometric identification may offer more robust"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Digital ID is widely adopted and supported in many jurisdictions (FATF has issued guidance on its use), and evidence suggests COVID-19 increased demand for remote financial services delivery. eID and verification is cited as among the "most mature and instantly useful elements of technology in AML" and was often cited by respondents as good practice. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "most mature and instantly useful elements of technology in AML"</sub>
- **permissive / finding or result**: Digital ID may improve customer access to financial services via mobile devices while ensuring security and accuracy through biometric information as a supplement to personal identity information. Some financial institutions may, from basic ID information and with customers' permission, collect additional data to diversify data sources and strengthen their ability to manage the business relationship. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "whilst ensuring the security and accuracy of customer information through biometric information"</sub>

## Box 10. Digital Identification solutions

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 10 (eIDAS Regulation): described as the first global cross-border framework for trusted electronic identification and trust services; it lets eIDs issued in one EU Member State be used to access online public services in another. Trust services include electronic signatures, electronic seals, time stamps, electronic delivery services and website authentication, with harmonised rules giving them the same legal status as paper-based equivalents. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The eIDAS Regulation is the first global cross-border framework for trusted electronic identification and trust services"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Box 10 (India eKYC): India's electronic verification of customer credentials works through Aadhaar, a 12-digit identification number issued by the Unique Identification Authority of India (UIDAI); enrolment captures name, address, gender, date of birth, mobile number and email address. FIs can use the eKYC API to access Aadhaar details for verification, and UIDAI ensures FIs comply with safety, security and privacy standards. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Aadhaar, a 12-digit identification number issued by Unique Identification Authority of India (UIDAI)"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Box 10 (India): customer authentication is by a One Time Password sent to the recorded mobile number or by biometrics. eKYC provisions were incorporated into the Prevention of Moneylaundering (Maintenance of Records) Rules, 2005 (PMLR) in 2019, with "eKYC authentication facility" defined in Rule 2(1)(ca). (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "“eKYC authentication facility” has been defined under Rule 2(1)(ca)."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Box 10 (India CKYC): India has a Central KYC Register (CKYC), a centralised repository of financial sector customer KYC records with uniform norms for inter-usability, managed by CERSAI, which avoids customers repeating KYC with multiple FIs. CKYC was incorporated in the PMLR in 2019 and is defined under Rule 2(1)(ac). (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "centralized repository of KYC records of customers in the financial sector with uniform KYC norms"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Box 10 (Singapore MyInfo): Singapore launched its first National Digital Identity service, MyInfo, in 2017, holding government-verified data. With consent, residents and corporates can share verified data with businesses, reducing the need for additional documents; using MyInfo for CDD enhanced efficiency, security and customer experience in onboarding and let FIs keep onboarding new clients during COVID-19. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Singapore launched the first National Digital Identity service in 2017, known as MyInfo"</sub>

## 3.4. Digital Solutions for Customer Due Diligence

- **permissive / finding or result**: Onboarding tools allowing quick CDD and client traits analysis (such as geolocation, credit checks, anti-fraud software) would enrich CDD and monitoring and lead to a more accurate understanding of the nature of the business relationship and its impact on institutions. (p. 32)[^original]

## Box 11. Machine learning for CDD purposes

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 11 (Brazil): Brazil's Systemically Important Financial Institutions (SIFIs) use machine learning in monitoring and CDD/Employee/Partner processes to identify new ML/TF risks and increase analysis speed and alert assertiveness, with specialised teams, data scientists and technology environments (e.g. SAS, Teradata, R-Studio, Foundry, Hadoop, Python) able to handle large data volumes. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Brazil’s Systemically Important Financial Institutions (SIFIs) are using machine learning"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Box 11 (Brazil, monitoring and alerts): by using analytics tools and integrating databases, SIFIs created new scenarios that reduced false positive alerts and improved alert analysis efficiency; many create thematic scenarios, found effective especially for COVID-19 situations such as purchase of hospital equipment with public resources and payment of emergency aid. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "which resulted in a reduction of false positive alerts and in gains of efficiency"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Box 11 (Brazil): some SIFIs used a gradient boosting machine learning algorithm to create risk clusters, enabling group-level decisions rather than individual analysis, to score the likelihood that an alert will be reported to the FIU. Some use supervised clustering to specify rules catching outliers in cash transactions, others use univariate and bivariate exploratory analysis, feature analysis and feature engineering to identify customers with transactions outside their profile, and one built a link-analysis tool mapping relationships, risks and geographic information among those involved in an alert. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Based on a machine learning algorithm of the gradient boosting type, some SIFIs have created risk clusters"</sub>

## 3.5. Application Programming Interfaces (APIs)

- **descriptive / definition**: An API is described as a type of software that allows different applications to connect and communicate, also often used to provide payment services such as accepting donations over websites. Respondents mentioned APIs among the most used and relevant solutions to identified ML/TF problems. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An API is a type of software which allows different applications to connect and communicate."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: APIs are useful for AML/CFT by, for example, connecting customer identification software with monitoring tools, or risk and threat identification tools with customer risk profiles, to generate alerts or alter risk classifications, more quickly and with larger datasets. This matters because integrating many different, often incompatible systems (including legacy technologies and specialised tools) is one of the most difficult challenges for many financial institutions. (p. 34)[^original]

## Box 12. Benefits of APIs

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Box 12 lists API benefits: enhancing interoperability of traditional banking data and moving away from siloed systems; increasing automation, reflected in optimised resources and output accuracy; and supplying an aggregated, normalised data feed that helps build a more complete risk profile for new customers, for instance during onboarding. (p. 34)[^original]

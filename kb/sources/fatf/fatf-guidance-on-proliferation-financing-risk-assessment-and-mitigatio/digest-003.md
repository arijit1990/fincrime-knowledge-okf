## Why is a PF risk assessment relevant to countries or private sector firms that are far away from the DPRK and Iran?

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Recent typologies show designated persons and entities seek new ways to evade TFS regardless of geographic proximity to the DPRK and Iran, e.g. arranging circuitous financial transactions or shipments through countries with weak AML/CFT/CPF controls. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "regardless of the geographical proximity to proliferating states (i.e. the DPRK and Iran)"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The UNSCR 1718 PoE identified designated persons routing transactions through countries as far as Africa and Europe to disguise fund and shipment flows. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "routing their transactions through countries as far away as those in Africa and Europe"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Past Iran UNSC PoE Reports (e.g. S/2014/394, S/2015/401) found designated persons conducting sanctioned activities in other regions' countries with WMD technology development capabilities, e.g. in academic or research institutes. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Past Iran UNSC PoE Reports (e.g. S/2014/394, S/2015/401)"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The Cayman Islands Financial Reporting Authority (February 2020) noted that as an international financial centre it is exposed to PF; financial services are 40% of GDP, mostly serving non-resident customers, raising PF risk, although there was no evidence of regulated entities financing proliferation. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial services accounts for 40% of the GDP"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Sectoral vulnerabilities are weaknesses and contextual features of a sector that prompt designated persons to exploit it for PF sanctions evasion, such as low PF risk awareness, poor understanding of TFS requirements and weak compliance culture; the complexity and reach of funds movement in each sector and sub-sector may also be considered. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Sectoral vulnerabilities refer to weakness in and contextual features of a particular sector"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Based on ML/TF risk assessment experience, countries tend to emphasise banking or money/value transfer sectors, because designated persons needed international financial system access to pay for overseas components or materials. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries tend to place greater emphasis on the banking or money or value transfer sectors"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Since recent typologies show sectors beyond finance are exploited, countries should know which parts of the economy are subject to sector-specific UN sanctions, as those sectors have higher exposure to PF-TFS breach or evasion. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Countries should therefore be aware of which parts of the economy are subject to sector-specific UN sanctions"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Trust and company service providers are exploited to create corporate entities that obscure links between transactions and designated persons. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "creating corporate entities that designated persons and entities use to obscure the links"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Dealers in precious metals and stones provide designated persons an alternative way to secretly move financial resources across borders. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "providing an alternative method for designated persons and entities to surreptitiously move financial resources"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: VASPs are exploited for products designated persons have mined and stolen and as a platform to move money across borders instantly. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "providing a platform for moving sums of money across international borders instantly"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The maritime sector is exploited to deliver WMD components and materials and to engage illicitly in UNSCR-prohibited economic sectors whose revenue can finance WMD programmes. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the means to deliver components and materials for use in WMD or their delivery systems"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Footnote 29 (UNSCR 1718 PoE Report, 2019): despite strengthened financial sanctions in 2017, their effectiveness is being systematically undermined by DPRK deception and Member State failures, and the DPRK retains international financial system access through rapidly adapting networks. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The DPRK enjoys ongoing access to the international financial system"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: TCSP misuse (including lawyers, notaries, other legal professionals and accountants providing such services) involves shell and front companies, nominee ownership and control, legal persons or arrangements lacking apparent business reasons, and company formation services. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "legal persons with ownership and control through nominees"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: DPRK and Iran PF-TFS (e.g. UNSCR 2231 (2015), UNSCR 2270 (2016) OP 16) note both countries frequently use front and shell companies, joint ventures and opaque ownership structures to violate UNSCR measures; UNSCR 2270 (2016) directs the 1718 Committee to designate those engaging in such practices. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "joint ventures and complex, opaque ownership structures"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: A 2019 UNSCR 1718 PoE investigation found at least five front companies set up by designated entities to hide beneficial ownership of cross-border USD transactions involving two Asian jurisdictions, with a different front company used for each transaction. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "at least five front companies had been established by designated entities"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In another UNSCR 1718 PoE investigation, shell and front companies used to transfer funds to designated persons were closed once the PoE began enquiring about them. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the companies were subsequently closed when the UNSCR 1718 PoE started enquiries"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Designated persons engage precious metals and stones dealers to move gold and diamonds for foreign exchange; PoE reports cite DPRK diplomats smuggling gold between two Middle East countries (August 2020 Report) and DPRK gold mining in Sub-Saharan Africa (March 2020 Report). (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "DPRK diplomatic representatives smuggling gold between two countries in the Middle East"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: A private sector firm's PF risk assessment may consider vulnerabilities in its products, services, customers and transactions, meaning weaknesses or features exploitable for sanctions evasion. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it may consider the vulnerabilities associated with its products, services, customers and transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Product or service vulnerabilities include complexity, cross-border reach (e.g. via distribution channels), easy customer access, a diverse customer base, or provision by multiple subsidiaries or branches. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is complex in nature, has a cross-border reach (e.g. via the distribution channels)"</sub>

## Which types of banking services/products are vulnerable to the potential breach, non-implementation, or evasion of PF-TFS?

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Correspondent banking is not uniformly high risk but has been increasingly exploited by designated persons because they often use international trade for sanctions evasion. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "though not always present a uniformly high-risk area, have been increasingly exploited"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Correspondent banking is one bank (the correspondent) providing banking services to another (the respondent), such as cash management, international wires, cheque clearing, payable-through accounts and FX, letting institutions serve foreign customers without a foreign presence; large international banks may act for thousands of banks. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the provision of banking services by one bank (the “correspondent bank”) to another bank"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: UNSCR 1718 PoE Reports since 2017 show designated entities used correspondent banking to make regular transfers via personal and front company accounts to facilitators in Asia and the Middle East, and set up a company in another Asian jurisdiction whose transactions cleared through several US correspondents with limited insight into origin or beneficiaries. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "cleared through several U.S. correspondent banks that would have limited insight"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Because layered corporate entities and shell companies make screening via foreign respondents hard, financial institutions should understand their foreign respondents' risk profiles and set appropriate mitigating measures. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial institutions should understand the risk profile of their foreign respondents"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Trade finance is exploited because PF evasion often involves cross-border trade; trade finance instruments give the intermediary FI more insight into the trade, so designated persons relying on them do so fraudulently via forged documents, misrepresented parties or a different end-destination or end-user than documented. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "using forged documents, misrepresenting the parties to a transaction"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The majority of trade is conducted through open-account transfers, though many transactions use trade finance instruments in which a financial institution guarantees payment if documentary requirements are met. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the majority of trade is done through open-account transfers"</sub>

## How are virtual assets misused for the purposes of the potential breach, non-implementation, or evasion of PF-TFS?

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: As formal financial system access has closed, designated persons, especially under DPRK-related PF-TFS, have turned to virtual assets to evade sanctions. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they have used virtual assets as another means to evade sanctions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The UNSCR 1718 PoE observed widespread, increasingly sophisticated DPRK cyber theft from financial institutions and VA exchanges worldwide, growing in number, sophistication and scope since 2008 with a clear shift in 2016 towards revenue-generating cyber/VASP attacks. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "including a clear shift in 2016"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Attacks on VA exchanges give the DPRK income that is harder to trace and less regulated than banking; activities include VA theft from exchanges and users, and crypto-jacking (malware turning computers into miners for DPRK hackers), plus mining on its own networks. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the mining of cryptocurrencies through crypto-jacking"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: To obscure stolen VAs, a digital form of layering created thousands of real-time transactions through one-time-use VA wallets; in one case funds moved through at least 5 000 separate transactions and multiple jurisdictions before conversion to fiat. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "transferred through at least 5 000 separate transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: One VA exchange suffered at least four attacks by DPRK-designated entities from 2017 to 2019 with losses of about USD 55 million; another lost USD 4.8 million initially and eventually 17% of overall assets, forcing it to close. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "resulting in losses of approximately USD 55 million in total"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Stolen VA proceeds were converted into anonymity-enhanced VAs via other exchanges, often through hundreds of transactions, aiming to cash out into fiat; some VA arrangements allow near-instant, nearly irreversible cross-border transfers. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "converted to anonymityenhanced VAs through other VA exchanges"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: For FI/DNFBP risk assessments, identifying customer and transaction vulnerabilities is crucial; a starting point is reviewing the number of customers already rated high risk, especially those transacting cross-border with legal persons/arrangements or multiple shell or front companies. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "review the number of customers already identified as high risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Relevant customer/transaction factors include customer type and identity, nature, origin and purpose of the relationship, and number, amount (especially cash) and frequency of transactions involving weak-regime jurisdictions, individuals acting for legal persons (e.g. signatories, directors), or activity unrelated to the stated business profile. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the number, amount (especially in cash), and frequency of transactions"</sub>
- **permissive / data or feature**: Additional sources for a PF risk assessment include domestic/international typologies, national and supranational risk assessments, sectoral reports by authorities, other (especially neighbouring) jurisdictions' risk reports, supervisory case reports, and FATF mutual evaluation reports. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "relevant risk reports of other (especially neighbouring) jurisdictions on their respective sectors"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: A private firm especially benefits from onboarding and ongoing CDD information, transaction monitoring and screening, internal audit and regulatory findings, and public-private information sharing on observed weaknesses. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "transaction monitoring and screening, as well as internal audit and regulatory findings"</sub>

## Analysis

- **descriptive / definition**: Risk can be considered a function of threat, vulnerability and consequence. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Risk can be considered as a function of threat, vulnerability, and consequence."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: In the analysis stage, countries, FIs, DNFBPs and VASPs should understand the nature, sources, likelihood and consequences of identified risks, assign relative value or importance to each, and prioritise them. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they should assign a relative value or importance to each of these risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Likelihood analysis may consider prevalence of known cases, intelligence, typologies, strength of CPF controls, and capabilities and intent of designated persons. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the prevalence of known cases, intelligence, typologies, strengths of CPF controls"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Consequence means impacts and harms, which can be categorised e.g. as physical, social, environmental, economic and structural. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Consequence refers to impacts and harms"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The starting assumption is that consequences of PF-TFS breach or evasion, including potential WMD development, would be severe, though not all PF methods have equal consequences, varying by source, channel or intended recipients. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The starting point is to assume that the consequences of the potential breach"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 30: UNSCR 1718 PoE findings drew on member state reports from Africa, the Americas, Asia and Europe. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "were drawn from reports provided by member states from Africa"</sub>

## Evaluation and follow-up

- **descriptive / method**: Risk analysis typically classifies risks as low, medium or high, with combinations such as medium-high or medium-low; the same risk may be high in one country or firm and low in another depending on context. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "with possible combinations between different categories (e.g. medium-high, medium-low)"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Evaluation uses analysis results to determine priority risk areas; Section 4.3 of the 2013 FATF NRA Guidance gives detailed guidance adaptable for PF. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Section 4.3 of the _2013 FATF NRA Guidance_ provides detailed guidance on this process"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The outcome of a PF risk assessment should be disseminated to competent authorities, including supervisors, and relevant personnel within relevant private sector firms. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The outcome of a risk assessment should be disseminated to competent authorities"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: At national level, competent authorities should establish and implement a national CPF legislative framework and national policies, priorities and action plans addressing identified risks, and may release assessment results as appropriate. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "competent authorities should establish and implement a national CPF legislative framework"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: FIs, DNFBPs and VASPs should consider adapting, calibrating or enhancing policies, controls and procedures to manage identified risks, allocate appropriate and proportionate resources, and train relevant staff on CPF measures based on findings. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should consider adapting/calibrating/enhancing their policies, controls, and procedures"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: Footnote 32: FATF Standards do not require filing PF-TFS information with FIUs, but where a jurisdiction requires reporting of suspected PF-TFS breaches, FIs, DNFBPs and VASPs may refer to such information in their risk assessments. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF Standards do not require filing of PF-TFS information to financial intelligence units."</sub>

## Public-private collaboration

- **descriptive / context**: Assessing PF risks requires public-private co-operation; effective information sharing and coordinated communication with the private sector are fundamental. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Assessment of proliferation financing risks requires co-operation between public and private sectors."</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: Sensitivity of PF sanctions evasion information should not prevent sharing it, or an unclassified/sanitised version, for risk assessment where possible and subject to appropriate safeguards. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this should not prevent it (or an unclassified/sanitised version of it) from being shared"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Public sector sharing methods include ad-hoc sharing of sensitive information with selected private participants and industry roundtables on best practice or trends; this is especially useful for smaller non-bank FIs, DNFBPs and VASPs. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "particularly useful for smaller, non-bank financial institutions, DNFBPs and VASPs"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The private sector may hold vital PF information; for instance banks likely hold information relevant to PF risks in other sectors such as TCSPs. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the banking sector would likely hold information relevant to the assessment of PF risks"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Ongoing public-private dialogue before and throughout a risk assessment, consistent with legislation, PPP frameworks and confidentiality, may improve data and analysis quality, build trust, educate the private sector on rapidly shifting PF threats, and give firms a feedback channel on applying risk assessments. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may enhance the quality of data used and analysis applied in a risk assessment"</sub>

## Maintaining an up-to-date assessment

- **mandatory / requirement**: FATF Standards (INR.1) require jurisdictions to maintain an up-to-date assessment of their PF risks, updated regularly as an evolving process reflecting current threats and sanctions requirements. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF Standards (INR.1) require jurisdictions to maintain an up-to-date assessment of their PF risks."</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Countries are strongly encouraged to publish updated risk assessment results or a sanitised version; if publication is not possible, they may share full or sanitised versions confidentially with private sector entities. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Countries are strongly encouraged to make available the results of the updated risk assessments"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Under INR.1 countries should ensure compliance with R.1 in all risk scenarios; where high PF risk is identified, they should require FIs, DNFBPs and VASPs to take commensurate measures to manage and mitigate it. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries should ensure compliance with R.1 in all risk scenarios"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: For lower identified PF risk, FATF requires countries to apply commensurate measures, but they should understand that PF threats keep changing and designated persons deliberately target jurisdictions that perceive weaker risk exposure. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "For countries that have identified a lower risk, the FATF requires countries to apply measures commensurate"</sub>

## SECTION TWO: MITIGATION OF PROLIFERATION FINANCING RISKS

- **mandatory / requirement**: FATF Standards require countries, FIs, DNFBPs and VASPs to take appropriate steps to manage and mitigate the proliferation financing risks they identify. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF Standards require countries, financial institutions, DNFBPs and VASPs to take appropriate steps"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Under FATF Recommendation 1 and this Guidance, proliferation financing risk means strictly and only the risk of potential breach, non-implementation or evasion of the TFS obligations in Recommendation 7. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "proliferation financing risk refers strictly and only to the risk of potential breach"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Recommendation 7 requires countries to freeze without delay funds or assets of persons designated by or under the UN Security Council under Chapter VII, or those acting for, directed by, or owned or controlled by them, and ensure no funds are made available to them directly or indirectly. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "freeze without delay the funds or other assets of, and to ensure that no funds"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Proliferation support networks use the international financial system via global networks of indirectly connected illicit intermediaries, front and shell companies hiding beneficial ownership, separating proliferation activity from designated persons and co-mingling legitimate and illicit business. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "co-mingle legitimate business with illicit transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The nature and extent of PF mitigation measures depend on contextual factors and the source of PF risk. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The nature and extent of mitigation measures would depend on contextual factors"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: FIs, DNFBPs and VASPs should identify, assess and understand their PF risks and take commensurate mitigation measures. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should identify, assess and understand their proliferation financing risks and take commensurate measures"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: It is inappropriate to indiscriminately terminate or restrict relationships with entire classes of customers without seriously assessing individual customers' risk and mitigation; risk avoidance is not risk mitigation and can cause financial exclusion, pushing customers to unregulated or weakly controlled providers. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Risk avoidance does not equate risk mitigation"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 34: the R.7 coverage of those acting on behalf of, controlled or owned by designated persons applies provided they are not themselves designated under national/supranational regimes. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "are not designated under national/supranational sanctions regimes"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Where de-risking decisions stem from misunderstanding regulatory expectations, supervisors should be able to provide appropriate guidance. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supervisors should be able to provide appropriate guidance"</sub>

## Risk mitigation measures by countries

- **descriptive / context**: Understanding how TFS breach or evasion could occur in a jurisdiction helps countries build an effective domestic mitigation framework, and risk assessments identify gaps for countries and private sectors to address. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An assessment of risks and vulnerabilities will identify potential gaps"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Countries should allow FIs, DNFBPs and VASPs to leverage existing TFS and/or compliance programmes to manage PF risks. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Countries should allow financial institutions, DNFBPs and VASPs to leverage their existing targeted financial sanctions"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: A PF risk assessment need not be a separate exercise and can be covered by existing ML or sanctions risk assessments; PF controls can sit within enterprise-wide risk management, which at large/complex FIs often already includes PF. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A PF risk assessment does not have to be an individual exercise"</sub>

## Foundational elements of proliferation financing risk mitigation

- **descriptive / context**: A robust TFS implementation system is a strong foundation for PF risk mitigation; national risk assessments should help countries and private entities determine and prioritise mitigation resources. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A robust system for implementing targeted financial sanctions sets a strong foundation"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: FIs, DNFBPs and VASPs should be required to identify and assess PF risks across customers, countries/geographies, and products, services, transactions or delivery channels; document assessments, keep them current, and be able to provide them to competent authorities and SRBs. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "They should document those assessments in order to be able to demonstrate their basis"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The guidance says the nature and extent of an institutional PF risk assessment by financial institutions, DNFBPs and VASPs should be appropriate to the nature and size of the business, and that they should always understand their PF risks. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should always understand their proliferation financing risks"</sub>
- **permissive / requirement**: Competent authorities or SRBs may determine that individual documented PF risk assessments are not required for financial institutions, DNFBPs and VASPs, if the specific risks inherent to the sector are clearly identified and understood. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may determine that individual documented risk assessments are not required"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Countries should have effective legal frameworks to implement proliferation-related TFS without delay in line with Recommendation 7, and establish/identify competent authorities to implement and enforce them, with clear institutional mechanisms to detect evasion and sanction violations. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "implement proliferation-related targeted financial sanctions without delay in line with Recommendation 7"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Countries should have mechanisms to notify designations promptly to FIs, DNFBPs and VASPs and efficient list-updating processes, so firms do not deal with designated persons while UN designations are being transposed domestically. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "designations are notified to all relevant parties, including financial institutions, DNFBPs and VASPs, in a timely manner"</sub>

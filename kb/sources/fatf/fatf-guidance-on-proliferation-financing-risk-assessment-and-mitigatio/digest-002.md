## Identification

- **descriptive / method**: A good starting point for identifying PF threats, at national and firm level, is a list of major known or suspected threats, exploited sectors/products/services, the types and activities of designated individuals/entities, and the main reasons designated persons are not identified or deprived of assets. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "begin by compiling a list of major known or suspected threats"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: R.7 and DPRK-related UNSCR PF-TFS requirements cover not only designated persons and entities but also persons and entities acting on their behalf. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "focus not only on the designated persons and entities, but also persons and entities acting on their behalf"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Although PF threat identification methodology may resemble ML/TF methodology, countries and firms should note that PF threats differ significantly in nature from ML/TF threats. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the nature of PF threats is significantly different from ML/TF threats"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: PF threats can be posed by persons and entities designated under relevant UNSCRs (DPRK and Iran) and the international networks they create to disguise activity, and can also be indirectly related to designated persons; their financing needs and methods may differ from those of money launderers and terrorists. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the international networks they have created to disguise their activities"</sub>
- **expected / context**: Financing in breach or evasion of PF-TFS can come from both legitimate and illegitimate activities used to raise funds or obtain foreign exchange, and need not involve laundering of proceeds. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may not necessarily involve laundering of proceeds"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Examples of exploiting legitimate activities for PF include procuring or trading dual-use goods or goods subject to export control, and trade in natural resources in contravention of relevant UNSCRs. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "procuring or trading of dual-use goods or goods subject to export control20"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 19: DPRK PF-TFS (e.g. UNSCR 1718 (2006)) cover funds, other financial assets and economic resources owned or controlled, directly or indirectly, by designated persons and entities. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "owned or controlled, directly or indirectly , by designated persons and entities are covered"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Footnote 19: FATF R.7.2(b), applying to both DPRK and Iran regimes, extends freezing obligations to funds wholly or jointly owned or controlled directly or indirectly by designated persons, funds derived from such funds, and funds of persons acting on behalf of or at the direction of designated persons. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF Standards (R.7.2(b)), applicable to both the DPRK and Iran regimes"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 20: examples of dual-use or export-controlled goods appear in the 2008 FATF Typologies Report on Proliferation Financing (page 7) and in lists such as the Nuclear Suppliers Group Control Lists, Australia Group Common Control Lists, Missile Technology Control Regime Guidelines and the Equipment, Software and Technology Annex. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Nuclear Suppliers Group Control Lists, the Australia Group Common Control Lists"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Illegitimate PF funding activities cited include smuggling of cash, gold and other high-value goods, cyberattacks, drugs trafficking, and export of arms and natural resources such as sand, often across multiple jurisdictions. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "smuggling of cash,22 gold, and other highvalue goods,23 cyberattacks,24 drugs trafficking,25"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Because countries and firms have different risk profiles and PF threats, they are encouraged to take a holistic approach to gathering threat information, drawing on domestic, regional and international PF threat sources. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "They are therefore encouraged to take a holistic approach when gathering threat information"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Footnotes 21 and 23 (UNSCR 1718 PoE Report): examples of DPRK sanctions-evading trade include sales of high-end electrical/electronic sound and image recording/reproduction apparatus and of luxury yachts. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "sale of high-end electrical/electronic apparatus for recording and reproducing sound and images"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Footnote 24: the UNSCR 1718 PoE Report found the DPRK used cyberattacks to force transfers from financial institutions and VASP exchanges to evade sanctions and gain foreign currency, attacks that have grown in sophistication and scale since 2016. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "have grown in sophistication and scale since 2016"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Footnote 26: the March 2020 UNSCR 1718 PoE report describes DPRK evading UNSCRs via illicit maritime exports of commodities, notably coal and sand, a revenue stream that historically contributed to its nuclear and ballistic missile programmes. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "illicit maritime export of commodities, notably coal and sand"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: FATF notes that illegitimate activities used to raise funds for proliferation (such as smuggling, cyberattacks or drug trafficking) can occur across multiple jurisdictions, and that designated persons and entities frequently use front and shell companies to conduct such businesses. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Frequently, designated persons and entities use front and shell companies to conduct such businesses."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: FATF describes the use of front and shell companies by designated persons and entities as a deliberate strategy to obscure the fact that economic resources, assets and funds are ultimately being made available to designated persons or entities. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Doing so is a deliberate strategy to obscure the fact that economic resources, assets, and funds"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The absence of known or suspected PF-TFS breach, non-implementation or evasion cases in a country does not necessarily mean the country or a firm faces low or no proliferation financing risk. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "does not necessarily mean that a country or a private sector firm faces low or any proliferation financing risk"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Since designated persons use diverse, evolving methods and spread operations across jurisdictions, countries and firms should still consider the likelihood of funds being made available directly or indirectly to them in their jurisdiction or via customer relationships or products. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should still consider the likelihood of funds being made available directly or indirectly"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: Where local case studies are lacking, countries and firms may use scenario building or focus groups with domestic or regional operational experts to assess PF risk; UN Panel of Experts reports (e.g. under UNSCR 1718 (2006) and UNSCR 1874 (2009)) also highlight exposure methods. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "techniques such as scenario building, or focus groups with domestic or regional operational experts"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: DPRK's state-owned Foreign Trade Bank (FTB), despite being designated, operated cover branches in several jurisdictions and was central to laundering money through the US financial system to acquire weapons programme components. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "was the centrepiece of efforts to launder money through the United States (U.S.) financial system"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: FTB kept correspondent bank accounts and representative offices abroad that created and staffed front companies to transact; in June 2020 US authorities seized millions of dollars in correspondent accounts held in names of FTB-controlled front companies operating in Asia, the Middle East and Europe. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In June 2020, U.S. authorities seized millions of dollars held in correspondent accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Guidance on risk mitigation in low-risk cases is in Section 2 (paragraphs 66-67); the 2019 FATF TFRA Guidance covers jurisdictions with few or no terrorism/TF cases (paragraphs 34-35). (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "risk mitigation measures in case of low risks (paragraphs 66-67)"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Potential PF threat information sources include actual or known typologies, summaries of case types and schemes in PF-TFS breach or evasion, and designated persons and entities targeted by relevant UNSCR PF-TFS. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Potential information sources may include actual or known typologies"</sub>
- **recommended / data or feature**: For a national PF risk assessment, authorities are encouraged to use financial intelligence and law enforcement data, and customs documents such as customs declarations, which show how PF-TFS breach or evasion may occur. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "customs documents (e.g. customs declaration) would provide additional information"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Domestic and foreign intelligence is another national source: on global/regional/national proliferation threats, on source, movement and use of funds by designated persons and those linked to DPRK and Iran, and on potential PF activities. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "domestic and foreign intelligence on (i) global, regional, and national proliferation threats"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Intelligence may not immediately reveal PF activity but helps build an overall threat and vulnerability picture; private sector firms may hold information on TFS breaches or typologies. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may be relevant to building an overall picture of threats and vulnerabilities"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: For a firm's PF risk assessment, relevant sources include firm and group-wide CDD databases from onboarding and ongoing due diligence (particularly beneficial ownership of legal persons and arrangements) and, if available, transaction records on sales of dual-use or export-controlled goods. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "particularly the beneficial ownership of legal persons and arrangements"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Other firm-level sources include threat analysis reports, national PF risk assessments, supervisory circulars on PF-TFS breach cases, and internal control rules designed to identify designated persons and those acting for them. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "threat analysis reports, national PF risk assessments, and supervisory circulars"</sub>

## Indicators of the potential breach, non-implementation or evasion of PF-TFS

- **descriptive / definition**: A risk indicator is something that demonstrates or suggests the likelihood of unusual or suspicious activity. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A risk indicator demonstrates or suggests the likelihood of the occurrence of unusual or suspicious activity."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: A single standalone PF indicator may not alone warrant suspicion but could prompt further monitoring; several indicators, especially across multiple categories, could warrant closer examination. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the occurrence of several indicators (especially from multiple categories) could also warrant closer examination"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Whether indicators suggest proliferation finance depends on the institution's business lines, products or services, how it interacts with customers, and its human and technological resources. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "dependent on the business lines, products or services that an institution offers"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The PF indicators apply to public and private sectors, including FIs, DNFBPs and VASPs of any size, and are meant for staff in compliance, screening and monitoring, investigations, onboarding and relationship management. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "regardless of whether they are small and mid-size businesses or large conglomerates"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Some indicators require cross-comparing data such as financial transactions, customs data and open market prices held externally, so the private sector will not observe all indicators and may need context from authorities via public-private partnerships, law enforcement or FIUs. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the private sector will not observe all of the indicators identified below"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: When using the indicators, private sector entities should consider the totality of the customer profile, including CDD information, trade financing methods and other contextual risk factors; indicators vary in weight. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "take into consideration the totality of the customer profile"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Some indicators do not necessarily correspond to PF-TFS breach or evasion and are not mandatory; the list is non-exhaustive, highlights current prevalent indicators (e.g. shell companies) since the 2018 FATF CPF Guidance (Annex A), and should be read with Section 2 on risk mitigation. (p. 19)[^original]

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Customer profile indicator: at onboarding the customer gives vague or incomplete information about proposed trading activities and is reluctant to provide more when asked. (p. 19)[^original]

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Customer profile indicator: the customer is connected, e.g. through business or trade relations, with a country of proliferation or diversion concern. (p. 20)[^original]

(p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a small end-of-day balance without clear business reasons"</sub>

  <sub>anchor: "A customer affiliated with a university or research institution is involved in the trading"</sub>

  <sub>anchor: "account holders have previously violated requirements under dual-use or export control regimes"</sub>

## b) Vulnerabilities

- **recommended / method**: After listing PF threats, the next step is listing major PF vulnerabilities; countries and firms are encouraged to adapt their ML/TF vulnerability methodology, considering structural, sectoral, product/service, customer and transaction factors. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the next step is to compile a list of major PF vulnerabilities"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Identified PF vulnerabilities depend on country context and threats and will differ between countries and sectors. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the results will be different from country to country, as well as from sector to sector"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Structural vulnerabilities are weaknesses in the national CPF regime that make a country or firm attractive to designated persons or those acting for them. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Structural vulnerabilities refer to weaknesses in the national counter proliferation financing regime"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Structural vulnerability examples: weak governance, law enforcement, export controls or regulation, weak cross-agency PF risk knowledge, and weak AML/CFT/CPF regimes identified in FATF Statements or Mutual Evaluations. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "having weak governance, law enforcement, export controls and/or regulatory regimes"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Structural vulnerability examples: no legislative CPF framework or national CPF priorities, and implementation issues with UNSCR PF-TFS and FATF Standards (especially R.7 and IO.11). (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "lacking a legislative CPF framework and national CPF priorities"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Structural vulnerability examples: being subject to UN sanctions or embargoes, and significant organised crime, corruption or other criminality exploitable by designated persons. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "having significant levels of organised crime, corruption, or other criminal activities"</sub>

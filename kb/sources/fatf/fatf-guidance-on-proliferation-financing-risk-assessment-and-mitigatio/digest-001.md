## Background and context

- **mandatory / requirement**: In October 2020 FATF revised Recommendation 1 and its Interpretive Note to require countries and private sector entities to identify, assess, understand and mitigate their proliferation financing (PF) risks. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In October 2020, the FATF revised Recommendation 1 and its Interpretive Note (R.1 and INR.1)"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: In the context of R.1 and this Guidance, 'proliferation financing risk' refers strictly and only to the potential breach, non-implementation or evasion of the targeted financial sanctions (TFS) obligations in Recommendation 7. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "proliferation financing risk refers strictly and only to the potential breach, non-implementation or evasion of the targeted financial sanctions"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: FATF footnote 2: 'private sector entities' means financial institutions, DNFBPs and VASPs, and references to FIs and/or DNFBPs are also relevant to VASPs; footnote 1: 'countries' includes territories, jurisdictions or member states as referred to in UNSCRs. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "refer to financial institutions, designated non-financial businesses and professions (DNFBPs), and virtual asset service providers (VASPs)"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The revised FATF Standards require private sector entities to have processes to identify, assess, monitor, manage and mitigate PF risks. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "require private sector entities to have in place processes to identify, assess, monitor, manage and mitigate proliferation financing risks"</sub>
- **permissive / expectation**: Private sector entities may meet PF risk requirements within existing TFS and/or compliance programmes and are not expected to establish duplicative PF risk assessment or mitigation processes. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "are not expected to establish duplicative processes for proliferation financing risk assessment or mitigation"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF (para 3) says the Guidance aims to build common understanding of how to implement the new PF risk assessment and mitigation requirements given the rule-based nature of TFS under Recommendation 7. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "given the rule-based nature of the targeted financial sanctions under Recommendation 7"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: One source of PF risk is a potential breach or non-implementation of TFS, which may materialise when designated entities and individuals access financial services or assets, e.g., due to delayed national communication of designations or lack of clear private sector obligations. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "as a result, for example, of delay in communication of designations at the national level"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Private sector failings contributing to breach/non-implementation risk include weak onboarding and ongoing monitoring, lack of staff training, ineffective risk management, lack of a proper sanctions screening system or irregular or inflexible screening, and general lack of compliance culture. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "lack of a proper sanctions screening system or irregular or inflexible screening procedures"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: FATF footnote 3: R.7 is limited to implementing TFS and does not cover other UNSCR requirements (including UNSCR 1540 (2004)); FATF PF requirements are limited to Recommendations 1, 2, 7 and 15, and R.1 PF requirements do not expand other Recommendations' scope. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The requirements of the FATF Standards relating to proliferation financing are limited to Recommendations 1, 2, 7 and 15 only"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: FATF footnote 4: 'individuals' equally means 'persons' as in UNSCRs, and DPRK UNSCR obligations also cover persons or individuals acting on behalf of designated persons. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In the DPRK UNSCRs, obligations also refer to those “persons” or “individuals” acting on these designated persons/individuals’ behalf"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Another source of PF risk is evasion of TFS through concerted efforts by designated persons and entities, e.g., using shell or front companies, joint ventures, dummy accounts, middlemen and other fraudulent or sham intermediaries. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "by using shell or front companies, joint ventures, dummy accounts, middlemen and other fraudulent/sham intermediaries"</sub>

## Objectives and scope

- **descriptive / context**: FATF (para 5) states the non-binding Guidance aims to help public and private sectors identify, assess and understand PF risk as defined in R.1, mitigate identified PF risks, and to guide supervisors/SRBs on supervising PF risk assessment and mitigation. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to provide additional guidance to supervisors/self-regulatory bodies (SRBs) on supervision or monitoring"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: R.7 obligations apply to two country-specific regimes, DPRK and Iran, requiring countries to freeze without delay the funds or other assets of, and ensure no funds or assets are made available directly or indirectly to or for the benefit of, UN-designated persons or entities, those acting on their behalf or direction, and those they own or control. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These R.7 obligations apply to two country-specific regimes for the Democratic People’s Republic of Korea (DPRK) and Iran"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: FATF (para 7) notes the Guidance flags in footnotes broader counter-proliferation information (e.g., unclear links to DPRK or Iran designees, activity-based prohibitions mandatory for UN Member States) that is outside the FATF Recommendations, not required under R.1 and not assessed in mutual evaluations, though awareness may help avoid conflict or duplication. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is not required under R.1, and is not assessed in the FATF mutual evaluation or assessment process"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The R.1/INR.1 amendments do not change or extend existing private sector obligations under Recommendation 7 or the ML/TF obligations in Recommendations 9 to 23. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "do not change or extend the existing obligations on private sector entities with respect to Recommendation 7"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF (para 8) states the Guidance is non-binding, does not restrict national authorities' and private entities' freedom in PF risk assessment, and recognises there is no one-size-fits-all approach. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is no one-size-fits-all approach when assessing or mitigating proliferation financing risks"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Countries and private sector entities should implement measures considering the context, risk profile and materiality of sectors and institutions, so obligations are proportionate to risk and consistent with objectives such as financial inclusion. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "proportionate to the risks faced by relevant entities, and be consistent with other complementary objectives such as financial inclusion"</sub>
- **permissive / requirement**: FATF (para 9) says countries may exempt a particular type of FI, DNFBP or VASP from PF risk assessment and mitigation requirements if there is a proven low PF risk; they should use this flexibility in a timely way considering financial exclusion and should monitor exemptions as risk profiles change. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "provided there is a proven low risk of proliferation financing relating to such private sector entities"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF (para 10) states this Guidance does not supersede the 2018 FATF Guidance on Counter Proliferation Financing, which remains relevant except for the new R.1/INR.1 PF risk assessment and mitigation obligations. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This Guidance does not supersede or replace the _2018 FATF Guidance on Counter Proliferation Financing_"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: FATF (para 11) acknowledges countries and firms may assess PF risk more widely than TFS breach/evasion; this is outside FATF requirements and assessments, but they may continue such wider assessments and mitigation under their own frameworks. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may choose to assess their exposure to proliferation financing risks in a wider context"</sub>

## Target audience, status, and contents

- **descriptive / context**: FATF (para 12) says the Guidance targets countries and competent authorities including supervisors, FIs and DNFBPs, and VASPs not classified as FIs or DNFBPs. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Virtual Asset Service Providers (VASPs) if they are not classified as financial institutions or DNFBPs"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Guidance, focused on the October 2020 R.1/INR.1 obligations, has three sections (assessment, mitigation, and supervision of PF risk) and was adopted by FATF in June 2021. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF adopted the present Guidance in June 2021"</sub>

## Objectives and scope

- **recommended / recommendation**: FATF (para 9) says countries should consider using this exemption flexibility in a timely and responsive manner to take into account financial exclusion concerns. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Countries should consider using this flexibility in a timely and responsive manner"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: FATF (para 9) says that because risk profiles can change over time, countries should monitor such exemptions. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "As risk profiles can change over time, countries should monitor such exemptions"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Even where such an exemption applies, full application of the targeted financial sanctions required by Recommendation 7 is mandatory in all cases. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "full application of the targeted financial sanctions as required by Recommendation 7 is mandatory in all cases"</sub>

## Introduction

- **descriptive / context**: FATF (para 15) states regularly identifying, assessing and understanding PF risks is essential to prevent designated persons and entities involved in WMD proliferation from raising, storing, moving and using funds, and implementing PF-related TFS is essential for a stronger CPF regime. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "from raising, storing, moving, and using funds, and thus other financial assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: FATF footnote 5: UNSCR operative paragraphs oblige member states to impose TFS on designated persons and entities and those acting on their behalf, at their direction, or owned or controlled by them; the Guidance uses 'designated persons and entities' as shorthand. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This guidance document uses “designated persons and entities” as a shorthand"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: FATF (para 16) notes R.1 requires countries to designate an authority or mechanism to co-ordinate risk assessment actions and apply resources to mitigate risks effectively, and since October 2020 to identify, assess, understand and mitigate PF risks for the country and private sector. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "require countries to designate an authority or mechanism to co-ordinate actions to assess risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF footnote 6: the section builds on the 2018 CPF Guidance, 2013 NRA Guidance, 2019 TF Risk Assessment Guidance, 2008 PF Report, 2010 PF status report, and UNSC Panel of Experts reports. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "as well as reports from United Nations Security Council (UNSC) Panel of Experts (PoE)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF (para 17) says the Standards do not prescribe a risk assessment methodology, there should be no one-size-fits-all approach to assessing PF-TFS risks, and an approach effective for one jurisdiction or firm may not suit others. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An effective approach for one jurisdiction or one private sector firm will not necessarily be effective for others"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: FATF (para 18) says PF-TFS risk assessments may be part of broader NRAs or stand-alone, and the Standards do not require assessing broader PF risks. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FATF Standards do not require a risk assessment of broader PF risks"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: A PF-TFS risk assessment need not use an entirely new methodology or be stand-alone if existing ML/TF methodologies can adequately incorporate PF risks. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It needs not require a stand-alone risk assessment if pre-existing risk assessment methodologies are adequate to incorporate PF risks"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: FATF (para 19) says countries and the private sector should share a common understanding of key concepts before a PF risk assessment, drawing on the 2013 NRA Guidance, 2019 TFRA Guidance and 2018 CPF Guidance. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries and private sector should have a common understanding of key concepts before conducting a proliferation financing risk assessment"</sub>

## Risk

- **descriptive / definition**: FATF (para 20) states PF risk, like ML/TF risk, can be seen as a function of three factors: threat, vulnerability and consequence, applied to potential breach, non-implementation or evasion of R.7 TFS. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "can be seen as a function of three factors: threat, vulnerability, and consequence"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: FATF footnote 7: broader PF risks (not covered by R.1) are the risk of WMD proliferation and of its financing; WMD proliferation covers manufacture, acquisition, possession, development, export, trans-shipment, brokering, transport, transfer, stockpiling or use of nuclear, chemical or biological weapons, means of delivery and related materials including dual-use goods and technologies for non-legitimate purposes. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "WMD proliferation refers to the manufacture, acquisition, possession, development, export, trans-shipment, brokering, transport, transfer, stockpiling or use"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: FATF footnote 7 defines financing of proliferation as raising, moving or making available funds, other assets, economic resources or financing to persons or entities for WMD proliferation purposes; understanding it is not required but may help understand PF-TFS risk and implement risk-based measures. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The financing of proliferation refers to the risk of raising, moving, or making available funds, other assets or other economic resources"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: FATF (para 21a) defines inherent risk as the natural level of risk before introducing controls; understanding it is not required by the Standards but is beneficial for judging whether controls are effective. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Inherent risk refers to the natural level of risk, prior to introducing any measures to mitigate"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Country-level inherent PF risk factors include close links with DPRK or Iran designated persons and entities, production of dual-use or export-controlled goods and their trade patterns, and loopholes in regulations implementing relevant UNSCRs. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "level of production of dual use goods or goods subject to export controls in the country, and trade patterns of such products"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Firm-level inherent PF risk factors include the nature, types and complexity of services, customer types, geographic distribution of customers and/or beneficial owners, and distribution channels. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "geographical distribution of its customers and/or beneficial owners, and channels of distribution"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: FATF (para 21b) defines residual risk as risk remaining after mitigation; a high residual risk may suggest inadequate controls requiring remedial action, e.g., FIs, DNFBPs or VASPs still unable to identify sanctioned persons after enhanced screening. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "cannot identify the sanctioned individuals/entities even after introducing enhanced screening measures"</sub>

## Threat, Vulnerability, and Consequence

- **descriptive / definition**: FATF (para 22a) defines PF threat as designated persons and entities that have caused or could evade, breach or exploit a failure to implement PF-TFS, including those acting for them; threats may be actual or potential and do not present the same risk to all countries and firms. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Threat refers to designated persons and entities that have previously caused or with the potential to evade, breach or exploit"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF footnote 8: DPRK PF-TFS under UNSCR 1718 (2006) OP8(d) covers persons or entities acting on behalf or at the direction of designated persons and entities. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "DPRK PF-TFS, i.e. UNSCR 1718 (2006) OP8(d), covers persons or entities acting on behalf or at the direction"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: FATF (para 22b) defines vulnerability as matters the threat can exploit or that facilitate breach, non-implementation or evasion of PF-TFS; for countries this includes weaknesses in CPF laws or contextual features enabling designated persons to raise or move funds. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Vulnerability refers to matters that can be exploited by the threat or that may support or facilitate the breach"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Country vulnerability examples: weak AML/CFT controls, not collecting beneficial ownership information on locally incorporated entities, or high levels of crime, smuggling, fraud or other illicit activity; for firms, sector, product or service features attractive for PF-TFS breach or evasion. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "does not collect information about the beneficial owners of entities incorporated under its laws"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: FATF (para 22c) defines consequence as the outcome where funds or assets reach designated persons, potentially allowing them to source materials for illicit nuclear, chemical or biological weapons or delivery systems, or where frozen assets are used without authorisation for PF. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Consequence refers to the outcome where funds or assets are made available to designated persons and entities"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Breach, non-implementation or evasion of PF-TFS may also cause reputational damage and punitive measures such as UN and/or national sanction designations. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "punitive measures such as sanction designations by the UN and/or national authorities"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The ultimate consequence of PF, the threat or use of a WMD, is more severe than ML or other financial crimes, closer to the potential loss of life from TF, and likely differs between countries, channels or sources. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is more severe than that of ML or other financial crimes, and is more similar to the potential loss of life"</sub>

## Stages of PF Risk Assessment

- **descriptive / definition**: FATF (para 23) describes a PF risk assessment as a product or process based on an agreed methodology to identify, analyse and understand PF risks in order to develop measures reducing risk to a lower or acceptable level, making informed judgments on threats, vulnerabilities and consequences. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "with a view to developing appropriate measures to mitigate or reduce an assessed level of risk to a lower or acceptable level"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: A national PF risk assessment should be comprehensive enough to inform national CPF strategies, support risk-based measures for PF-TFS, and help prioritise mitigation resources. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it should be comprehensive enough to inform national counter proliferation financing strategies"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The ultimate goal of a PF risk assessment is full implementation of PF-TFS under relevant UNSCRs, and it is likely more targeted in scope than an ML/TF risk assessment because the assessed risk is narrower. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a PF risk assessment may likely to be more targeted than an ML/TF risk assessment"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: FATF (para 24) says a PF risk assessment may follow the same six stages as ML/TF assessments: (1) preliminary scoping; (2) planning and organisation; (3) identification of threats and vulnerabilities; (4) analysis; (5) evaluation and follow-up; (6) update. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "(1) preliminary scoping; (2) planning and organisation; (3) identification of threats and vulnerabilities; (4) analysis"</sub>

## Preliminary Scoping

- **descriptive / finding or result**: FATF (para 25) notes that before the October 2020 amendments only a limited number of countries and firms had completed a PF risk assessment; footnote 10 names Cayman Islands, Gibraltar, Latvia, Portugal and the United States as having published one, not assessed in FATF evaluations. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "They are Cayman Islands, Gibraltar, Latvia, Portugal and the United States"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Countries and firms are strongly encouraged to start with a scoping exercise defining objectives, scope, focus and methodology, considering national threat profile, vulnerabilities, CPF context and strategies, and sector, company and customer profiles. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "are strongly encouraged to conduct a scoping exercise first to determine the objectives, scope, and focus of the assessment"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF footnote 11: for a national assessment, scoping may include deciding whether PF risk is assessed stand-alone or within a broader NRA including ML and TF. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "whether the PF risk is to be assessed standalone, or as part of a broader NRA"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: FATF (para 26, based on review of published MERs) notes literature on typologies of PF-TFS breach, non-implementation or evasion is limited, so a contextual analysis during scoping may be beneficial. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Given the limited literature on typologies of the breach, non-implementation or evasion of PF-TFS"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: Contextual analysis may review methods, trends and typologies in UNSC Panel of Experts reports on DPRK and Iran, other jurisdictions' PF risk assessments, TFS evasion typologies, recent cases and examples from tertiary institutes, applied to the national or business context. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identified in the UNSC Panels of Experts (PoE) on DPRK and Iran’s reports"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Countries and firms should identify information and data gaps to address during the risk assessment process. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should also identify information and data gaps that they should attempt to address"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF footnote 9 refers readers to Part 2 of the 2013 NRA Guidance and Part 1 of the 2019 TFRA Guidance for scoping and planning, and Parts 4 and 5 of the NRA Guidance for identification, analysis and outcome stages. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Countries and private sector are encouraged to refer to Part 2 of the _2013 FATF NRA Guidance_"</sub>

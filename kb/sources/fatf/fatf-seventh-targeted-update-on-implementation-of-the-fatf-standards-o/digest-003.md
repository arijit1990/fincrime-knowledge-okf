## 2.4. Offshore VASPs

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Some oVASPs actively solicit customers in jurisdictions where they are unlicensed or VA activity is restricted, including by advising users to circumvent regulatory requirements via VPNs or by providing false information. Affiliate schemes, sponsoring of local events, and promotion via social media and encrypted messaging apps are commonly used to reach customers, reinforcing the appeal of offshore platforms among users with limited awareness of licensing and regulatory status. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "advising users to circumvent regulatory requirements through the use of VPNs or the provision of false information"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Other oVASPs may actively lower KYC requirements and offer advantageous pricing by leveraging lower compliance costs, undermining a level playing field for regulated VASPs. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "actively lower KYC requirements and offer advantageous pricing by leveraging lower compliance costs"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Where host VASPs maintain weak AML/CFT/CPF controls, the risks of nested oVASP activity are materially amplified, including exposure to sanctioned actors and enforcement action. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Where host VASPs maintain weak AML/CFT/CPF controls, the risks associated with nested oVASP activity are materially amplified"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Regulatory arbitrage is an additional structural risk: oVASPs may incorporate or route customer activity through jurisdictions with weak supervisory frameworks to minimise compliance obligations, including CDD and Travel Rule requirements, distorting competition and undermining domestic regulatory frameworks by converting lower compliance costs into pricing advantages. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "oVASPs may incorporate or route customer activity through jurisdictions with weak supervisory frameworks"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Jurisdictions have developed measures to detect, regulate and enforce obligations against oVASPs. Detection draws on a combination of blockchain analytics, open-source intelligence and suspicious transaction reports from onshore VASPs and FIs. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Detection efforts draw on a combination of blockchain analytics, open-source intelligence"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Nested and intermediated arrangements are a further vulnerability: oVASPs obtain trading, liquidity and fiat on- and off-ramps through accounts at licensed onshore VASPs or FIs. Although nesting can be legitimate, oVASPs have been seen deliberately presenting themselves as retail users to open such accounts, then handling illicit transactions far above typical retail volumes while making up only a marginal share of the host VASP's total activity. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "oVASPs have been observed deliberately misrepresenting themselves as retail users when opening such accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: To improve regulatory clarity, a growing number of jurisdictions license on an activity basis: oVASPs must register wherever they actively offer services, whether or not they are physically present. Regulatory anchors include targeted marketing, onboarding of residents and use of domestic payment rails. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "requiring oVASPs to register where they actively provide services regardless of physical presence"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: To ensure licensing includes meaningful in-country substance, some jurisdictions also require oVASPs to maintain a material local presence, including a compliance officer with unrestricted access to customer data and sufficient seniority to act independently. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "material local presence, including a compliance officer with unrestricted access to customer data"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Where oVASPs remain unresponsive or dispute territorial scope, jurisdictions have applied graduated enforcement ranging from public warnings and app-store takedowns to restricting access to domestic financial intermediaries and criminal prosecution. Effective responses also relied on domestic coordination across supervisory, FIU, law enforcement and tax authorities, and international cooperation through supervisor-to-supervisor channels or, where unavailable, diagonal cooperation via FIU networks such as the Egmont Group. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "graduated enforcement measures ranging from public warnings and app-store takedowns"</sub>

## 2.5. Decentralised Finance (DeFi)

- **descriptive / limitation**: Jurisdictions across the FATF Global Network continue to face significant challenges identifying individuals or entities exercising control or sufficient influence over DeFi arrangements, so most have not in practice imposed licensing or registration requirements on DeFi arrangements within the scope of the FATF Standards. Shortcomings persist in assessing ML/TF/PF risks of the DeFi ecosystem, a potentially growing gap as FIs and VASPs expand their engagement with DeFi platforms. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "most jurisdictions have not, in practice, imposed licensing or registration requirements on DeFi arrangements"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: 2026 survey: only a minority (18%; 26 of 142) of responding jurisdictions have assessed risks associated with DeFi arrangements, and 9% (12 of 142) are conducting a risk assessment that includes DeFi-related risks. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Only a minority (18%; 26 of 142) have assessed the risks associated with DeFi arrangements"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: 2026 survey: a large majority (93%; 132 of 142) have not identified DeFi arrangements operating in their territory that qualify as VASPs under the FATF Standards. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A large majority (93%; 132 of 142) have not identified DeFi arrangements operating in their territory"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: 2026 survey: some jurisdictions (31%; 44 of 142) reported their risk mitigation measures apply to DeFi arrangements, but only four have imposed licensing or registration requirements and just two have actually licensed or registered such arrangements. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "only four jurisdictions have imposed licensing or registration requirements, and just two have actually licensed or registered"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Jurisdictions report persistent difficulty identifying qualifying DeFi arrangements within their regulatory perimeter because of their cross-border nature, frequent lack of formal legal structures and absence of clear regulatory anchors; supervisors also face resource and capacity constraints in developing the technical expertise to determine where control or sufficient influence is exercised. Enforcement is low, with only one jurisdiction reporting enforcement actions in practice. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "due to their cross-border nature, frequent lack of formal legal structures, and the absence of clear regulatory anchors"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FATF has produced a Targeted Report on Regulatory Challenges from Decentralised Finance (DeFi), analysing the characteristics, vulnerabilities and ML/TF/PF risks of DeFi arrangements and offering guidance on how competent authorities can identify control or sufficient influence and apply licensing and registration requirements when appropriate. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FATF has produced a Targeted Report on Regulatory Challenges from Decentralised Finance (DeFi)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In the 2026 survey, 50 of 142 jurisdictions (35%) said their competent authorities had held engagement with the private sector in order to understand DeFi business models and their risks better. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Some jurisdictions (35%; 50 of 142) reported that their competent authorities have engaged with the private sector"</sub>

## SECTION THREE: Next Steps for the FATF and VACG

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The FATF's work on VAs and VASPs in the previous biennium highlighted inconsistent implementation of R.15. Persistent gaps include insufficient risk assessments, inconsistent application of the travel rule, and limited domestic and international cooperation for effective supervision, investigations and confiscation of VAs, which is particularly concerning as VAs are increasingly used for payments and investment and typologies show misuse for sanction evasion, fraud and TF/PF. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Persistent gaps include insufficient risk assessments, inconsistent application of the travel rule"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FATF will continue to monitor R.15 implementation and emerging risks in the virtual assets ecosystem, with particular attention to jurisdictions with significant VASP sectors, using its existing toolkit including assessment, risk analysis and capacity building. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF will continue to monitor the implementation of R.15 and emerging risks within the virtual assets ecosystem"</sub>

## Annex A. Updated Table: Status of Implementation of Recommendation 15

- **descriptive / context**: Annex A states that virtual assets are inherently international and borderless, so failure to regulate VASPs in one jurisdiction can have serious global implications. It notes Cambodia-based scam compounds generate multi-billion USD annually through "pig butchering" investment fraud, a single Cambodia-based conglomerate is reported to have laundered at least USD 4 billion between 2021 and 2025, scammers are often lured or trafficked into compounds and coerced, and the DPRK has exploited the same infrastructure to launder stolen VAs supporting its WMD and ballistic missile programmes. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Virtual assets (VAs) are inherently international and borderless, meaning a failure to regulate VASPs in one jurisdiction"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The FATF published the first R.15 implementation table in March 2024, based on self-reported information, covering FATF members and 20 jurisdictions with materially important VASP activities. The 2025 update added 9 non-FATF jurisdictions that newly met the materiality criteria and the 2026 update identifies 2 further jurisdictions; the jurisdictions covered account for approximately 97 percent of the global VA market. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These jurisdictions covered in the table account for approximately 97 percent of the global VA market."</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The table is based on responses to the FATF's 2026 self-reported survey, with relevant questions posed with three possible answers (Yes/No/In Progress); from February to June 2026 all jurisdictions were asked to provide up-to-date progress information. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "alongside a selection of three possible answer choices (Yes/No/In Progress)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: While most jurisdictions report significant progress in fundamental R.15 areas, particularly conducting risk assessments and licensing/registering VASPs, gaps remain in taking supervisory and/or enforcement actions against VASPs; recent MER/FUR results also suggest effective R.15 implementation remains challenging. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "gaps remain in taking supervisory and/or enforcement actions against VASPs"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: VAs are also being used to an increasing extent worldwide to raise and transfer funds by terrorist groups such as ISIL, Al-Qaeda and their affiliates, and by ethnically or racially motivated terrorist entities. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "are also increasingly using VAs to raise and move funds globally"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The table aims to enable the FATF network to support jurisdictions in regulating and supervising VASPs for AML/CFT and to encourage those with materially important VASP activity to fully implement R.15 in a timely manner; the FATF and VACG will continue outreach and assistance, particularly to lower-capacity jurisdictions, with FSRB Secretariats and international organisations providing technical assistance and training. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "encourage jurisdictions with materially important VASP activity to fully implement R.15 in a timely manner"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In total, 2 non-FATF jurisdictions newly met the inclusion criteria: 1 met the trading volume criterion and 1 met the user base criterion. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "1 non-FATF jurisdictions met the first criterion (trading volume) and 1 met the second criterion (user base)"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Subject to their own ML/TF/PF risk assessment, jurisdictions may also consider designating all VASPs from jurisdictions that do not effectively implement licensing or registration requirements as higher-risk (FATF 2021 guidance, para. 199). (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions may also consider designating all VASPs from jurisdictions which do not effectively implement licensing or registration requirements as higher-risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Neither the open-source data used to compile the table nor the blockchain analytics data used for verification is based on any assessment of a jurisdiction's illicit finance risks associated with VAs or of compliance with the FATF standards, so inclusion in the table carries no indication, positive or negative, about a jurisdiction's risk or its level of compliance with R.15. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "carries no indication – either positive or negative – regarding that jurisdiction’s degree of risk or its level of compliance with R.15"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The purpose of the exercise remains the same as in 2024: to identify jurisdictions with materially important virtual asset sectors so the FATF network can best support them in regulating and supervising VASPs for AML/CFT purposes and encourage timely full implementation of Recommendation 15. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The purpose of this exercise remains the same as in 2024."</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The FATF built the updated table on the methodology adopted at the June 2023 Plenary. The first of its two criteria covers jurisdictions with materially important VASP activity, measured by trading volume above 0.25% of global trading; the second (applied alternatively or in addition) covers jurisdictions with a large VA user base, namely the top 30 by VA ownership and adoption rates. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "based on trading volume (over 0.25% of global trading)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Although the methodology and criteria are unchanged, the updated table applies a different user-base inclusion threshold from the previous table, because open-source information on user numbers, which the earlier threshold relied on, is no longer freely accessible. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the updated table uses a different inclusion threshold for userbase"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: In line with the FATF's 2021 Updated Guidance on a Risk-Based Approach for VASPs, jurisdictions should consider the risks of VA transfers involving jurisdictions that have not taken steps towards regulating or banning VASPs. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions should consider the risks of virtual asset transfers with jurisdictions that have not taken steps"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Subject to their own ML/TF/PF risk assessment, jurisdictions may also consider designating all VASPs from jurisdictions that do not effectively implement licensing or registration requirements as higher-risk. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions may also consider designating all VASPs from jurisdictions which do not effectively implement licensing or registration requirements as higher-risk"</sub>

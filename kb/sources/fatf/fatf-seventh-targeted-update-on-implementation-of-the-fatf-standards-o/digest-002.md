## 2.4.1. Developing and implementing a regime for VASPs

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The proportion of surveyed jurisdictions that have identified how they intend to regulate the VA sector rose from 82% in 2025 (134 of 163) to 89% (128 of 144) in 2026. A minority still report not having decided if and how to regulate: 18% in 2025 (29 of 163) falling to 11% in 2026 (16 of 144). (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "increasing from 82% in 2025 (134 of 163 jurisdictions) to 89% (128 of 144 jurisdictions) in 2026"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Most jurisdictions (66%; 95 of 144 respondents) have decided to permit the use of VAs and the operation of VASPs, while 23% of respondents reported opting to prohibit VASPs, rising from 11% in 2023 (16 of 151), 14% in 2024 (20 of 147) and 20% in 2025 (33 of 163). (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "23% of respondents reported opting to prohibit VASPs, indicating a steady increase over the past few years"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: While a prohibition is permissible under the FATF Standards, it may be difficult to implement effectively unless jurisdictions proactively identify prohibited VA/VASP activities and apply supervisory and enforcement measures to prevent and sanction them; an increase in prohibitions may raise concerns in future if jurisdictions cannot enforce them effectively. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "While a prohibition is permissible under the FATF Standards"</sub>

## 2.4.1. Developing and implementing a regime for VASPs

- **descriptive / finding or result**: A prohibition approach appears more common in certain regions. As in the 2025 report, members of MENAFATF (Middle East and North Africa) have more commonly chosen a partial or total prohibition approach than members of other FSRBs. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A prohibition approach appears to be more common in certain regions."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Among jurisdictions taking a prohibition approach, 36% (12 of 33) partially prohibit specific VA/VASP activities and 64% (21 of 33) fully prohibit all VA/VASP activities. The partial share is higher than in the 2024 report (30%; 6 of 20) but lower than in 2025 (48%; 16 of 33), suggesting no clear upward trend toward partial prohibition. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "suggesting no clear upward trend toward partial prohibition"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Partial prohibition approaches impose targeted restrictions rather than full bans: use of VAs as a means of payment is frequently prohibited while limited investment or trading use is allowed under controlled frameworks. Many jurisdictions incorporate VASPs into AML/CFT regimes with licensing, registration and supervision, while adding restrictions on higher-risk activities such as anonymity-enhancing services, privacy coins, mining or custody. Some maintain outright or de facto bans; others are developing frameworks via new legislation, sandbox initiatives or alignment with models such as MiCA. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the use of virtual assets as a means of payment is frequently prohibited"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Overall, there is a converging regulatory pattern toward restricting payment functions while integrating virtual asset activities into formal regulatory and supervisory systems. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is a converging regulatory pattern toward restricting payment functions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Assessment results indicate that successfully prohibiting the use of VAs and VASPs is still challenging. Six jurisdictions partially or fully prohibiting VAs and VASPs have been assessed as largely compliant with the FATF standards, and although more prohibition-approach jurisdictions have conducted risk assessments, many still struggle to conduct comprehensive and effective ones. Only one jurisdiction taking a prohibition approach has fully met the relevant R.15 requirement (sub-criterion 15.3). (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "only one jurisdiction taking a prohibition approach has fully met the relevant R.15 requirement"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Jurisdictions with a prohibition approach reported increased supervisory or enforcement activity against VASPs operating illegally: 16 of 21 jurisdictions explicitly prohibiting VAs and VASPs reported such actions, compared with 9 of 17 in the 2025 Targeted Update and 9 of 14 in 2024; implementation remains uneven. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "16 of 21 jurisdictions explicitly prohibiting the use of VAs and VASPs reported taking such actions"</sub>

## 2.4.2. Licensing/Registering VASPs and taking enforcement and supervision actions

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Jurisdictions have developed VASP licensing or registration frameworks but operational implementation is uneven. 73% of respondents (95 of 130), excluding those that prohibit or plan to prohibit VASPs entirely, require VASPs to be licensed or registered, but only 58% (76 of 130) reported having licensed or registered a VASP in practice in 2026, compared with 65% (76 of 117) in 2025. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "58% of respondents (76 of 130) reported having licensed or registered a VASP in practice"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Assessment results show only 40% of assessed jurisdictions (59 of 149) satisfactorily require VASPs to be licensed or registered (criterion 15.4 rated met or mostly met). This is similar to the 2025 Targeted Update findings, which noted assessment results may not fully reflect updates in survey responses; the difference may also reflect methodological differences or inconsistencies in self-reported survey data. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "only 40% of assessed jurisdictions (59 of 149) satisfactorily require VASPs to be licensed or registered"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Of the 95 jurisdictions reporting VASP licensing/registration requirements, 81% (77 of 95) reported conducting supervisory inspections of VASPs (up from 73%, 70 of 96, in 2025) and 71% (67 of 95) reported enforcement or other administrative actions against VASPs, slightly lower than 73% (70 of 96) in 2025. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supervisory inspections of VASPs (81%; 77 of 95) and taking enforcement actions or other administrative actions against VASPs (71%; 67 of 95)"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The inspection and enforcement data may require further analysis given the mismatch between survey and FATF assessment data and some confusing survey responses; for example, some countries reported conducting an inspection but also noted they had not licensed or registered a VASP in practice. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "some countries reported conducting an inspection but also noted that they had not licensed or registered a VASP in practice"</sub>

## 2.4.2. Licensing/Registering VASPs and taking enforcement and supervision actions

- **expected / requirement**: The FATF Standards state that jurisdictions should require VASPs to be licensed or registered if they are physically located or incorporated in their jurisdiction. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions should require VASPs to be licensed or registered if they are physically located or incorporated in their jurisdiction"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: About two-fifths of jurisdictions with VASP licensing or registration requirements (44%; 50 of 114) report meeting this standard (licensing VASPs created as legal persons or located as natural persons in their jurisdictions), and about a third (34%; 39 of 114) take a more extensive approach, requiring offshore VASPs that are not created or located there to be licensed/registered if they meet certain other requirements. The report refers readers to the targeted report Understanding and Mitigating the Risks of Offshore Virtual Asset Service Providers. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Approximately a third of jurisdictions (34%; 39 of 114) reported taking a more extensive approach"</sub>

## 2.5. Implementation of the FATF's Travel Rule

- **descriptive / finding or result**: In the 2026 survey, 83% of respondents (91 of 109 jurisdictions) have passed legislation implementing the Travel Rule, up from 73% (85 of 117) in 2025; an additional 11 of 109 are in the process, compared with 14 of 117 in 2025. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "83% of respondents (91 of 109 jurisdictions) have passed legislation implementing the Travel Rule"</sub>
- **mandatory / definition**: The Travel Rule extends the payment transparency requirements of FATF Recommendation 16 to VAs. It obliges VASPs and financial institutions, when they carry out payments or value transfers (VAs and hybrid payment chains included), to obtain, keep and pass on specified originator and beneficiary details immediately and securely. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Travel Rule requires VASPs and financial institutions to obtain, hold, and transmit specific originator and beneficiary information"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Experience in supervising and enforcing the Travel Rule remains limited though improved on 2025: 60% (55 of 91) of jurisdictions that have passed Travel Rule legislation have not yet issued findings or directives or taken enforcement or other supervisory actions against VASPs on Travel Rule compliance, likely reflecting recent enactment and a focus on establishing supervision frameworks. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Slightly more than half of the 91 jurisdictions (60%; 55 of 91)"</sub>

## 2.5. Implementation of the FATF's Travel Rule

- **descriptive / context**: Jurisdictions may also be engaging with VASPs, have ongoing enforcement cases, or be working with VASPs to remediate shortcomings; the low number of enforcement actions may also reflect the challenges detailed in the FATF's Best Practices in Travel Rule Supervision report. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Jurisdictions may also be focusing on engaging with VASPs, have ongoing enforcement cases"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Persistent gaps in Travel Rule implementation remain a serious concern. Because Travel Rule effectiveness depends on consistent, effective and global implementation and enforcement, jurisdictions that have introduced it should rapidly operationalise it, including through effective supervision and enforcement in case of non-compliance; the FATF's Best Practice in Travel Rule Supervision paper provides further guidance. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions that have introduced the Travel Rule should rapidly operationalise it"</sub>

## 2.6. FATF work to accelerate global implementation of R.15

- **descriptive / context**: Beyond VACG Roadmap monitoring of R.15 implementation and updating the table of jurisdictions with materially important VASP activity, the FATF Secretariat participated in several FSRB plenaries and other outreach. A VACG symposium in December 2025 attended by more than 400 participants from over 80 jurisdictions and FATF observer organisations aimed to highlight offshore VASP risks, examine regulation and supervision challenges, identify emerging good practices and gather feedback on FATF's ongoing work. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "More than 400 participants from over 80 jurisdictions and FATF observer organisations attended the 2025 December VACG symposium."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The symposium built on a September 2025 virtual VACG meeting on stablecoins and offshore VASPs, whose key issues included growing ML/TF/PF risks of stablecoins, particularly through unhosted wallets, and the challenges of supervising and obtaining information on offshore VASPs exploiting regulatory arbitrage. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the growing ML/TF/PF risks of stablecoins, particularly through unhosted wallets"</sub>

## 2.1.1 Organised Crime Groups' VA-Enabled Fraud

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Organised crime groups such as Cambodia-based scam centres have emerged as significant generators of illicit VA proceeds, commonly via investment fraud schemes known as "pig butchering" that use sustained social engineering to induce victims to transfer funds to fraudulent investment platforms, which are then diverted to criminal-controlled wallets. Competent authorities and international organisations indicate such activity generates multi-billion USD proceeds annually, rapidly laundered through complex VA transaction chains involving multiple unhosted wallets, OTC brokers and regionally based financial service providers. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These operations, commonly associated with investment fraud schemes often referred to as “pig butchering,”"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Competent authorities found that a Cambodia-based financial services conglomerate laundered at least USD 4 billion in illicit proceeds between August 2021 and January 2025, acting as a critical node connecting cyber-enabled fraud networks, underground banking infrastructure and VA-based money laundering pipelines; linked entities have been subject to sanctions and law enforcement actions in multiple jurisdictions. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "laundered at least USD 4 billion in illicit proceeds between August 2021 and January 2025"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The same Cambodia-based conglomerate was extensively exploited by DPRK-affiliated actors, a major PF threat, to launder virtual assets stolen through state-sponsored cyberattacks. Of the USD 4 billion processed, at least USD 37 million is attributed to DPRK cyber heists, with proceeds assessed as directly supporting DPRK WMD and ballistic missile programmes in violation of applicable UN Security Council Resolutions. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "at least USD 37 million has been attributed to DPRK cyber heists"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The intersection of OCG-driven fraud and proliferation financing within one intermediary illustrates the convergence risk in jurisdictions with deficient AML/CFT frameworks and shows that regionally embedded entities with inadequate KYC and AML controls can simultaneously serve multiple categories of illicit actor. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "illustrates the convergence risk inherent to jurisdictions with deficient AML/CFT frameworks"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The Borrelli network used a Hong Kong-based corporate and banking structure to present itself as a legitimate foreign exchange and virtual asset investment business, collecting funds via cash, bank transfers and VA transfers and moving proceeds through payment gateways and exchange accounts held under different names. The case shows VAs can serve both as the fraud lure and as a laundering channel, and the increasingly transnational and segmented structure of organised fraud networks. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "moving the proceeds through payment gateways and exchange accounts held under different names"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Operation Borrelli: in June 2025 Spain's Guardia Civil, backed by Europol and by authorities in Estonia, France and the United States, broke up a cryptocurrency investment fraud network alleged to have laundered roughly EUR 460 million taken from over 5 000 victims around the world. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "allegedly laundered approximately EUR 460 million from more than 5 000 victims worldwide"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The cases reflect the increasing industrialisation of VA-enabled fraud and its structural convergence with human trafficking, PF, sanctions evasion and cross-border money laundering, posing ongoing detection and disruption challenges for authorities, financial institutions and VASPs and systemic risks from uneven FATF Standards implementation, particularly for VA service providers in or through jurisdictions with weak supervision. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "increasing industrialisation of VA-enabled fraud and its structural convergence with human trafficking"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: The report states that stronger multilateral enforcement, enhanced information-sharing mechanisms and accelerated implementation of FATF Recommendation 15 across all relevant jurisdictions remain critical to disrupting this typology at scale. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "accelerated implementation of FATF Recommendation 15 across all relevant jurisdictions remain critical"</sub>

## 2.1.2. Cross-Cutting Risks in VA-Enabled Illicit Finance

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The DPRK continues to exploit vulnerabilities in VASPs and DeFi protocols and in wider third-party infrastructure those services rely on, including RPC nodes and multi-signature security providers, while laundering proceeds through bridges, unhosted wallets and OTC networks. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "including RPC nodes and multi-signature security providers"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Terrorist financing trends noted include use of Tron-based USDT, services that obscure gas-fee funding patterns and make investigations harder, and crypto donation campaigns linked to lone actors and small cells that may provide early indicators of planned attacks. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the use of Tron-based USDT, as well as the emergence of services that obscure gas-fee funding patterns"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: According to private sector dialogue, digital-asset risks of proliferation financing, terrorist financing and sanctions evasion are increasingly converging on common infrastructure and repeatable operating models. Sanctions evasion networks are shifting away from domestic exchanges towards distributed offshore intermediaries, global exchanges, OTC brokers and stablecoin payment rails, which can additionally fund proxy groups and buy weapons, drone components and dual-use goods. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Sanctions evasion networks are moving from domestic exchanges toward distributed offshore intermediaries"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: AI coding assistants and AI agents may be used to analyse smart contract vulnerabilities, generate exploit code and identify attack paths at the internal transaction level, increasing the speed and precision of attacks on DeFi protocols and cross-chain infrastructure. Stolen assets can be rapidly moved and fragmented through stablecoins, DEXs, bridges and multiple VASPs, making tracing and freezing harder, and open-weight AI models and self-hosted GPU infrastructure may let criminals bypass commercial AI safeguards and automate social engineering, exploit development and fund-flow design. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The use of open-weight AI models and self-hosted GPU infrastructure may further enable criminals"</sub>

## 2.2. Stablecoins

- **descriptive / case example**: Within VA-enabled fraud ecosystems, a Cambodia-based conglomerate identified as a key ML node for transnational organised crime launched a USD-pegged proprietary stablecoin marketed as immune to asset freezing and outside traditional regulatory oversight. This contrasts with some legitimate issuers that retain the technical ability to block transactions or freeze and seize/burn tokens at competent authorities' request. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "launched a USD-pegged stablecoin marketed as immune to asset freezing"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The capability gap was exploited after a third-party stablecoin issuer froze assets valued at over USD 29 million in a wallet linked to the same conglomerate due to suspected illicit activity, which the report describes as a direct causal link between regulatory exposure and deliberate development of freeze-resistant VA infrastructure. The stablecoin was issued across multiple public blockchains and a proprietary chain, complicating tracing and cross-chain monitoring. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "froze assets valued at over USD 29 million held in a wallet linked to the same conglomerate"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: The typology shows obligated entities, including VASPs, may be unable to rely on issuer-level freeze/burn mechanisms as a compliance safeguard, underscoring the need for jurisdictions to ensure stablecoin issuance arrangements are subject to robust AML/CFT requirements, including the technical capacity to comply with lawful blocking, freeze and seizure orders. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "including the technical capacity to comply with lawful blocking, freeze and seizure orders"</sub>

## 2.3. P2P Transactions via Unhosted Wallets

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: P2P transfers via unhosted wallets present elevated illicit finance risks as they occur directly between parties without a regulated intermediary, falling outside AML/CFT/CPF obligations. Although public blockchain transactions remain technically visible, pseudonymity lets threat actors obscure attribution through layered unhosted wallets transactionally distant from Travel Rule-covered wallets, compounded cross-border by near-instant stablecoin settlement. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "P2P transfers via unhosted wallets present elevated illicit finance risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Survey results: 88% of jurisdictions (58 of 66) rated P2P transactions as high risk, 8% (5 of 66) as medium and 5% (3 of 66) as low. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "88% of jurisdictions (58 of 66) rated P2P transactions as high risk"</sub>

## 2.1.2. Cross-Cutting Risks in VA-Enabled Illicit Finance

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Increasing AI abuse is described as a risk factor throughout the lifecycle of fraud, hacking and ML and may amplify convergence risks. In recent cases, attackers posed with fabricated profiles and deepfake video interviews in AI-enabled recruitment scams to win victims' trust, then got them to install malware or give wallet access; more than USD 1 million in virtual assets, mainly stablecoins, was stolen. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "synthetic profiles and deepfake video interviews in AI-enabled recruitment scams"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Authorities should treat AI-enabled virtual asset crime as more than a technical threat: it is a structural factor that can heighten ML/TF and sanctions-evasion risks. They should also reinforce risk-based supervision of the VASP, DeFi and stablecoin ecosystem, factoring in deepfake and synthetic-identity controls, AI-native investigative capabilities and public-private information-sharing mechanisms. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Authorities should see AI-enabled virtual asset crime not merely as a technical threat"</sub>

## 2.2. Stablecoins

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The FATF targeted report on stablecoins and unhosted wallets is cited as documenting a growing preference among terrorist organisations such as ISIL and Al-Qaeda for stablecoins over Bitcoin. Methods include rotating wallet addresses, splitting funds into micro-amounts, and multi-hop transfers via VASPs and OTC brokers that apply minimal CDD, together with DeFi tools and unhosted wallets used to sidestep compliance controls. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "terrorist organisations, including ISIL and Al-Qaeda, increasingly favour stablecoins over Bitcoin"</sub>

## 2.3. P2P Transactions via Unhosted Wallets

- **descriptive / limitation**: No obliged entity is responsible for filing STRs on peer-to-peer transactions via unhosted wallets, which the update calls a structural gap in the AML/CFT/CPF framework. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "represents a structural gap in the AML/CFT/CPF framework"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: Stablecoin issuers may play a complementary part by acting on law enforcement requests to freeze suspected wallets and, where appropriate, by submitting STRs to the FIU. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "stablecoin issuers may play a complementary role by responding to law enforcement requests to freeze suspected wallets"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: FATF previously conducted market metric analysis of P2P transactions but could not present definitive figures due to significant discrepancies in data across companies. Only 23% (31 of 133) of 2026 survey respondents collect and assess P2P market metrics to evaluate ML/TF/PF risks from unhosted wallets, so it is important to keep monitoring the availability and accuracy of data on the scale and proportion of P2P transactions. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "only 23% (31 of 133) of respondent jurisdictions indicated that they collect and assess P2P market metrics"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Offshore VASPs (oVASPs) are VASPs that provide services to clients in jurisdictions outside their jurisdiction of incorporation or physical location; they can be incorporated in any jurisdiction and may operate across multiple markets. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Offshore VASPs (oVASPs) are VASPs that provide services to clients in jurisdictions outside its jurisdiction of incorporation or physical location."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Where oVASPs are incorporated or located in jurisdictions with weak or underdeveloped AML/CFT/CPF frameworks, ML/TF/PF abuse risk increases in both home and host jurisdictions; this can be compounded where host jurisdictions do not require licensing or registration of oVASPs, which often lack a physical or legal presence where their customers reside. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it can increase the risk of ML/TF/PF abuse not only in the home jurisdiction but also the host jurisdictions"</sub>

## 1. Introduction

- **descriptive / context**: Express trusts and similar legal arrangements are generally formed for essential and legitimate purposes, but the ability to adequately and effectively identify their beneficial owners is key to preventing abuse by criminals circumventing AML/CFT measures. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Express1 trusts and similar legal arrangements are generally formed for essential and legitimate purposes"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Countries should assess ML/TF risks of misuse of legal arrangements and take preventive measures, ensuring adequate, accurate and up-to-date information on express trusts and similar arrangements is efficiently and timely obtainable or accessible by competent authorities. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries should ensure that there is adequate, accurate, and up-to-date information on express trusts"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: The required trust information covers the settlor(s), trustee(s), protector(s), beneficiary(ies) or class(es) of beneficiaries, and any other person exercising ultimate effective control. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "settlor(s), trustee(s), protector(s) and beneficiary(ies), or class(es) of beneficiaries"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Trusts are not a type of legal entity or corporate vehicle but a relationship between the principal parties to the arrangement. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "trusts are not a type of legal entity or corporate vehicle but a relationship"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The R.25 guidance targets all public and private stakeholders that regulate, supervise, enforce, form, manage or administer trusts, complements the R.24 guidance, and supports implementation of R.25 as adopted in February 2023; R.24 and R.25 guidance should be read in parallel. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Guidance on R.24 and R.25 should be read in parallel"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The R.25 guidance is non-binding and does not override national authorities' purview. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This Guidance is non-binding and does not override the purview of national authorities"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: References to 'trusts' in the guidance mean express trusts as distinct from implied trusts, per the FATF Glossary. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "referring to express trusts as distinct from implied trusts"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The FATF Glossary defines a legal arrangement as express trusts and other similar arrangements, examples including fiducie, certain types of Treuhand, fideicomiso and Waqf. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "fiducie, certain types of Treuhand, fideicomiso and Waqf"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FATF strengthened Recommendation 24 in March 2022 and Recommendation 25 in February 2023, and in March 2023 published updated guidance on beneficial ownership of legal persons covering risk assessment, accuracy mechanisms and sanctions. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF strengthened its Recommendation 24 and Recommendation 25 in March 2022 and February 2023 respectively"</sub>

## 2. Scope of R.25: Trusts and other similar legal arrangements

- **mandatory / requirement**: R.25 requires countries to assess ML/TF risks linked to legal arrangements and take mitigating measures. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "require countries to assess the ML/TF risks linked to legal arrangements and take mitigating measures"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: An express trust is a trust clearly created by the settlor, usually by a document such as a written trust instrument, as opposed to trusts arising through operation of law without the settlor's clear intent. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "means a trust clearly created by the settlor, usually in the form of a document"</sub>

## Express trusts

- **descriptive / definition**: Trusts govern the relationship between parties (settlor, trustee, beneficiaries) and assets and have no legal personality; the trustee is the legal owner of the assets and contracts on behalf of the trust. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The trustee is the legal owner of the assets and enters into agreements on behalf of the trust"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Trusts serve to manage and distribute assets or income per the trust instrument and the trustee's fiduciary duties, and are categorised as trusts for persons (beneficiaries) or purposes (charitable or non-charitable). (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "trusts are categorised as trusts for persons (beneficiaries), or purposes (charitable or non-charitable)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Typical reasons for trusts include asset protection from creditors or bankruptcy, asset management and business continuity, privacy (e.g., safety of high-profile individuals), overcoming legal obstacles such as residency requirements, tax planning, estate and succession planning, and holding investment vehicles such as pension funds. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "preserving assets from perceived future external risks, such as claims by creditors or risk of bankruptcy"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Because some trusts have other purposes, each arrangement's functions and characteristics need specific consideration and assessment against applicable requirements and ML/TF risks. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the need to specifically consider and assess each arrangement’s functions and characteristics"</sub>

## Box 2.1. Charitable Trusts

- **descriptive / definition**: Charitable trusts are set up for a charitable purpose rather than a group of people, so have no identifiable beneficiaries; purposes usually fall within the test from the English case of Pemsel (relief of poverty, education, religion, other community-beneficial purposes). (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "As such, there are no identifiable beneficiaries"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Charitable trusts benefit the public, are enforced by the Attorney General (or equivalent), a Court or statutory authority since no individual beneficiary can claim, and are not subject to the Rule Against Perpetuities. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Rule Against Perpetuities does not apply to charitable trusts"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Charitable trusts and similar arrangements such as Waqf cannot be concluded in absolute terms to be lower risk; some of their features may create enhanced ML/TF misuse risk. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is not possible to conclude in absolute terms that they present a lower risk"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: No a priori conclusions can be drawn on categories of legal arrangements; assessment must be anchored to the country's legal framework, risk, context and materiality. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a priori conclusions cannot be drawn on specific (categories) of legal arrangements"</sub>

## Identifying legal arrangements similar to express trusts

- **descriptive / context**: Similar legal arrangements may exist regardless of whether a country is a party to the Hague Convention or whether it has recognised or regulated express trusts. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "similar legal arrangements may exist regardless of whether a country is a party to the said Convention"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Civil law countries are not exempt from assessing under R.25 whether trusts or similar legal arrangements exist under their law, even though civil law arrangements need not replicate the trust concept identically. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "does not mean that civil law countries may be exempt from assessing their consideration under R.25"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Similar legal arrangements may functionally separate legal control of assets from benefit, or have a person hold title and manage entrusted assets for others or a purpose, creating a fiduciary obligation (duty of care, loyalty, good faith, prudence and disclosure). (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a duty of care, loyalty, good faith, prudence, and disclosure"</sub>
- **expected / method**: To identify similar legal arrangements, countries should assess whether legislation explicitly provides for trusts or similar arrangements (involving contract law experts and trust practitioners), consider the structure and purpose of all other arrangements including through case law, and may assess the legislator's intention. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries are encouraged to involve contract law experts and other trust practitioners"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Hague Convention Article 2: a trust is a legal relationship created inter-vivos or on death by a settlor placing assets under a trustee's control for a beneficiary or purpose, where assets form a separate fund outside the trustee's estate, title stands in the trustee's name (or another on its behalf), and the trustee has accountable powers and duties to manage them. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the assets constitute a separate fund and are not a part of the trustee's own estate"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Under the Hague Convention, the settlor reserving certain rights and powers, or the trustee also being a beneficiary, is not necessarily inconsistent with a trust existing. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "are not necessarily inconsistent with the existence of a trust"</sub>

## Scoping issues

- **expected / method**: Countries should assess both structure and function of all categories of legal instruments under their law to decide whether they qualify as legal arrangements; candidates include certain nominee arrangements, investment vehicles and foundations, with nominee arrangements also addressed in R.24 guidance. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "certain types of nominee arrangements, investment vehicles, and foundations"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Countries may usefully indicate when an assessment concludes that some legal instruments are not similar to an express trust and explain the rationale. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "some types of legal instruments are not similar to an express trust and the rationale"</sub>
- **permissive / requirement**: Foundations are legal persons under R.24 but may resemble trusts; FATF Standards let countries choose whether R.24 or R.25 rules determine the beneficial owner based on structure and function, provided the instrument is covered by one of them. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "provided that it is included under either R.24 or R.25"</sub>

## Parties to a trust

- **descriptive / definition**: Trust parties may include settlor(s), trustee(s), protector(s) if any, each beneficiary or class(es) of beneficiaries and objects of a power, and any other natural person exercising ultimate effective control, including through a chain of ownership/control. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the class(es) of beneficiaries and objects of a power"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Countries should require trustees to obtain and hold adequate, accurate and up-to-date beneficial ownership information on all trust parties. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Countries should require trustees to obtain and hold adequate, accurate, and up-to-date beneficial ownership information"</sub>
- **permissive / requirement**: For beneficiaries designated by characteristics or class, trustees need not obtain fully adequate and accurate information until the person becomes entitled at payout or intends to exercise vested rights (footnote 75 to INR.25). (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "until the person becomes entitled as beneficiary at the time of the payout"</sub>
- **permissive / requirement**: Following the RBA, countries may decide it is unnecessary to identify individual beneficiaries of certain charitable or statutory permitted non-charitable trusts. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "certain charitable or statutory permitted non-charitable trusts"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Where a trustee or other trust party is a legal person or arrangement, the trustee should obtain and hold its beneficial ownership information and take reasonable measures to verify identity, per R.24 guidance. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "take reasonable measures to verify its identity"</sub>

## Settlor

- **descriptive / definition**: Settlors are natural or legal persons transferring ownership of assets to trustees via a trust deed or similar, or providing or undertaking to provide property or funds; this requires an element of bounty, not a transfer for full consideration. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This requires an element of bounty"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The settlor named in a trust instrument may be only a nominee for the real 'economic settlor' who owns the contributed assets, requiring additional consideration to identify the economic settlor. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the named settlor is effectively only acting as a ‘nominee’ for the real economic settlor"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Any person contributing assets to a trust is identified as settlor; if Person A transfers assets to Person B intending B to endow a trust with them, Person A should be identified as settlor. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Person A should be identified as settlor"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Where the settlor or a party with a similar role is a legal person, its beneficial owner should be identified. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the beneficial owner of that legal person should be identified"</sub>

## Trustee

- **descriptive / definition**: Trustees are legal or natural persons able to act on trust assets, with powers subject to obligations derived from the trust instrument and, in some countries, case law and legislation; they are the central source of information on the trust and its parties. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they are the central source of information on the trust and parties to the trust"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Trustees may be professional (paid to act in the course of business, e.g., a lawyer or trust company) or non-professional (e.g., acting for a family). (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "if they are paid to act as a trustee in the course of their business"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: All trustees, professional and non-professional, must comply with R.25 requirements. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "all – professional and non-professional trustees – must comply with requirements under R.25"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Modern legislation widens trustees' scope to delegate to custodians, investment managers and other service providers, but the trustee generally remains responsible for monitoring and reviewing delegates' arrangements. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the trustee generally remains responsible for monitoring and reviewing the arrangements under which a delegate acts"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: A delegate is effectively administering a trust if it is proactively involved in trust transactions with ongoing oversight of others dealing with trust property and acts as a central source of information; administrators differ from trustees mainly in lacking discretion over disposition of trust property. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "save that the power to exercise discretion concerning the disposition of the trust property rests with the latter"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In modern international trust practice administrators commonly offer comprehensive services (liaising with beneficiaries, minutes, bank instructions, financial statements) and may appoint staff as professional trustees, possibly alongside the settlor's family or associates. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "liaising with beneficiaries, drafting minutes, instructing banks, and preparing financial statements"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Expert delegates performing in isolation investment advice or management, custody, legal or tax advice, real estate management, accounting or book-keeping are not administering a trust; nor are those serving underlying investments, e.g., directors of a trust-held trading company. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "acting as a director to a trading company held in a trust"</sub>

## Protector

- **descriptive / definition**: There is no universal definition of protector; a protector, appointor or guardian oversees trustees in beneficiaries' interest and may control a trust by approving or revoking trustee decisions, removing or appointing trustees, or changing the trust's jurisdiction. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they may change the jurisdiction of the trust"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: A trustee is expected to obtain information to identify and verify the protector's identity; a protector may be a legal person. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A trustee is expected to obtain information to enable it to identify and verify the identity of the protector"</sub>

## Natural person exercising effective control

- **descriptive / definition**: Ultimate effective control over trusts includes control exercised through a chain of ownership/control and any individual with power to decide who can exert control over the trust. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "any other individual with the power to decide who can exert control over the trust"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: 'Control' means a power, alone, jointly or with consent, under the trust instrument or by law, including to dispose of or invest trust property other than as an investment manager. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "dispose of or invest (other than as an investment manager) trust property"</sub>

## 8. International co-operation

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: FATF (para 175) notes express trusts and similar legal arrangements may be used in cross-jurisdictional ML/TF. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Express trusts and other similar legal arrangements may be used in crossjurisdictional ML/TF"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Effective international co-operation under FATF R.37 and R.40 requires access, through full co-operation of jurisdictional authorities, to accurate beneficial owner information in international ML/TF investigations. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Effective international co-operation, as set out in FATF R.37 and R.40, requires access"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Countries should be able to obtain trust information, including beneficial ownership, from other countries, and in turn must respond to foreign counterparts' requests for domestically available trust beneficial ownership information. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Countries should be able to obtain information, including beneficial ownership information, on express trusts"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: R.25 contains specific international co-operation requirements, including facilitating foreign competent authorities' access to information held by registries or other domestic authorities, e.g., via an efficient request mechanism. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "facilitating access by foreign competent authorities to any information held by registries or other domestic authorities"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: R.25 also requires exchanging available information on express trusts so foreign authorities can quickly move along a chain of legal ownership. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to enable foreign authorities to quickly move along a chain of legal ownership"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: R.25 also requires using competent authorities' powers, under domestic law, to obtain beneficial ownership information on behalf of foreign counterparts, not only in their own investigations. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to obtain beneficial ownership information on behalf of foreign counterparts"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: FATF (para 177) says information exchange with foreign counterparts should avoid unduly restrictive conditions, subject to internationally agreed standards; these may include refusing requests because they involve fiscal (including tax) matters or on bank secrecy grounds.   <sub>anchor: "the refusal of requests for assistance on the grounds that they involve fiscal, including tax,37 matters, or on the grounds of bank secrecy"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: FATF footnote 37: tax-related requests should follow international tax information agreements, and tax authorities that decline assistance where agreement conditions aligned with international standards are not met, or where declining is allowed by those standards, should not be judged as imposing unduly restrictive conditions.   <sub>anchor: "they should not be judged as imposing “unduly restrictive conditions.”"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: FATF (para 178) suggests countries may publish points of contact, agency or registry information and access/request procedures (e.g., online or via posted guidance) to help foreign counterparts request assistance. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "and the procedure for accessing or requesting this information, publicly available (e.g., online)"</sub>

## Asset protection

- **descriptive / context**: Trusts are regularly used for asset protection from perceived external risks such as divorce actions, creditor claims or bankruptcy risk, forced heirship provisions, and political uncertainty. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Trusts are regularly used to preserve assets from perceived external risk"</sub>

## Asset management

- **descriptive / context**: Holding assets managed by a trusted decision maker for beneficiaries is central to many trusts, e.g., where there is concern about a beneficiary's capacity or responsibility (such as a minor, or someone at risk of future incapacity). (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "have those assets managed by, a trusted decision maker for the trust beneficiaries is central to the use of many trusts"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Further asset management uses include beneficiaries with mental incapacity or severe disability, impressionable (e.g., young) beneficiaries, cases where regulation prevents the beneficiary owning the asset, and where independent oversight is required. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The beneficiary suffers from a mental incapacity or severe disability that prevents them from managing their affairs"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Trusts may also be used to set up spendthrift trusts limiting beneficiary access to assets, to stop a beneficiary pledging trust assets for debts, and to ensure business continuity. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "To set up 'spendthrift trusts' (where the beneficiary has limited access to assets)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Operationally, trusts may separate business management from enjoyment of proceeds and prevent fragmentation or dilution of ownership. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "prevent fragmentation or dilution of ownership"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Continuity of management under a trust can reduce short-termism and disruption to assets like commercial real estate or closely held businesses, and lets less-involved beneficiaries share in value and income without gaining control that outright ownership brings. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may benefit from an income stream, without acquiring the control that comes with outright ownership"</sub>

## Privacy

- **descriptive / context**: Trusts provide a layer of privacy, which may matter for the security of high-profile individuals or for managing beneficiaries' expectations (e.g., avoiding disincentives to self-sufficiency). (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Trusts provide a layer of privacy"</sub>

## Overcoming legal obstacles

- **descriptive / context**: Where jurisdictions limit asset ownership (e.g., only residents may own real property), appointing a resident trustee to own the asset on someone's behalf can overcome such rules. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Appointing a resident trustee to own the asset on your behalf"</sub>

## Tax optimisation

- **descriptive / context**: In some countries trusts may be exempt from income and other taxes or taxed at lower rates than companies or beneficiaries, though in some jurisdictions trusts are taxed at significantly higher effective rates than individual beneficiaries. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "trusts are taxed at a significantly higher effective rate than individual beneficiaries"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Placing trustees in a jurisdiction where they are tax-exempt avoids potential double taxation, and together with discretion over timing of appointments to beneficiaries can delay when tax arises on a beneficiary. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "can delay the time when taxation arises on a beneficiary"</sub>

## Estate planning and succession

- **descriptive / context**: Trusts aid estate planning by providing continuity of management and the ability to create and protect future interests for people not yet ascertainable, such as unborn descendants of the grantor. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "such as unborn descendants of the trust’s grantor"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Continuity during the grantor's life avoids difficulties of managing an incapacitated adult's property under a power of attorney, which is often not recognised by financial institutions. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "under a power of attorney that often is not recognised by financial institutions or others"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Because the trustee holds legal title, ownership continues unaffected by the death of settlor or beneficiary, avoiding succession delays, costs and formalities such as bank accounts frozen pending grant of succession or assets held in multiple countries. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is continuity of ownership which is unaffected by the death of the settlor"</sub>

## Investment or commercial holding vehicle

- **descriptive / context**: Trusts may be holding vehicles for joint investments such as unit trust arrangements and pension funds. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Trusts may be used as a holding vehicle for joint investments, such as unit trust type arrangements and pension funds"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Commercial uses of trusts include ring-fencing funds for consumer protection (e.g., tenants' deposits, holiday prepayments) and for employees (e.g., group life policies with lump sum death benefits). (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Ring-fence funds to ensure consumer protection"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Trusts may also fulfil future obligations (e.g., funeral or building maintenance services, oil field decommissioning) and provide security for contracts (e.g., contingent consideration on share or asset sales, bond issues). (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "in relation to the future decommissioning of oil fields"</sub>

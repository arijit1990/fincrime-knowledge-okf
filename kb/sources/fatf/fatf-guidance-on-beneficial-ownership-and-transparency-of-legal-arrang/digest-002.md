## Box 2.2. Natural person exercising effective control

- **descriptive / definition**: Powers constituting 'control' over a trust also include directing, making or approving distributions; varying or terminating the trust; adding or removing beneficiaries or class members; and appointing or removing trustees (source: FATF RBA Guidance for Legal Professionals, 2019). (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "add or remove a person as a beneficiary or to or from a class of beneficiaries"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: A trust beneficiary is any person who is or may become entitled, directly or indirectly, to the benefit of a trust arrangement, and may be a natural person or a legal person or arrangement. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is(are) or may become entitled to the benefit, directly or indirectly, of any trust arrangement"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: All trusts other than charitable or statutory permitted non-charitable trusts must have ascertainable beneficiaries, though at set-up they may have only a class of beneficiaries and objects of a power until entitlement arises, e.g., after an accumulation period or trustee discretion in a discretionary trust. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "All trusts (other than charitable or statutory permitted non-charitable trusts) are required to have ascertainable beneficiaries"</sub>

## Beneficiaries

- **descriptive / context**: Trust instruments may give trustees varying discretion over which persons benefit, how much each class member receives, and whether to distribute or accumulate income. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "whether to distribute income, or to accumulate it"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Beneficiaries may include those named in the trust deed, those clearly identifiable though unnamed (e.g., the settlor's children), and those becoming beneficiaries on an event such as trustee discretion (objects of a power) or end of an accumulation period. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a child of the settlor if the trust instrument names the beneficiaries as being the settlor’s children"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Under R.25 the obligation to obtain and hold adequate, accurate and up-to-date beneficial ownership information applies to all current beneficiaries, with exceptions for those identified by class per INR.25.1 and separate provisions for objects of a power. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "applies to all current beneficiaries, with certain exceptions in the case of those identified by class"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The class-beneficiary approach broadly aligns with international tax standards such as the Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework and the 2023 Common Reporting Standard update, which require enough information to identify class beneficiaries at payout or when exercising vested rights. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework, and the 2023 update to the Common Reporting Standard"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Where no beneficiaries are ascertainable at set-up, per INR.25.1 the trustee should obtain and hold some information on the class of beneficiaries, its characteristics and objects of a power. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the trustee should obtain and hold some information on the class of beneficiaries and its characteristics"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: A country's RBA decision not to identify individual beneficiaries of certain charitable or statutory permitted non-charitable trusts should be based on, and clearly align with, a risk assessment of that type of trust. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should clearly align with the findings of that risk assessment"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Trustees must know who the beneficiaries are to properly consider their interests; otherwise they cannot in practice perform the trust. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "trustees must be conscious about their identity, or else they are, in practice, unable to perform the trust"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Trustees with broad discretion need not identify all possible beneficiaries (which may include the unborn), but must survey the range of beneficiaries before distribution and determine with certainty whether an individual belongs to a class and can benefit. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The trustee has a duty to survey the range of beneficiaries before considering the appropriateness of distribution(s)"</sub>

## Object of a power

- **descriptive / definition**: An 'object of a power' requires two cumulative elements: the trustee has identified the person as a member of a class of possible beneficiaries without identifiable trust property or fixed interest, and the trustee has a clear and realistic belief the person may in fact benefit in future (e.g., named in a letter of wishes or the class has narrowed). (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the trustee must have a clear and realistic belief that, under the terms of the trust"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A possible beneficiary may not know they are an object of a power if the trust instrument defers informing them until discretion is exercised, an age is reached or another event; at times no beneficiaries may be ascertainable. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may not be aware that they are an “object of a power”"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Although letters of wishes are not binding on trustees, they are generally given significant weight, so persons specifically named in them should in principle be deemed objects of a power. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "any persons specifically named therein should, in principle, be deemed “objects of a power”"</sub>

## 3. Understanding and assessing the risks associated with trusts and similar legal arrangements

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: ML/TF risks of trusts mostly stem from obstacles to transparency, including private nature, choice of law, ease of formation, flexibility, overlap of parties, flee clauses and asset protection. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Potential overlap of several parties to the trust"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Trust ML/TF risks also arise from multiple layers and distance between beneficiary and other parties and from multi-jurisdictional elements (administration, assets and residences of parties in separate jurisdictions) complicating identification, verification and international co-operation. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "multiple layers and distance that can be created between the beneficiary and the other parties"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: A key vulnerability of trusts is greater privacy than alternatives: terms, or even existence, may be private from some parties, e.g., a trust for a child upon adulthood. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the terms or even the existence of the trust may be private from some of the parties"</sub>

## Private nature of the arrangement

- **descriptive / context**: Regulatory requirements for trusts are generally not comparable to those for legal persons (with exceptions like the fiducie), and registration is not always required except e.g. for tax, certain financial-centre trusts or Waqf. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "are generally not comparable to those in place for legal persons"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: 'Residence' means place of residence for natural persons and place of establishment for legal persons, determined by each country's legal framework. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "in the case of legal persons shall be taken to refer to “place of establishment”"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Trust party identities are usually disclosed only by the trustee to FIs, DNFBPs and authorities; restrictive conditions in some countries make identifying parties extremely difficult and hamper international co-operation. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "particularly restrictive conditions might render the identification of the parties to the trusts extremely difficult"</sub>

## Choice of law

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Under Article 6 of the Hague Convention a trust is governed by the law chosen by the settlor; this freedom can cause ML/TF risks such as regulatory arbitrage and adjudication complications when formed under one jurisdiction's law and administered in another. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it could lead to regulatory arbitrage or to complications adjudicating issues"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Simple trust formation, often a trust instrument drawn up by a notary or lawyer, combined with privacy and lack of registration, allows transfers of legal and equitable ownership without public knowledge; beneficiaries may not know their status. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "transfer the legal and equitable ownership of assets without making that transfer open to public knowledge"</sub>

## Flexibility

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Flexibility features relevant to AML/CFT: trust instruments may omit party names so parties change without disclosure, and settlors may retain control without express rights, e.g., by revoking the trust or appointing a protector with powers. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they can be easily changed without any disclosure or notification"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Further flexibility features: shielding assets from beneficiaries' creditors, powers to name future beneficiaries, purpose trusts without beneficiaries, settlor rights to revoke and reacquire assets, and easy change of legal owner via new trustee or distribution. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The possibility for the settlor to retain a right to revoke the trust and reacquire the trust assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Many flexibility elements appear in ML/TF cases involving trusts, and vulnerability may increase with the number of flexibility elements national law or case law permits. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Many of the above elements are found in ML/TF cases involving trusts"</sub>

## Potential overlap of several parties to the trust

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: A settlor may also act as trustee or protector, but where the settlor coincides with the beneficiary, especially as sole beneficiary, additional focus on the trust's purpose may be needed. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a situation where the settlor coincides with the beneficiary (particularly if it is the only beneficiary)"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: A flee clause triggers removal of the trustee and transfer of the trust to a trustee in another jurisdiction upon events such as regime change, breakdown of law and order, natural disaster or state of emergency; FATF considers such clauses outdated and largely unjustified. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The interconnectedness and transparency requirements of the global financial system make the resort to flee clauses outdated"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The ML/TF concern with flee clauses is that they can be triggered by service of process or an authority's information request, insulating the trust from law enforcement or slowing action. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the clause can be triggered upon a service of process or information request by an authority"</sub>

## Protection of assets

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Separating legal and beneficial ownership lets trusts shield assets from third parties; courts have held that effectively alienated trust property is no longer the settlor's and can be secured from creditors, and trust assets are generally not a beneficiary's assets. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "once there was an effective alienation of the trust property, they are no longer part of the settlor’s assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The Statute of Elizabeth (Fraudulent Conveyance Act) voided fraudulent transfers into trusts; in Webb v Webb [2020] UKPC 22 the settlor's claim failed because, as settlor, trustee and beneficiary, his retained rights were indistinguishable from ownership. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Webb v Webb [2020] UKPC 22"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Civil law courts also apply anti-fraud principles to transfers into trusts, which can extend to provisional measures such as freezing assets pending conviction (e.g., Cassazione penale sez. II, 25/03/2015, n.15804). (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "freezing of the assets pending conviction"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Asset protection trust features in some jurisdictions include a 1-2-year limitation period for fraudulent transfer actions, no recognition of foreign judgments including bankruptcy, a 'beyond a reasonable doubt' burden of proof for fraud, and requirements to sue domestically and/or pay deposits. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A 1-2-year statute of limitation within which a creditor must bring an action of fraudulent transfer"</sub>

## Risk assessment of legal arrangements governed under the law of a country

- **expected / requirement**: INR.25 requires countries to assess ML/TF risks of trusts and similar arrangements governed under their law, considering particular vulnerabilities and how far their governing law creates ML/TF opportunities, and to manage and mitigate identified risks. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the extent to which the country’s governing law presents opportunities for ML/TF"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A trust's only connection with a country may be use of its trust law and courts; assets, trustee residence and administration need not be in that country. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the only connection that a trust may have with a country is the use of its trust law"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF does not require countries to have trust law or recognise trusts, but residents may use foreign trust law; source-of-law countries may not know of an arrangement without a territorial connection, while administration or trustee-residence jurisdictions can assess risk and are also required to. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is no requirement for countries to do so"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Countries providing the source of law should assess vulnerabilities of their legal framework to mitigate ML/TF abuse, and may assess concentrated areas of use using data such as numbers of trusts registered, law enforcement data or supervision results of trust service providers. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may consider also assessing specific concentrated areas of use of the country’s governing law"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: A source-of-law risk assessment could include typologies on how far trusts governed under its law are used inside and outside the country. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "include the identification of typologies on the extent to which trusts"</sub>

## Risk assessment of legal arrangements administered in the country or for which the trustee or equivalent resides in the country

- **expected / requirement**: Countries should also assess and mitigate ML/TF risks of foreign-law legal arrangements administered in their territory, e.g., via TCSP trust administration, or whose trustee resides there. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "legal arrangements governed under the law of another country which are administered in their territory"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Countries should have processes to determine whether residents act as trustees of foreign legal arrangements, professionally or not, for example using tax authority information (accessible per international standards) and supervisors, supported by inter-agency co-operation. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries should have processes in place to determine if persons residing in their jurisdiction act as a trustee"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The FATF Glossary defines supervisors as designated competent authorities or non-public bodies ensuring FI and/or DNFBP AML/CFT compliance; non-public bodies must have supervisory and sanctioning powers, be legally empowered and be supervised by a competent authority. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the designated competent authorities or nonpublic bodies with responsibilities aimed at ensuring compliance"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: Countries could use publicly available information about types, forms and features of foreign-law trusts, how they are set up and how their information can be obtained, when assessing foreign arrangements administered locally. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "how their basic and beneficial ownership information can be obtained"</sub>

## Risk assessment of foreign legal arrangements having sufficient links with the country

- **expected / requirement**: Countries should identify, assess and mitigate ML/TF risks from foreign legal arrangements with sufficient links to the country; what is a sufficient link may be determined on the basis of risk (footnote 71 to INR.25). (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "What constitutes a sufficient link may be determined on the basis of risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: INR.25 sufficient link examples: the trust or trustee has significant and ongoing business relations with local FIs or DNFBPs (judged by market size or impact), significant real estate or other local investment such as securities, or is subject to local taxation (e.g., VAT, income, property or wealth tax). (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "has significant real estate or other local investment in the country"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Significant real estate investment could be judged by reference to average real estate prices and the local asset market, or quantity held. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "with reference to the average price of the real estate and the corresponding asset market"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Beyond INR.25 examples, sufficient links may include foreign trusts owning or controlling legal persons or arrangements established in the country. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "own, or control legal persons or arrangements established within the country"</sub>

## Mechanisms for preventing and mitigating risk

- **expected / requirement**: Countries should manage and mitigate identified risks based on R.25 and related Recommendations (R.10, R.24, R.22), with a comprehensive risk assessment analysing sources, nature and extent of risk, and clearly establishing the features and purposes of each type of trust. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the risk assessment should be comprehensive and involve sufficient analysis of the sources, nature, and the extent of risk"</sub>

## Mandatory elements

- **mandatory / requirement**: Mandatory risk mitigation elements include a strong understanding of ML/TF risks, sanctions for trusts operating locally but bypassing registration by registering elsewhere, and mechanisms to supervise or monitor non-TCSP trust administrators such as lawyers and accountants. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Sanctions for trusts that operate in their country but bypass registration requirements"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Mandatory elements also include risk-based enhanced due diligence by FIs/DNFBPs with trust relationships and mechanisms to investigate violations of registration and beneficial ownership reporting rules, with attention to higher risk arrangements. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Mechanisms for enhanced due diligence by FIs/DNFBPs that have business relationships with trusts"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Mandatory elements also include sufficient enforcement powers for competent authorities, verification and enforcement capacity for trust or BO registries and TCSP supervisors, and a regime for rapid, constructive and effective international co-operation on trust beneficial ownership information. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "rapid, constructive and effective international co-operation in relation to information"</sub>

## Additional mitigating measures

- **recommended / recommendation**: Additional mitigating measures include registers of trusts administered locally or with local trustees, registers of trusts governed by local law where registration confers legal capacity, and licensing or registration of professional trustees. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Establishing licensing or registration requirements for professional trustees"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Further optional measures: applying R.10 to R.12 to non-professional trustees and non-TCSP professional administrators, and disclosure requirements for arrangements operating, owning significant assets or registering locally, or receiving foreign or high-risk funding. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Applying R.10 to R.12 to: (i) non-professional trustees"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Further optional measures: enabling actors in higher risk sectors to detect and report activity of concern, and legislative measures such as anti-abusive provisions, limits on abuse-prone features and disclosure of other trust parties. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Introducing legislative measures, such as anti-abusive provisions"</sub>

## Mechanisms for preventing and mitigating risk

- **descriptive / requirement**: FATF standards impose no mandatory registration requirement for express trusts governed under a country's law, so a country may find it hard to gauge foreign use of its trust law; countries should still assess their legal framework's vulnerabilities. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is no mandatory requirement for registration for express trusts"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Countries should ensure adequate, accurate and up-to-date trust information on settlor, trustee and beneficiary is efficiently and timely accessible to authorities; INR.25 covers basic and beneficial ownership information and places the obligation to obtain and hold it on the trustee or equivalent. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the obligation to obtain and hold the information be imposed on the trustee or person holding an equivalent position"</sub>

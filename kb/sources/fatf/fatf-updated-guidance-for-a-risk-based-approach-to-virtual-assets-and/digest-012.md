## Internal controls and foreign branches and subsidiaries

- **descriptive / expectation**: Under Recommendation 18, successful implementation and effective operation of a RBA to AML/CFT depends on strong senior management leadership, including oversight of development and implementation of the RBA across the VASP sector. Recommendation 18 also requires information sharing within the group, where relevant, particularly regarding unusual transactions or activities. (p. 89)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "depends on strong senior management leadership"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: VASPs and other obliged entities should maintain AML/CFT programmes and systems adequate to manage and mitigate their risks. The nature and extent of controls depends on factors including the nature, scale and complexity of the VASP's business, diversity of operations (including geographical), customer base, product and activity profile, and degree of risk in each operational area. (p. 89)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should maintain AML/CFT programmes and systems that are adequate to manage and mitigate their risks"</sub>

## STR reporting and tipping-off

- **expected / expectation**: Under Recommendation 20, VASPs and other obliged entities engaged in VA activities, products and services should be able to flag for further analysis unusual or suspicious movements of funds or transactions, or activity otherwise indicative of potential illicit involvement, whether fiat-to-fiat, virtual-to-virtual, fiat-to-virtual or virtual-to-fiat. They should have appropriate systems so such funds or transactions are scrutinised in a timely manner and a determination made on whether they are suspicious. (p. 89)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "regardless of whether the transactions or activities are fiat-to-fiat, virtual-to-virtual, fiat-to-virtual, or virtual-to-fiat in nature"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: VASPs and other obliged entities should promptly report suspicious funds or transactions, including those involving VAs and/or providers, to the FIU in the manner specified by competent authorities, and their escalation processes should reflect this. (p. 89)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should promptly report funds or transactions, including those involving or relating to VAs and/or providers that are suspicious to the FIU"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: While VASPs and other obliged entities can apply the policies and processes that lead to forming a suspicion on a risk-sensitive basis, they should report ML/TF suspicions once formed, regardless of the transaction amount or whether the transaction has completed. The obligation to report suspicious transactions is not risk-based, and reporting does not discharge other AML/CFT obligations. (p. 89)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they should report their ML/TF suspicions once formed and regardless of the amount of the transaction or whether the transaction has completed"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: VASPs and other obliged entities should comply with applicable STR requirements even when operating across different jurisdictions. (p. 89)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should comply with applicable STR requirements even when operating across different jurisdictions"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Where a VASP (or other obliged entity) controls both the ordering and beneficiary side of a VA funds or wire transfer, it should take into account all information from both sides to determine whether it gives rise to suspicion and, where necessary, file an STR with the appropriate FIU and make relevant transaction information available to the FIU. (p. 90)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "controls both the ordering and the beneficiary side of"</sub>
- **expected / typology or red flag**: The lack of required originator or beneficiary information should be considered a factor in assessing whether a transfer involving VAs or VASPs is suspicious and whether it must be reported to the FIU. The same holds for other obliged entities such as traditional FIs involved in such a transfer. (p. 90)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The lack of required originator or beneficiary information should be considered as a factor in assessing whether a transfer"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: Where a VASP requests further information on a counterparty, or from its customer in the case of receiving a VA transfer from an entity that is not a VASP or other obliged entity, it expects the customer to respond in a timely fashion with documents/information at the level of detail requested. If the customer does not answer, this may trigger concern that the customer cannot reasonably explain the soundness of the transaction and lead the VASP to consider filing an STR on the customer. (p. 90)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Where their customer does not answer, it may trigger concerns for a VASP on their customer being unable to reasonably explain the soundness of its transaction"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: A request for information could be followed by a reassessment of the customer's attributes and risk profile when necessary. (p. 90)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A request for information could be followed by a reassessment of the customer’s attributes and risk profile when necessary."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Further red-flag indicators for VAs that could suggest criminal behaviour are in the FATF's Virtual Asset Red Flag Indicators of Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing, which help VASPs and other obliged entities detect and report suspicious VA transactions. (p. 90)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Virtual Asset Red Flag Indicators of Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Key red flag indicator categories listed: technological features that increase anonymity such as mixers, tumblers or AECs; geographical risks, since criminals can exploit countries with weak or absent national measures for VAs; and transaction patterns, including transactions structured to avoid reporting or that appear irregular, unusual or uncommon. (p. 90)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "criminals can exploit countries with weak, or absent, national measures for VAs"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Further key red flag indicator categories listed: transaction size, where the amount and frequency has no logical business explanation; sender or recipient profiles; and source of funds or wealth. (p. 90)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "if the amount and frequency has no logical business explanation"</sub>

## Summary of Jurisdictional Approaches to Regulating and Supervising VA Activities and VASPs

- **descriptive / context**: Section V gives an overview of jurisdictional approaches to regulating and supervising VA financial activities and providers, including tools for sanctioning or enforcement against persons failing AML/CFT obligations, which countries might consider in developing national frameworks. Some of these countries have not yet been assessed for compliance with INR. 15. (p. 91)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Some of these countries have not yet been assessed for their compliance with the requirements set forth in INR. 15."</sub>

## Italy

- **descriptive / case example**: Italian VA-related service providers are required to be listed in a special section of the register held by the Organismo degli Agenti e dei Mediatori (OAM); registration is a precondition to carry out activity in Italy, and work to implement the register was ongoing. VASPs are obliged entities subject to the full set of AML/CFT measures. (p. 91)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The registration is a precondition for service providers related to VAs in order to carry out their activity in Italy."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: On March 21, 2019, Italy adopted an update of its National Risk Assessment including the ML/TF risks from VAs. Results will be used to strengthen the national strategy, and obliged entities and subjects (financial and non-financial) are requested to take them into account in conducting or updating their risk assessments. (p. 91)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "On March 21, 2019, Italy adopted the update of the National Risk Assessment (NRA)."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: STRs and further analysis by the Italian FIU (UIF) allow it to collect information on VASPs operating in Italy (business data, location, beneficial owner, administrator and connected subjects); detailed single-transaction information (date, amount, executor, counterparts, wallet accounts); bank account data; customer or wallet-holder personal and economic profile and information to match VA addresses to owner identity; wallet or account information (overall VAs owned, main movements traced to the same or linked subjects in a timeframe, statements in editable format); and the type and main features of VAs. (p. 91)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "information useful to match VA addresses to the identity of the owner of the VAs"</sub>

## Summary of Jurisdictional Approaches to Regulating and Supervising VA Activities and VASPs

- **descriptive / case example**: In Italy, Legislative Decree No. 231 of 2007 (as amended by Legislative Decrees No. 90 of 2017 and No. 125/2019) brings providers carrying out the FATF's five functional activities within the group of recipients of AML/CFT obligations. (p. 91)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "includes providers engaged in the five functional activities as defined by the FATF"</sub>

## Italy

- **descriptive / case example**: Since 2015 the Bank of Italy has warned consumers of high risks of buying or holding VAs and warned supervised intermediaries (January 2015), with a new consumer warning recalling that of ESMA, EBA and EIOPA in March 2018. The UIF issued a Communication on January 30, 2015 on anomalous use of crypto-assets addressed to FIs (banks, payment institutions) and gambling operators, stressing attention to anomalous transactions such as wire transfers, cash deposits and withdrawals, and prepaid card use associated with crypto-asset purchases or investments. (p. 92)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "underlining the necessity for these obliged entities to focus their attention on possible anomalous transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The UIF issued an updated Communication in 2019 giving more detail on recurring elements, operational methods and behavioural risk profiles identified in VA-related STRs, with specific instructions for completing the pre-set STR format regarding VASPs, transactions, users/customers and wallets/accounts. In December 2016 and July 2018 it published collections of sanitized ML/TF cases, including typologies connected to anomalous use of VAs. (p. 92)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "more details on recurring elements, operational methods, and behavioural risk profiles identified in STRs related to VAs"</sub>

## Finland

- **descriptive / case example**: Finland's Act on Virtual Currency Providers (572/2019) came into force on May 1st 2019, requiring VASPs to register (authorization) with the FIN-FSA. Existing providers had to be registered by November 1st 2019 and new actors before starting operations. The VASP definition includes exchanges (fiat to VAs, between VAs, and VAs and other goods such as gold), custodian wallet providers and issuers of virtual currency. (p. 92)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Act on Virtual Currency Providers (572/2019) came into force on May 1st 2019."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Finnish registration requirements include basic fit and proper checks, requirements for handling customer funds and simple marketing rules (obligation to give all relevant, truthful information). VASPs are obliged entities under the AML Act (444/2017) and had to comply with AML/CFT obligations from December 1st 2019. The VASP's AML/CFT risk assessment and its AML/CFT procedures and guidelines are reviewed as part of registration. (p. 92)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The requirements for registration include basic fit and proper checks, requirements for handling customer funds"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The FIN-FSA was given powers to issue regulations and guidelines on certain VASP activity; its regulations and guidelines entered into force July 1st 2019, covering holding and protecting client money and segregation of client money from own funds, with guidelines on AML/CFT compliance. (p. 93)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIN-FSA was given powers to issue regulations and guidelines on certain parts of VASP activity"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Before the Act, FIN-FSA worked with ICO organizers from a securities markets perspective to identify when a VA was a financial instrument (transferable security); such assessments are still occasionally required. FIN-FSA drafted a checklist used in all ICO-related enquiries to assess the nature of the asset to be issued, available with FAQs on its website. (p. 93)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FIN-FSA has drafted a checklist that is used in all ICO-related enquiries"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: FIN-FSA supervisory experience shows VASPs are now willing and keen to be regulated and to seek supervisors' endorsement; the challenge is communicating to the public that authorization does not equal endorsement. VASPs have had challenges opening bank accounts, which could partly explain the change in attitude towards regulation. (p. 93)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The challenge is to communicate to the general public that authorization does not equal endorsement."</sub>

## Japan

- **descriptive / case example**: Japan amended the Payment Services Act and the Act on Prevention of Transfer of Criminal Proceeds (PTCP Act) in 2016 in response to the 2014 bankruptcy of a large VASP and the 2015 FATF VC Guidance. After the laws were enacted in April 2017, the JFSA established a VASP monitoring team in August 2017, composed of AML/CFT and technology specialists. (p. 93)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "in response to the bankruptcy of a large VASP in 2014 and the 2015 FATF VC Guidance"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In registration, the JFSA assesses applicants' AML/CFT programs, focusing on consistency between the applicant's risk assessment and business plan, through document-based assessment and off-site or on-site interviews (31 VASPs registered as of July 2021). The registration questionnaire was published in October 2018 for predictability and transparency, then revised by consolidating and eliminating some items and re-published in April 2020 to let JFSA examine the AML/CFT regime more efficiently. (p. 93)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "with a focus on consistency between the applicants’ risk assessment and their business plan"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: JFSA's registration process: it sends the published questionnaire; asks further questions and requests evidence based on answers; interviews board members on their mindset toward risk management and the business plan; and conducts a document-based examination focusing on risk management of the VA to be dealt in, segregation of customers' assets, IT system management, the AML/CFT regime, outsourcing management, and business management including internal audit. (p. 94)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "conduct interviews with the board members about their mindset toward risk management as well as the business plan."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: JFSA makes it a rule to visit the applicant's office to verify the effectiveness of planned operation of rules and policies, especially the risk management regime (on-site examination); once it confirms no concerns throughout the registration examination, it accepts the application and gives registration. (p. 94)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "JFSA makes it a rule to visit the applicant's office to verify the effectiveness of the planned operation"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: JFSA annually collects AML/CFT statistical and qualitative data from obliged entities to assess risk exposure and assigns risk ratings per entity using its own methodology, which feed the annual off-site monitoring plan. By March 2019 it had conducted on-site inspections of 22 VASPs (including 13 then-deemed VASPs operating on a tentative basis) and imposed administrative dispositions of 21 business improvement orders, six business termination orders and one refusal of registration. (p. 94)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "JFSA has conducted on-site inspections of 22 VASPs"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: The source data collected from obliged entities is approximately 60 KPI data tailored to each sector, non-exclusive and subject to annual revision. For VASPs, JFSA collects information including whether blockchain analysis tools are used for transaction monitoring and/or risk analysis, the types of VAs offered, and the number of customers identified for mixer and/or tumbler usage history. (p. 94)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "approximately 60 KPI data, which are tailored to each sector"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Further JFSA VASP data items: percentage allocation of hot wallet versus cold wallet usage; whether the VASP accepts corporate clients (number of accounts, transaction value); whether it offers business payment services; attributes of counterparty VASPs (geographical distribution and transaction volume); and the number and geographical location of VA ATMs it manages. (p. 94)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the percentage of hot wallet vs. cold wallet usage allocation"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: JFSA closely co-operates with the Japan Virtual Currency Exchange Association (JVCEA), a self-regulatory body certified in October 2018 that acts as an educational and monitoring body for member VASPs and prepares self-regulatory AML/CFT rules and guidelines. In consultation with the JVCEA, JFSA has conducted outreach, some with other authorities, to help VASPs improve AML/CFT compliance. (p. 95)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the self-regulatory body certified in October 2018"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: JFSA established the Study Group on the Virtual Currency Exchange Business in March 2018 and in March 2019 submitted to the Diet a bill to amend the acts, including applying the Payment Services Act and PTCP Act to VA custodian service providers and introducing an ex ante notification system for each change of type of VA dealt in by VASPs, taking into account VA anonymity. (p. 95)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the introduction of _ex ante_ notification system concerning each change of a type of VA dealt in by VASPs"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: In April 2019 JFSA prepared and publicized red flag indicators of suspicious transactions specific to VASPs, covering several transactions where anonymization technology was utilized. (p. 95)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "red flag indicators of suspicious transactions, which are specific to VASPs, in April 2019"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The amended Payment Services Act (enacted May 2019) came into effect in May 2020, bringing crypto-asset custodial services (managing crypto-assets on behalf of customers and transferring to designated addresses on customer instruction, without trading) under regulation. (p. 95)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "so-called crypto-asset custodial services, consisting of managing (safekeeping/administrating) crypto-assets on behalf of customers"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: On the travel rule, the JVCEA aims to introduce it by revising its self-regulatory rules and setting a target date, with JFSA strengthening co-operation. In March 2021 JFSA asked the JVCEA to consider appropriate implementation, resolve technical and operational issues and establish necessary systems; JFSA has held extensive dialogue with the VASP industry and will continue to monitor progress. (p. 95)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "JFSA issued a request to JVCEA to proceed with consideration for appropriate implementation of the travel rule"</sub>

## Mexico

- **descriptive / case example**: In March 2018 Mexico reformed its Federal Law for the Prevention and Identification of Operations with Resources of Illegal Proceeds to establish as a Vulnerable Activity the exchange of VAs by entities other than Financial Technology Institutions and Credit Institutions. Its Law to Regulate Financial Technology Institutions (March 2018) lets such institutions operate with VAs if authorised by the Bank of Mexico and using VAs it determines. (p. 95)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to establish as a _Vulnerable Activity_ the exchange of VAs made by entities other than Financial Technology Institutions and Credit Institutions"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In September 2018 Mexico published standards setting AML/CFT measures and procedures related to VAs; in March 2019 the Central Bank published standards defining internal operations for Credit Institutions and Financial Technology Institutions that directly or indirectly carry out operations with VAs; and later in March 2019 the general provisions under Article 115 of the Law of Credit Institutions were reformed to set measures credit institutions must follow on AML/CFT related to VAs. (p. 96)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the standards that establish the measures and procedures in terms of AML/CFT related to VAs were published"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Mexican Central Bank said VAs carry significant ML/TF risk due to the ease of transferring them to different countries and the absence of homogeneous global controls and prevention measures, but seeks to promote technologies that could be beneficial as long as used internally between Financial Technology Institutions and Credit Institutions. (p. 96)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "VAs carry a significant ML/TF risk, due to the ease of transferring VA to different countries"</sub>

## Norway

- **descriptive / case example**: VASPs have been subject to the Norwegian AML Act since October 15, 2018. Under Section 1-3 of the AML regulation (as updated May 2021), providers of exchange services between virtual and official currency are obliged entities if registered in the Norwegian business registry, operating from Norway, or directed towards the Norwegian market; this applies correspondingly to virtual currency custodianship services. (p. 96)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "VASPs have been subject to the Norwegian AML Act and its obligations since October 15, 2018."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The Norwegian regulation defines virtual currency as a digital expression of value not issued by a central bank or government authority, not necessarily attached to a legally established currency and without legal status of currency or money, but accepted as a means of exchange and able to be transferred, stored or traded electronically. Virtual currency custodianship services means custodianship of private cryptographic keys on behalf of customers for transferring, storing or trading in virtual currency. (p. 96)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "which is not issued by a central bank or a government authority"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Under the Norwegian provision, such providers can only be legal entities registered with the Financial Supervisory Authority. A registration application must contain the applicant's name, type of enterprise and organisation number, business address, the service offered, names and identity details of the general manager, board members and other contact persons, documentation under paragraph 4, and the enterprise's risk assessment and policies and procedures (AML Act sections 7 and 8). (p. 97)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the enterprise’s risk assessment and policies and procedures"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The Norwegian Financial Supervisory Authority may supervise compliance with the AML Act for such providers, can refuse applications that do not meet the requirements, and may recall a registration if requirements are no longer fulfilled. (p. 97)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "can refuse applications which do not fulfil the requirements"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: As of October 2021, nine VASPs were registered with the Norwegian FSA. Many more applied, with a slight drop in applications since 2020, and many are returning applicants previously rejected for shortcomings in AML policies and procedures. The FSA continues to issue numerous cease and desist orders to domestic and foreign-based VASPs operating without permission; identifying and closing unregistered VASPs resulted from closer co-operation with the FIU and a joint preventive project from 2019. (p. 97)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "As of October 2021, nine VASPs are registered with the FSA"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Norwegian FSA conducted its first VASP inspection in late May 2021 and gathered additional information from all registered VASPs to map sector risk and services. Experience, including from registration attempts, shows the sector includes actors differing in size, complexity, competence, AML and compliance experience, and professionalism. (p. 97)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FSA conducted its first inspection of a VASP in late May 2021"</sub>

## Sweden

- **descriptive / case example**: Since 2013 the Swedish Financial Supervisory Authority has considered bitcoin and ethereum as means of payment, so professional exchange services are subject to a licensing regime and, after a successful licence application, AML/CFT supervision. The regulation is not an explicit AML/CFT regulation of VA exchange services (they are not specifically mentioned in the law) but an implicit recognition they should be regulated; once licensed, all activities regardless of the VA are subject to AML/CFT regulation and supervision. (p. 97)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "has considered bitcoin and ethereum as means of payment since 2013"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In Sweden, thematic supervision has been carried out and as a result part of the sector ceased operations. VASPs have submitted STRs to the FIU, and feedback from operational authorities suggests criminals are choosing to take their business to unregulated exchanges elsewhere. (p. 97)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "criminals are choosing to take their business to unregulated exchanges elsewhere"</sub>

## Switzerland

- **descriptive / context**: Switzerland took a technology-neutral approach and applied its existing AML/CFT regulatory regime to virtual assets (VAs) and virtual asset service providers (VASPs) from an early stage of VA-related market development. (p. 98)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Switzerland adopted a technology-neutral approach and applied the existing AML/CFT regulatory regime to VAs and the respective VASPs"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Under Switzerland's principle-based regime, all financial intermediation activities related to virtual assets fall within the scope of the Anti-Money Laundering Act (AMLA). This covers exchange between VA and fiat currencies and/or between forms of VA, any transfer activities of VAs, safekeeping and/or administration of VAs or instruments enabling control over VAs, and the issuance of payment tokens. (p. 98)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "all activities involving financial intermediation and related to virtual assets fall within the scope of the Anti-Money Laundering Act"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Because transfer models for assets are becoming more decentralised, with several persons assisting in disposing over assets, Switzerland introduced in its anti-money laundering ordinance a new criterion for defining a service related to payment transactions in virtual currencies. (p. 98)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a new criteria for defining a service related to payment transactions in virtual currencies"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The text states that this modification is meant to ensure that Switzerland's AMLA is future proof with respect to VAs. (p. 98)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This modification should ensure that Switzerland’s AMLA is future proof with respect to VAs."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FINMA has repeatedly warned consumers of specific risks of crypto assets and related activities such as ICOs, and focused early in its supervision on VA-related topics. (p. 98)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FINMA has repeatedly warned consumers of specific risks of crypto assets and related activities such as ICOs."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: In Switzerland, because transfer models are increasingly decentralised, the anti-money laundering ordinance now treats a financial intermediary as providing a service related to payment transactions in virtual currencies not only when it has power of disposal over the virtual currencies for the customer, but also when it helps transfer virtual currencies to a third party, provided it has an ongoing business relationship with the customer. Examples are decentralised trading platforms that do not hold the client's private key but enable the transfer through a smart contract, and wallet providers that facilitate a transaction without directly holding a private key. (p. 98)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "assists the transfer of virtual currencies to a third party, insofar as it has an ongoing business relationship"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Before taking up VA business in Switzerland, any natural or legal person acting as a financial intermediary and subject to AMLA must either hold a prudential FINMA licence (for example a banking licence) or join a Supervisory Organisation (SO) or a self-regulatory organization (OAR), both of which FINMA in turn supervises. (p. 98)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "must either hold a prudential FINMA license (e.g. a banking license), become a member of a Supervisory Organisation"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Swiss anti-money laundering ordinance (AMLO) covers financial intermediaries that act in, or from within, Switzerland. (p. 98)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "covers financial intermediaries acting inor from within Switzerland"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: FINMA communicated its expectations regarding the travel rule in 2019. In Switzerland, financial intermediaries also have to fulfil the travel rule requirements when offering transfers to or from an unhosted wallet. (p. 99)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "financial intermediaries also have to fulfil the travel rule requirements when offering transfers to or from an unhosted wallet"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FINMA has assessed the implementation of virtual asset related requirements by supervised entities, namely banks. (p. 99)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the implementation of virtual asset related requirements by supervised entities"</sub>

## United States

- **descriptive / context**: The United States has a comprehensive and technology-neutral AML/CFT regulatory and supervisory framework for "digital assets", under which covered providers and activities are subject to substantially the same regulation as providers of non-digital assets within the existing framework for financial institutions. (p. 99)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "subjects covered providers and activities in this space to substantially the same regulation"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The U.S. approach draws on tools and authorities of several departments and agencies, including FinCEN (the U.S. FIU and administrator of the Bank Secrecy Act, BSA), OFAC, the IRS, the SEC, the CFTC and the DOJ. FinCEN, the IRS, the SEC, the CFTC and the DOJ in particular have regulatory, supervisory and enforcement authority over certain digital asset activities involving money transmission, securities, commodities or derivatives, or tax implications, and authority to mitigate misuse of digital assets for illicit financial transactions or tax avoidance. (p. 99)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The U.S. approach draws on the tools and authorities of various departments and agencies"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: If a person falls under the regulatory definition of a "bank", FinCEN and the U.S. federal banking agencies (the Federal Reserve Board, FDIC, OCC and NCUA) have authority, sometimes concurrent with state banking regulators, to regulate and supervise it when it engages in financial activity involving digital assets. (p. 99)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "If the person falls under the regulatory definition of a “bank,” FinCEN and the U.S. federal banking agencies"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Existing general tax principles apply to digital asset transactions in the United States because the IRS classifies digital assets as property. (p. 99)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "existing general tax principles apply to transactions involving digital assets in the United States because the IRS"</sub>

## Box 6. Case Study: U.S. Regulation and Supervision (Including Licensing and Registration) of Digital Asset-Related Providers

- **descriptive / requirement**: At federal level, FinCEN regulates as money transmitters any person engaged in the business of accepting and transmitting value, physical or digital, that substitutes for currency (including convertible virtual currency, whether virtual-to-virtual, virtual-to-fiat or virtual-to-other value) from one person or location to another by any means. (p. 99)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FinCEN regulates as moneytransmitters any person engaged in the business of accepting andtransmitting value"</sub>
- **descriptive / requirement**: Under the BSA, money transmitters must register with FinCEN as money services businesses and institute AML programs, recordkeeping and reporting measures, including filing suspicious activity reports. These AML requirements apply equally to domestic and foreign-located money transmitters, even without a physical presence in the United States and regardless of where incorporated or headquartered, as long as they do business in whole or substantial part in the United States. (p. 99)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "transmitters must register with FinCEN as money services businessesand institute AML programs, recordkeeping, and reporting measures"</sub>
- **descriptive / requirement**: Applicable state laws require relevant covered entities to obtain state money transmitter licenses in most states in which they operate, regardless of jurisdiction of incorporation or physical head office location. Money transmitters may also be subject to other requirements, including safety, soundness and capital reserve requirements, depending on the state and on whether their operations make them subject to rules of other U.S. regulatory bodies. (p. 99)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "laws also require relevant covered entities to obtain state money"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Since 2014 the IRS and FinCEN have examined a range of digital asset-related providers: administrators, some of the largest exchangers by volume, individuals who exchange as a business for others, foreign-located exchangers, digital asset/crypto-precious metal dealers, kiosk companies, numerous trading platforms, and registered and unregistered FIs. (p. 99)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Since 2014, the IRS and FinCEN have conducted examinations of various"</sub>
- **descriptive / requirement**: To the extent a digital asset is a security in the United States, the SEC has regulatory and enforcement authority extending to the offer, sale and trading of, and other financial services and conduct relating to, those digital assets. Secondary-market trading platforms for digital assets that are securities generally must register as national securities exchanges or operate under an exemption such as SEC Regulation ATS (alternative trading systems), and report information about their operations and trading to the SEC. (p. 100)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "generally must register as national securities exchanges or operate pursuant to an exemption from registration"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A foreign-located securities exchange, broker-dealer, investment adviser or other securities-related entity with no physical U.S. presence may still be subject to SEC regulations and jurisdiction when it offers, sells or conducts activities relating to securities (including digital assets that are securities) to U.S. persons or otherwise affects U.S. securities markets. Additional state licensing obligations may apply depending on the activity and the state. (p. 100)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may be subject to SEC regulations and jurisdiction when they offer, sell, or conduct activities relating to securities"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Certain trading in digital assets, including on platforms, may still qualify as money transmission under the BSA and state laws or regulations. If a digital asset is a security it is subject to SEC jurisdiction, as are any derivatives on that security. (p. 100)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Certain trading in digital assets, including trading on platforms, may still"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In the United States a digital asset may be a commodity as defined by the Commodity Exchange Act, in which case the CFTC would generally have regulatory jurisdiction over derivatives on it, including futures, options and swaps. The CFTC also maintains broad anti-fraud and anti-manipulation authority over the sale of such a digital asset that is not otherwise a security. (p. 101)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a digital asset may be a commodity, as defined by the Commodity Exchange Act."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The CFTC has broad authority to act against any person or entity, inside or outside the United States, associated with or engaged in fraud or manipulative activity in connection with a digital asset that is a non-security commodity, for example in U.S. CFTC v. Blue Bit Banc. Footnote 64 adds that the CFTC has determined that "virtual currency" is a commodity under CEA section 1a(9), citing consent orders in re Coinflip, Inc. (d/b/a Derivabit) and in re TeraExchange LLC in September 2015. (p. 101)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "broad authority to take action against any person or entity located inside or outside the United States"</sub>
- **descriptive / requirement**: A natural or legal person transacting in securities, commodities or derivatives is generally subject to additional oversight by a self-regulatory organization. Securities activities require registration with FINRA, and commodities and derivatives activities require registration with the NFA; depending on activities, dual registration with FINRA and the NFA may be required. As with money transmitter licenses, such a person must be licensed with each state regulator for states in which it does business. (p. 101)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Securities activities require registration with the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Certain SEC and CFTC registrants also have BSA obligations: establishing AML programs, reporting suspicious activity to FinCEN, identifying and verifying customer identity, and applying enhanced due diligence to certain accounts involving foreign persons. The relevant regulatory and supervisory bodies also monitor digital asset activities and examine registrants for compliance with their obligations, including (for certain registrants) AML/CFT obligations under the BSA. (p. 101)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Certain registrants of the SEC and CFTC also have BSA obligations"</sub>

## U.S. Law Enforcement, Sanctions, and Other Enforcement Capabilities

- **descriptive / context**: U.S. law enforcement uses financial intelligence from FinCEN and other information in digital asset investigations. FinCEN information, sourced from reporting it collects and disseminates to competent authorities, has been useful in developing evidence of criminal activity and identifying individuals who may be involved in ML or TF. (p. 102)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "U.S. law enforcement uses financial intelligence information from FinCEN and other information to conduct investigations"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Two key FinCEN data sources for detecting suspected ML or TF involving digital assets are (i) SARs filed by reporting FIs such as banks or securities broker-dealers that sent fiat currency for conversion into a digital asset at an exchanger or related business, or received fiat from one after conversion from a digital asset; and (ii) SARs filed by digital asset providers, often operating as money transmitters, that receive funds and convert them into digital assets or allow storage, trading and exchange of digital assets. (p. 102)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "suspicious activity reports (or SARs) filed by reporting FIs, such as banks or broker-dealers in securities"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: FinCEN also collects other information, namely foreign bank account, currency and monetary instrument, and currency transaction reports, which could contain investigative leads and evidence needed to deter and prosecute criminal activity associated with digital assets. (p. 102)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "foreign bank account, currency and monetary instrument, and currency transaction reports"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: U.S. departments and agencies have taken civil and criminal enforcement actions, administrative and in federal court, against illicit digital asset activity, seeking relief including cease and desist orders, injunctions, disgorgement with prejudgment interest, civil money penalties for wilful violations, and criminal sentences involving forfeiture and imprisonment. U.S. regulators and supervisors engage extensively with one another, state regulators, the DOJ and other law enforcement agencies. (p. 102)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "strong civil and criminal enforcement actions in both administrative proceedings and federal court"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A variety of criminal and civil authorities, policy tools and legal processes help U.S. agencies identify illicit digital asset activity, attribute transactions to a specific individual or organization, mitigate threats and perform analysis. The DOJ relies on statutory authorities covering money laundering, money services business registration, financial institution recordkeeping and reporting, fraud, tax evasion, sale of controlled substances and other illegal items and services, computer crimes, and terrorist financing. (p. 102)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "DOJ relies on a range of statutory criminal and civil authorities"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Footnote 65 gives select examples of U.S. enforcement, investigative and/or sanctions actions: a 2015 civil money penalty against Ripple Labs, Inc.; 2016 Operation Dark Gold; 2017 civil money penalties against BTC-e with a concurrent indictment of Alexander Vinnik; the 2017 terrorist financing case U.S. v. Zoobia Shahnaz; the 2018 sentencing of an unlicensed bitcoin trader; and the 2019 identification of digital currency addresses associated with the OFAC SamSam designation. (p. 102)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "2015 civil money penalty against Ripple Labs, Inc.; 2016 Operation Dark Gold"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The United States has charged and prosecuted individuals acting as individual peer-to-peer exchangers for violating the BSA or for money laundering, and foreign-located persons and organizations that violate U.S. law, among other digital asset prosecutions. (p. 102)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "has charged and prosecuted individuals operating as individual “peer-to-peer” exchangers"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The DOJ has broad authority to prosecute digital asset providers and individuals who violate U.S. law even if not physically in the United States. Where digital asset transactions touch financial, data storage or other computer systems within the United States, the DOJ has jurisdiction to prosecute those directing or conducting them. (p. 103)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "DOJ has broad authority to prosecute digital asset providers and individuals who violate U.S. law"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The United States has jurisdiction to prosecute foreign-located persons who use digital assets to import illegal products or contraband into the United States, or who use U.S.-located digital asset businesses, providers or FIs for money laundering. Foreign-located persons who provide illicit services to, defraud or steal from U.S. residents may also be prosecuted under U.S. law. (p. 103)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdiction to prosecute foreignlocated persons who use digital assets to import illegal products or contraband"</sub>
- **descriptive / requirement**: OFAC, typically in consultation with other agencies, administers U.S. financial sanctions and associated licensing, regulations and penalties, which relate to digital assets as well as most other asset types. OFAC has made clear that sanctions compliance obligations are the same whether a transaction is denominated in digital assets (national or non-national, such as bitcoin) or traditional fiat currency, and U.S. persons and others subject to OFAC jurisdiction are responsible for ensuring they do not engage in unauthorized transactions prohibited by OFAC sanctions. (p. 103)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "U.S. sanctions compliance obligations are the same, regardless of whether a transaction is denominated in digital assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: OFAC's December 2020 enforcement action and associated fine for failures related to VA services is cited as further confirmation that sanctions obligations apply equally to digital assets (footnote 66 gives a link to a 2020 BitGo document). (p. 103)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "OFAC’s December 2020 enforcement action and associated fine for failures related to VA services"</sub>

## International Co-operation is Key

- **descriptive / context**: The inherently global nature of the digital asset ecosystem makes digital asset activities particularly well suited to carrying out and facilitating transnational crimes. Customers and services can transact with little regard to national borders, creating jurisdictional hurdles, so effectively countering criminal activity involving digital assets requires close international partnerships. (p. 103)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "makes digital asset activities particularly well suited for carrying out and facilitating crimes that are transnational in nature"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Mutual legal assistance requests remain a key mechanism for strengthening international co-operation in digital asset cases. (p. 103)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Mutual legal assistance requests remain a key mechanism for enhancing co-operation."</sub>

## Initial Risk Assessment

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 31: the FATF Recommendations do not treat any sector as higher risk in advance. They identify sectors that may be vulnerable to ML/TF. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF Recommendations do not prejudge any sector as higher risk"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 31: individual jurisdictions should determine the overall national-level risk through an assessment of the sector, here the VASP sector. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the overall risk at a national level should be determined by individual jurisdictions through an assessment of the sector"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 31: within a sector, different entities can pose higher or lower risk depending on factors such as products, services, customers, geography, business models and the strength of the entity's compliance program. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Different entities within a sector may pose a higher or lower risk depending on a variety of factors"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Paragraph 34: VAs can enable non-face-to-face business relationships or transactions without the involvement of a VASP or FI, can quickly move funds globally, nearly instantaneously and largely irreversibly, and can facilitate a range of financial activities (money or value transfer to securities, commodities or derivatives activity). These factors may indicate higher ML/TF risks and may require risk mitigating measures such as strong digital identity solutions. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "quickly move funds globally, nearly instantaneously and largely irreversibly"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: If customer identification and verification measures do not adequately address the risks of non-face-to-face or opaque transactions, ML/TF risks increase, as does the difficulty in tracing the associated funds and identifying transaction counterparties. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the ML/TF risks increase, as does the difficulty in tracing the associated funds and identifying transaction counterparties"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 35: the extent to which users can use VAs or VASPs globally for payments or transfers is an important factor countries should take into account when determining risk level. Illicit users may take advantage of the global reach and speed of VAs and of inadequate or uneven regulation or supervision across jurisdictions, creating an inconsistent regulatory playing field. As with other mobile or Internet-based payment services usable globally, VAs can be more attractive to criminals than purely domestic business models. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is also an important factor that countries should take into account when determining the level of risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Paragraph 36: VASPs in one jurisdiction may offer services to customers in another jurisdiction with different AML/CFT obligations and oversight, which is of concern where the VASP is in a jurisdiction with weak or non-existent AML/CFT controls or where jurisdictions' ability to provide the widest range of international co-operation falls short. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "weak or even non-existent AML/CFT controls"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The range of providers in the VA space and their presence across several, if not nearly all, jurisdictions can increase ML/TF risks due to potential gaps in customer and transaction information, a particular concern for cross-border transactions and where it is unclear which entities are subject to AML/CFT measures and which countries are responsible for regulating (licensing/registering) and supervising them. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "potential gaps in customer and transaction information"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Paragraph 34: VA products or services that enable pseudonymous or anonymity-enhanced transactions carry higher ML/TF risk, especially where they limit a VASP's ability to identify the beneficiary. The guidance calls the absence of customer and counterparty identification especially worrying for VAs because they are cross-border in nature. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "facilitate pseudonymous or anonymityenhanced transactions also pose higher ML/TF risks"</sub>

## Box 1. Stablecoins and ML/TF risks

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Stablecoins purport to maintain a stable value relative to some reference asset or assets. As noted in the FATF's G20 report, they share many ML/TF risks with some VAs because of potential for anonymity, global reach and use to layer illicit funds, but certain stablecoin projects could have greater potential for mass-adoption, which could heighten ML/TF risks. Mass-adoption is particularly relevant when assessing stablecoin risks. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "certain stablecoin projects could have greater potential for mass-adoption, which could heighten ML/TF risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Mass-adoption is an important ML/TF risk factor because criminals' ability to use a VA as a means of exchange depends on it being freely exchangeable and liquid, which mass-adoption could facilitate. If a VA achieves global adoption as a medium of exchange and store of value without a VASP or other obliged entities, lack of or insufficient AML/CFT controls could heighten ML/TF risks. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "criminals’ ability to use a VA as a means of exchange depends to a great extent on it"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: By maintaining a stable value, stablecoins are designed to overcome the price volatility that has held back wide adoption of VAs as a means of payment. Reduced volatility could encourage widespread use for payments or transfers, particularly where sponsored by large technology, telecommunications or financial firms that could offer global payment arrangements. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "stablecoins are designed to overcome the price volatility issues often associated with many VAs"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A stablecoin's features affect the extent to which ML/TF risks materialise. Stablecoins can be more centralised or decentralised in governance and in who can access them and offer related services (for example whether unhosted wallets are allowed, or the system is permissioned or permissionless); these design choices have ML/TF risk implications and may change over time. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "whether it allows unhosted wallets or not, whether the system is permissioned or permissionless"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Box 1: the central governance bodies of stablecoins will generally fall under the FATF standards as either a VASP or a FI. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "central governance bodies of stablecoins will, in general, be covered by the FATF standards either as a VASP"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Box 1: where a similar function is performed with some decentralisation, countries are expected to apply a functional approach to identify the obliged entities, and to mitigate the relevant risks on a RBA whatever the institutional design and naming. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is expected that countries will take a functional approach to identify obliged entities"</sub>

## Peer-to-peer transactions

- **descriptive / definition**: Paragraph 37: the FATF defines peer-to-peer (P2P) transactions as VA transfers conducted without the use or involvement of a VASP or other obliged entity, for example VA transfers between two unhosted wallets whose users act on their own behalf. A footnote says that VASPs marketing themselves as P2P platforms are separate, and that where a natural person VASP is involved the transaction is not P2P. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "VA transfers conducted without the use or involvement of a VASP or other obliged entity"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: P2P transactions are not explicitly subject to AML/CFT controls under the FATF Standards, because the Standards generally place obligations on intermediaries rather than individuals themselves (with some exceptions, such as requirements related to implementing targeted financial sanctions). (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "P2P transactions are not explicitly subject to AML/CFT controls under the FATF Standards"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Paragraph 38: P2P transactions could pose specific ML/TF risks because they can potentially be used to avoid AML/CFT controls; no obliged entities are involved in preventing or mitigating risks. Illicit actors can exploit the lack of an obliged intermediary to obscure proceeds of crime, as no obliged entity performs core functions such as CDD and filing suspicious transaction reports (STR). P2P transactions are also used for licit activity. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "illicit actors can exploit the lack of obliged intermediary in P2P transactions to obscure the proceeds of crime"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Visibility of P2P transactions on public ledgers might support financial analysis and law enforcement investigations, especially combined with other information sources, unless anonymity-enhancing protocols and technologies are associated with the VA. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "visibility of P2P transactions on public ledgers might support financial analysis and law enforcement investigations"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 39: the Second 12-Month Review, using data from seven blockchain analytic companies, indicated that a potentially significant amount of certain VAs is transferred on a P2P basis and that the share of identified illicit transactions appears higher for P2P transactions than for direct transactions with VASPs. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "based on data provided by seven blockchain analytic companies"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Because of high variation in the data provided by the blockchain analytic companies, there is no consensus on the size of the P2P sector and its associated ML/TF risk. The review also reveals challenges and limitations of blockchain analytics research in coverage, timeliness, accuracy and reliability, even though P2P transactions are recorded on public ledgers. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is no consensus on the size of the P2P sector and its associated ML/TF risk"</sub>

## Box 1. Stablecoins and ML/TF risks

- **expected / expectation**: Box 1: consistent with general FATF obligations on new technologies, countries, VASPs and other obliged entities should assess the ML/TF risks of stablecoins before launch and keep doing so on a forward-looking basis, taking appropriate risk management and mitigation measures before launch. Mitigation should continue after launch and allow for evolving risk if the stablecoin(s) become mass-adopted. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should identify and assess ML/TF risks relating to stablecoins before launch and in an ongoing and forward-looking manner"</sub>

## Peer-to-peer transactions

- **descriptive / expectation**: Paragraph 40: results underscore the need for countries to understand ML/TF risks related to P2P transactions and how they are used in their jurisdiction on a dynamic basis, particularly when new types of VA enter the market or pre-existing VAs reach mass-adoption. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the need for countries to understand the ML/TF risks related to P2P transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The FATF has not observed a distinct trend towards increased P2P usage so far, but there remains potential risk that more VA transactions will move to P2P to avoid regulation/supervision as more jurisdictions implement the Standards. If illicit activity occurred to a significant degree on a P2P basis without interacting with VASPs or other on- and off-ramps to the fiat economy, this could potentially challenge the effectiveness of implementing the FATF Standards. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could potentially challenge the effectiveness of implementing the FATF Standards"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: ML/TF risks related to P2P transactions should be monitored in an ongoing and forward-looking manner. If countries determine P2P transactions present higher ML/TF risks needing additional mitigations, they can consider the non-exhaustive list of optional measures in paragraphs 105-106, on the basis of the assessed risk. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should be monitored in an ongoing and forward-looking manner"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 41: countries and VASPs should seek to understand what types of P2P transactions pose higher or lower risk and the drivers of P2P transactions and their risk profiles. Factors that could, depending on VA design, affect P2P engagement include the VA's accessibility and protocols controlling its privacy, transparency, security and transaction fees. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries and VASPs should seek to understand what types of P2P transactions pose higher or lower risk"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Jurisdictions need to weigh these factors and assess how they manifest in each country to assess risk and apply mitigating controls consistent with a RBA. The rapid evolution of the sector means changes in level and nature of risk are likely to come quickly and to merit concerted supervisory attention. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Jurisdictions need to weigh these various factors and assess how they manifest themselves in each country"</sub>

## Risk factors relating to VAs and VASPs

- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 42: ML/TF risks exist in relation to VAs, VA financial activities and VASPs. In addition to consulting previous FATF work and general risk assessment guidance, countries and VASPs should consider a non-exhaustive list of elements when identifying, assessing and mitigating risks of covered VA activities and VASP products or services. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries and VASPs should consider the following non-exhaustive list of elements"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: VA risk element (a): the number and value of VA transfers; value and price volatility of the VA issued; market capitalisation; value in circulation; the number of jurisdictions of users and number of users in each; market share in payments in each jurisdiction; and the extent of use for cross-border payments and remittances. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the market share in payments for a VA in each jurisdiction"</sub>

## Elements relating to VAs

- **descriptive / data or feature**: VA risk element (b): potential ML/TF risks of VAs exchanged with or for fiat currency or other VAs, and the extent to which VA-based transaction channels/platforms interact with or connect to fiat-based channels/platforms and digital services/platforms. Element (c): the nature and scope of the VA payment channel or system (for example open- versus closed-loop systems, or systems for micro-payments or government-to-person/person-to-government payments). (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "open- versus closed-loop systems or systems intended to facilitate micro-payments"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: VA risk element (d): the number and value of VA transfers, and those relating to illicit activities (for example darknet marketplaces, ransomware and hacking), across three categories: between VASPs/other obliged entities; between obliged and non-obliged entities; and between non-obliged entities (P2P transactions). (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "between nonobliged entities (i.e. P2P transactions)"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: VA risk element (e): use of anonymizing techniques for VA transfers (for example AECs, mixing and tumbling services, clustering of wallet addresses, privacy wallets) and of de-anonymizing techniques (for example risk assessment of wallet addresses using blockchain analytical tools). (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the clustering of wallet addresses, privacy wallets"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: VA risk element (f): exposure to Internet Protocol (IP) anonymizers such as Tor and the Invisible Internet Project (I2P) and other anonymizing software, which may further obfuscate transactions and inhibit a VASP's ability to know its users and implement effective AML/CFT measures. Element (g): the size of the business, existing customer base, stakeholders, and significance of cross-border activities of the issuer and/or central governing entity (where this exists). (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Exposure to Internet Protocol (IP) anonymizers such as The Onion Router (Tor)"</sub>

## Elements relating to VASPs

- **descriptive / data or feature**: VASP risk element (a): the number and types of VASPs based in a jurisdiction and/or offering services to persons in it, and the number and amount of transactions per service. Element (b): the sophistication of the VASP's AML/CFT program, including appropriate oversight tools to monitor VA/VASP activities and the knowledge and expertise of those responsible for compliance with the VA-related AML/CFT program. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The sophistication of the VASP’s AML/CFT program, including the existence or absence of appropriate oversight tools"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: VASP risk elements (c)-(f): the size and type of the VASP's user base, including its access to data on users and their activity and potential aggregation across platforms; the nature and scope of the VA account, product or service (for example small-value savings and storage accounts primarily enabling financially-excluded persons to store limited value); parameters lowering risk exposure (for example limits on transactions or account balance); and whether the VASP operates entirely online (platform-based exchanges) or in person (for example trading platforms facilitating transactions between individuals, or kiosk-based exchanges). (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "limitations on transactions or account balance"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: VASP risk elements (g)-(h): potential ML/TF and sanctions risks associated with the VASP's connections and links to jurisdictions; and whether the VASP implements the 'travel rule' and how effectively it has mitigated the 'sunrise issue' (see Recommendation 16). (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "how effectively it has mitigated the ‘sunrise issue’"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: VASP risk element (i): transactions from or to non-obliged entities (for example unhosted wallets with no obliged entity, or VASPs in jurisdictions where they are not regulated and supervised) and transactions where P2P transactions occurred at an earlier stage, provided such data collection is in line with national privacy legislation. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "provided that such data collection is in line with national privacy legislation"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: VASP risk elements (j)-(k): the specific types of VAs offered or planned and unique features of each, such as AECs, embedded mixers or tumblers, or other products that may obfuscate transactions or undermine the VASP's ability to know its customers and implement effective CDD; and the VASP's interaction with or management of smart contracts that may be used to conduct transactions. A footnote defines a smart contract in a VA context as a program or protocol designed to automatically execute specific actions, such as VA transfers between participants, without direct involvement of a third party when conditions are met. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "VASPs’ interaction with, or management of, any smart contracts"</sub>

## Prohibition or limitation of VAs/VASPs

- **permissive / context**: Paragraph 43: some countries may decide to prohibit or limit VA activities or VASPs, and VA activities carried out by non-obliged entities, based on their risk assessment and national regulatory context or to support other policy goals not addressed in the Guidance (for example consumer or investor protection, market protection, safety and soundness, or monetary policy). (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Some countries may decide to prohibit or limit VA activities or VASPs"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: In such cases some of the specific requirements of R. 15 would not apply, but jurisdictions would still need to assess risks associated with covered VA activities or providers and have tools and authorities to take action for non-compliance with the prohibition or limitation. Countries should understand the ML/TF risks of VAs in deciding whether to prohibit or limit, and should ensure they have the technical capacity and resources to enforce it. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "have tools and authorities in place to take action for non-compliance with the prohibition or limitation"</sub>

## FATF Definitions and Features of the VASP Sector Relevant for AML/CFT

- **mandatory / requirement**: Paragraph 44: the FATF Recommendations require all jurisdictions to impose specified AML/CFT requirements on FIs, DNFBPs and VASPs and ensure their compliance with those obligations. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF Recommendations require all jurisdictions to impose specified AML/CFT requirements on FIs, DNFBPs and VASPs"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: FATF Glossary: a 'virtual asset' is a digital representation of value that can be digitally traded or transferred and can be used for payment or investment purposes; it does not include digital representations of fiat currencies, securities, and other financial assets already covered elsewhere in the FATF Recommendations. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a digital representation of value that can be digitally traded or transferred and can be used"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The FATF Glossary defines a 'financial institution' as any natural or legal person who, as a business, carries out one or more of several specified activities or operations for or on behalf of a customer. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "any natural or legal person who conducts as a business one or more of several specified activities or operations"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: FATF Glossary: a 'virtual asset service provider' is any natural or legal person not covered elsewhere under the Recommendations who as a business conducts one or more of these activities for or on behalf of another person: (i) exchange between virtual assets and fiat currencies; (ii) exchange between one or more forms of virtual assets; (iii) transfer of virtual assets; (iv) safekeeping and/or administration of virtual assets or instruments enabling control over virtual assets; and (v) participation in and provision of financial services related to an issuer's offer and/or sale of a virtual asset. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Safekeeping and/or administration of virtual assets or instruments enabling control over virtual assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: A footnote says that in the VA context, 'transfer' means conducting a transaction on behalf of another natural or legal person that moves a virtual asset from one VA address or account to another. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "moves a virtual asset from one virtual asset address or account to another"</sub>

## Background and general considerations for the definition of VA and VASP

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 45: the new VA and VASP definitions were added to broaden applicability of the FATF Standards to new types of digital assets and providers of certain services in those assets. They were not intended to subtract from the existing definitions of 'funds' or 'funds or other assets' or from the scope of financial services in the definition of 'financial institution'. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to broaden the applicability of the FATF Standards to encompass new types of digital assets"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 46: a financial asset should not be treated as outside the FATF Recommendations merely because of the format in which it is offered, and no financial asset should be read as falling wholly outside the FATF Standards. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "no financial asset should be interpreted as falling entirely outside the FATF Standards"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 46: if a country decides a digital asset is outside the VA definition but is a financial asset, the FATF Recommendations still cover it as that financial asset, and the provider of related services may be treated as a FI. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "that asset is still covered by the FATF Recommendations as the relevant financial asset"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Paragraph 46: every country must decide whether given assets and their activity fall within the VA definition or are another kind of financial asset, and whether the providers are VASPs or FIs. In either case the FATF Recommendations apply in a similar way, with only minor accommodations, which a footnote says concern CDD (Recommendation 10) and wire transfer rules (Recommendation 16, the travel rule). (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Each country must determine whether such assets and their activity fall into the definition of VA"</sub>

## What is a virtual asset?

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 47: the VA definition is meant to be read broadly, with jurisdictions relying on its underlying concepts to take a functional approach that can handle technological advances and innovative business models. In keeping with the ethos of the Recommendations, the definitions aim for technology neutrality. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The definition of VA is meant to be interpreted broadly, with jurisdictions relying on the fundamental concepts"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 47: as part of that technology neutrality, the definitions should be applied according to the basic characteristics of the asset or service rather than the technology it uses. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they should be applied based on the basic characteristics of the asset or the service, not the technology it employs"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Paragraph 48: VAs must be digital and must themselves be digitally traded or transferred and be capable of being used for payment or investment purposes. The FATF intentionally chose the broad terms 'traded' and 'transferred', which could include issuing an asset to another person, exchanging it, transferring it to or on behalf of someone else, changing its ownership, or destroying it. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "VAs must be digital and must themselves be digitally traded or transferred"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Paragraph 50: a bank record in digital format representing a person's ownership of fiat currency is not a VA, nor is a mere declarative record of ownership or positions in a financial asset already covered by the Standards. A digital asset exchangeable for another asset, such as a stablecoin exchangeable for fiat or a VA at a stable rate, could still qualify as a VA. The key question is whether the VA has inherent value to be traded or transferred and used for payment or investment, or is simply a means of recording ownership of something else. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The key question in this context is whether the VA has inherent value to be traded or transferred"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 51: the FATF does not intend an asset to be both a VA and a financial asset at the same time, though the same asset may be classified differently under different national frameworks or regulated under multiple categorizations. In determining whether a new digital asset is a financial asset or a VA, authorities should consider whether their existing financial asset regime or VA regime can appropriately be applied; for example, if the asset is the functional digital equivalent of cash, a bearer negotiable instrument or bearer share, authorities should consider how the relevant regime's mitigation measures would apply. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF does not intend for an asset to be both a VA and a financial asset"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: If a jurisdiction defines an asset as a financial asset rather than a VA, existing AML/CFT standards and guidance for financial assets apply. Consistent with technology neutrality, a blockchain-based asset defined as a financial asset would likely not fall under this VA-focused Guidance, since technology is not the deciding factor, though elements of the Guidance may still help and should supplement other guidance in the RBA context. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a blockchain-based asset that is defined as a financial asset would likely not fall under this VA-focused Guidance"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Every asset for payment or investment should be subject to obligations applicable either as a VA or another type of financial asset. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "every asset for payment or investment should be subject to obligations applicable either as a VA"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Paragraph 49: a VA cannot be just a digital representation of fiat currency, securities or other financial assets already covered elsewhere in the FATF Recommendations; it must itself be inherently able to be traded or transferred digitally and possibly used for payment or investment. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "VAs cannot be merely digital representations of fiat currencies, securities and other financial assets"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 50: assets that fail to qualify as VAs should not be assumed to be outside the FATF Standards, because they may be another kind of financial asset such as a security, commodity, derivative or fiat currency. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "assets that do not qualify as VAs should not be presumed to fall outside the scope of the FATF Standards"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 52: where classifying an asset is hard, jurisdictions should review their regulatory systems and choose the designation that best mitigates and manages the product or service's risk. They should also consider how the asset is commonly used (for example for payment or investment) and which regulatory regime fits best. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions should assess their regulatory systems and decide which designation will best mitigate and manage the risk"</sub>

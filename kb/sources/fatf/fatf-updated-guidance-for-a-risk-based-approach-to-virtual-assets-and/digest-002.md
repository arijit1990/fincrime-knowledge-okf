## Background

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 1: new technologies, products and services can spur financial innovation and efficiency and improve financial inclusion, but also create new opportunities for criminals and terrorists to launder proceeds or finance illicit activities. The risk-based approach (RBA) is central to effective implementation of the revised FATF Standards adopted by FATF members in 2012, and the FATF actively monitors risks relating to new technologies. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they also create new opportunities for criminals and terrorists to launder their proceeds or finance their illicit activities"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 2: in June 2014 the FATF issued Virtual Currencies: Key Definitions and Potential AML/CFT Risks, and in June 2015 it issued the Guidance for a Risk-Based Approach to Virtual Currencies (the 2015 VC Guidance) as part of a staged approach to the ML/TF risks of virtual currency payment products and services. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "as part of a staged approach to addressing the money laundering and terrorist financing (ML/TF) risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 3: the 2015 VC Guidance focused on the points where virtual currency activities intersect with and provide gateways to and from (the on and off ramps to) the traditional regulated financial system, in particular convertible virtual currency exchangers. In recent years the virtual asset space has evolved to include new products, services, business models and activities, including virtual-to-virtual asset transactions. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the virtual asset space has evolved to include a range of new products and services"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Paragraph 4: the virtual asset ecosystem has seen the rise of anonymity-enhanced cryptocurrencies (AECs), mixers and tumblers, decentralized platforms and exchanges, privacy wallets and other products and services that reduce transparency and increase obfuscation of financial flows, plus business models such as initial coin offerings (ICOs) that present ML/TF, fraud and market manipulation risks. A footnote says privacy wallets, also called mixing-enabled wallets, allow transfers where multiple people's transactions are combined into a single transfer. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "enable or allow for reduced transparency and increased obfuscation of financial flows"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: New illicit financing typologies continue to emerge, including increasing use of virtual-to-virtual layering schemes that attempt to further obfuscate transactions in a comparatively easy, cheap and secure manner. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "increasing use of virtual-to-virtual layering schemes that attempt to further obfuscate transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 5: in October 2018 the FATF adopted two new Glossary definitions, 'virtual asset' (VA) and 'virtual asset service provider' (VASP), and updated Recommendation 15. The aims were to clarify application of the Standards to VA activities and VASPs, ensure a level regulatory playing field for VASPs globally, help jurisdictions mitigate ML/TF risks of VA activities and protect the integrity of the global financial system. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to ensure a level regulatory playing field for VASPs globally"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FATF also clarified that the Standards apply to both virtual-to-virtual and virtual-to-fiat transactions and interactions involving VAs. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Standards apply to both virtual-to-virtual and virtual-to-fiat transactions and interactions involving VAs"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 6: in June 2019 the FATF adopted an Interpretive Note to Recommendation 15 (INR. 15) clarifying how the requirements apply to VAs and VASPs, covering application of the RBA, supervision or monitoring for AML/CFT, licensing or registration, preventive measures (CDD, record-keeping, suspicious transaction reporting), sanctions and other enforcement measures, and international co-operation. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Interpretive Note to Recommendation 15 (INR. 15) to further clarify how the FATF requirements should apply"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 7: the FATF adopted this Guidance at its June 2019 Plenary and continued enhanced monitoring of the VA sector. In March 2020 it released its Guidance on Digital ID (useful for VASPs identifying customers in the digital context). In June 2020 it completed its 12-Month Review of the Revised FATF Standards on VAs and VASPs, which identified areas where greater guidance was needed, and released its Report to the G20 on So-called Stablecoins setting out how the revised Standards apply to so-called stablecoins. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In June 2020, the FATF completed its _12-Month Review of the Revised FATF Standards on VAs and VASPs,_"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 8: in September 2020 the FATF released a report on VA Red Flag Indicators of ML/TF for public and private sector use, and in March 2021 its Guidance on a Risk-Based Approach to AML/CFT Supervision, which includes a compendium of information on AML/CFT supervision of VASPs. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "released a report on _VA Red Flag Indicators of ML/TF_ for use by the public and private sectors"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The July 2021 Second 12-Month Review found jurisdictions had continued to make progress implementing the revised Standards, but gaps in implementation mean there is not yet a global regime to prevent misuse of VAs and VASPs for ML/TF. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "gaps in implementation mean that there is not yet a global regime to prevent the misuse of VAs"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Second 12-Month Review includes market metrics on peer-to-peer transactions (transactions that do not involve any obliged entity) and notes that jurisdictions' lack of implementation of the travel rule is acting as a disincentive to private sector investment in travel rule solutions. It concludes that updated Guidance will provide necessary clarity to aid implementation. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "lack of implementation of the travel rule by jurisdictions is acting as disincentive to the private sector"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 9: the 12-month review, G20 report and second 12-month review committed the FATF to release updated Guidance and set out six main areas where greater guidance was sought. The Guidance was updated in November 2021 to (1) clarify VA and VASP definitions as expansive so no relevant financial asset is left uncovered (as a VA or other financial asset), (2) address 'so-called' stablecoins and clarify a range of entities involved in stablecoin arrangements could qualify as VASPs, (3) add guidance on risks and tools for peer-to-peer transactions, (4) update licensing and registration guidance, (5) add travel rule implementation guidance, and (6) include Principles of Information-Sharing and Co-operation Amongst VASP Supervisors. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these reports set out six main areas where greater Guidance was sought"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Guidance was also updated to reflect the passage of time and publication of other FATF reports; the updates are summarised in Annex B. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Guidance was also updated to reflect the passage of time and the publication of the other FATF reports"</sub>

## Purpose of the Guidance

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 10: the updated Guidance expands on the 2015 VC Guidance, explains application of the RBA to AML/CFT measures for VAs, identifies the entities conducting VA activities or operations (VASPs) and clarifies application of the FATF Recommendations to VAs and VASPs. It is intended to help national authorities understand and develop regulatory responses to covered VA activities and VASPs, including by amending national laws where applicable. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "including by amending national laws, where applicable, in their respective jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 11: the Guidance is also intended to help private sector entities seeking to engage in VA activities as defined in the FATF Glossary understand their AML/CFT obligations and comply effectively. It provides guidelines to countries, competent authorities and industry for designing and implementing a risk-based AML/CFT regulatory and supervisory framework for VA activities and VASPs, including preventive measures such as CDD, record-keeping and suspicious transaction reporting. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "guidelines to countries, competent authorities, and industry for the design and implementation of a risk-based AML/CFT regulatory and supervisory framework"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 13: the Guidance seeks to explain how the FATF Recommendations should apply to VA activities and VASPs, provides examples where relevant, and identifies obstacles to applying mitigating measures alongside potential solutions. It is intended to complement R. 15 and INR. 15, which describe the full range of obligations applicable to VASPs and VAs, including Recommendations relating to 'property', 'proceeds', 'funds', 'funds or other assets' and other 'corresponding value', and supports development of a common understanding of what a RBA to AML/CFT entails. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identifies obstacles to applying mitigating measures alongside potential solutions"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A footnote on terminology says the FATF considers 'stablecoin' not a clear legal or technical category but primarily a marketing term used by promoters. The FATF used 'so-called stablecoins' in its G20 report; the Guidance uses 'stablecoins' to reflect common usage, which does not represent endorsement of their claims. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "primarily a marketing term used by promoters of such coins"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 12: the Guidance uses the terminology the FATF adopted in October 2018, and points readers to the FATF Glossary definitions of 'virtual asset' and 'virtual asset service provider' (Annex A). (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Guidance incorporates the terms adopted by the FATF in October 2018"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 14: some countries have implemented regulatory regimes for VAs and VASPs, but many jurisdictions have not yet put in place effective AML/CFT frameworks for mitigating VA ML/TF risks, even as VA activities develop globally and VASPs increasingly operate across jurisdictions. The FATF therefore makes urgent country action a key priority. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "many jurisdictions have not yet put in place effective AML/CFT frameworks for mitigating the ML/TF risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The use of virtual assets by ransomware networks is a critical concern, and growth of ransomware attacks has increased the importance of introducing effective AML/CFT frameworks globally. The FATF recognizes that other policy considerations separate from AML/CFT may shape the regulatory response to the VASP sector in individual jurisdictions. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The use of virtual assets by ransomware networks is also a critical concern"</sub>

## Scope of the Guidance

- **mandatory / requirement**: Paragraph 15: the FATF Recommendations require all jurisdictions to impose specified, activities-based AML/CFT requirements on financial institutions (FIs), DNFBPs and VASPs and ensure their compliance with those obligations. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "require all jurisdictions to impose specified, activitiesbased AML/CFT requirements on financial institutions"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 16: the Guidance focuses on VAs convertible to other funds or value, including VAs convertible to another VA and VAs convertible to fiat or that intersect with the fiat financial system. It does not address other regulatory matters such as consumer and investor protection, prudential safety and soundness, tax, anti-fraud or anti-market manipulation issues, network IT security standards, or financial stability. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It does not address other regulatory matters that are potentially relevant to VAs and VASPs"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 17: the Guidance does not address central bank-issued digital currencies (CBDCs), which are not VAs for FATF's purposes because they are digital representations of fiat currencies. The FATF Standards apply to CBDCs like any other form of fiat currency issued by a central bank. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "For FATF’s purposes, these are not VAs as they are digital representation of fiat currencies"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 15: the FATF has agreed that every funds- or value-based term in the Recommendations (for example property, proceeds, funds, funds or other assets, and other corresponding value) covers VAs. The Guidance mainly aims to describe how the Recommendations apply to VAs, VA activities and VASPs. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "all of the funds- or value-based terms in the FATF Recommendations"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 15: countries should apply all the relevant measures under the FATF Recommendations to VAs, VA activities and VASPs. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries should apply all of the relevant measures under the FATF Recommendations to VAs, VA activities, and VASPs"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: CBDCs may have unique ML/TF risks compared with physical fiat currency depending on their design, and such risks should be addressed in a forward-looking manner before the launch of any CBDCs. Their non-inclusion does not indicate the FATF considers them unimportant, but reflects that they are categorized as fiat currency rather than VAs. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Such ML/TF risks should be addressed in a forward-looking manner before the launch of any CBDCs"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 18: an effective RBA will reflect the nature, diversity and maturity of a country's VASP sector, the sector's risk profile, the risk profile of individual VASPs and the country's legal and regulatory approach, taking into account the cross-border, Internet-based nature and global reach of most VA activities. National authorities will have to take into consideration their national context, including supervisory approach, legal framework and risks present in their jurisdiction. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an effective RBA will reflect the nature, diversity, and maturity of a country’s VASP sector"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 19: illicit actors can abuse VASPs engaging in VA activities for ML, TF, sanctions evasion, fraud, ransomware payments and other nefarious purposes, just as they can abuse any institution engaged in financial activities. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "illicit actors can abuse VASPs engaging in VA activities, for ML, TF, sanctions evasion, fraud, ransomware payments"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Countries should not necessarily categorize VASPs or VA activities as inherently high ML/TF risk, although VAs may provide another form of value for ML and TF. The cross-border nature, potential enhanced anonymity, non-face-to-face business relationships and transactions, and opportunities for disintermediated activity with no obliged entity involved should nevertheless inform a country's assessment of risk. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries should not necessarily categorize VASPs or VA activities as inherently high ML/TF risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 20, citing the September 2020 Virtual Asset Red Flag Indicators report: the FATF has observed that VAs are becoming increasingly mainstream for criminal activity. Most VA-related offences highlighted focused on predicate or ML offences, but criminals also used VAs to evade financial sanctions and to raise funds to support terrorism. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "VAs are becoming increasingly mainstream for criminal activity more broadly"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 19: the overall risks and threats of covered VA activities are also shaped by how extensive and good a country's regulatory and supervisory framework is, and by how well VASPs put risk-based controls and mitigating measures into effect. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The extent and quality of a country’s regulatory and supervisory framework"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Paragraph 19: the Guidance recognises that some residual risk may remain despite these measures, and says competent authorities and VASPs should consider it when devising appropriate solutions. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there may still be some residual risk, which competent authorities and VASPs should consider"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 19: each jurisdiction should look at VAs and VASP activities separately, against the background of its own financial sector and regulatory and supervisory system, to reach its own risk assessment. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Jurisdictions should individually examine VAs and VASP activities in the context of their own financial sectors"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Offence types reported by jurisdictions include ML, sale of controlled substances and other illegal items (including firearms), fraud, tax evasion, computer crimes (for example cyberattacks resulting in thefts and ransomware), child exploitation, human trafficking, sanctions evasion and TF. Two types stood out as most common: illicit trafficking in controlled substances (sales transacted directly in VAs or VAs used as an ML layering technique), and frauds, scams, ransomware attacks and extortion. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "two types of misuse stood out as the most common"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: More recently the FATF has observed that professional ML networks are exploiting VAs as one of their means to transfer, collect or layer proceeds. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "professional ML networks are exploiting VAs as one of their means to transfer, collect, or layer proceeds"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The Second 12-Month Review also highlights challenges such as decentralization and the definitions of VA and VASP, peer-to-peer transactions, and implementation including of the travel rule. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "decentralization and the definitions of VA and VASP, peer-to-peer transactions and implementation"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 22: 'new' or innovative technologies or mechanisms for financial activity may not automatically constitute 'better' approaches, and jurisdictions should also assess and appropriately mitigate risks arising from new methods of performing a traditional or already-regulated financial activity, such as use of VAs in payment services or securities activities. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions should also assess the risks arising from and appropriately mitigate the risks of such new methods"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Paragraph 23: VASPs, FIs and other obliged entities providing banking or other financial services to VASPs or persons involved in VA activities should also consider the aforementioned factors. As with all customers, FIs should apply a RBA when considering establishing or continuing relationships with VASPs or VA-involved customers, evaluate the ML/TF risks of the relationship, and assess whether those risks can be appropriately mitigated and managed. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs should apply a RBA when considering establishing or continuing relationships with VASPs"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: It is important that FIs apply the RBA properly and do not resort to wholesale termination or exclusion of business relationships within the VASP sector without an appropriately-targeted risk assessment. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "do not resort to the wholesale termination or exclusion of business relationships within the VASP sector"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 24: in implementing AML/CFT regimes for VASPs, the FATF and jurisdictions should be aware of the intersection and potential impact of AML/CFT requirements on other regulatory requirements and policy areas, such as data protection and privacy, financial inclusion, de-risking, consumer and investor protection and financial innovation. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should be aware of the intersection and potential impact AML/CFT requirements have on other regulatory requirements"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 21: the Second 12-Month Review found a big rise in VA use for collecting ransomware payments and for committing and laundering the proceeds of fraud, and judged that the pace, sophistication and cost of ransomware attacks were likely to grow. It describes VAs as essential to ransomware actors, since their crime would otherwise be far harder to monetize. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "VAs are a vital tool for ransomware actors, without which their underlying crime would be much harder to monetize"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 21: the Second 12-Month Review names jurisdictional arbitrage (and the linked problem of non-compliant or weakly compliant VASPs) and anonymity-increasing tools and methods as the main trends in the VA ML/TF risk landscape. It reports that illicit actors exploit weak CDD and screening at such VASPs for ML/TF, and that anonymity tools continue to be used and developed. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Illicit actors are taking advantage of poor CDD and screening processes within these VASPs"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 25(a), functional equivalence and objectives-based approach: FATF requirements are compatible with varied legal and administrative systems and broadly explain what must be done without being overly specific about how. Clarifications should not require jurisdictions that have already adopted adequate measures to change the form or substance of their laws, and the Guidance supports objectives-based implementation rather than a rigid one-size-fits-all regime. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Any clarifications to the requirements should not require jurisdictions that have already adopted adequate measures"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 25(b), technology-neutrality and future-proofing: requirements applicable to VAs, covered VA activities and VASPs apply irrespective of the technological platform, do not give preference to specific commercial products, services or solutions (including technological compliance solutions), and are intended to be flexible enough to apply to existing and emerging technologies without further revision. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "apply irrespective of the technological platform involved"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 25(c), level-playing field (functional treatment): countries and competent authorities should treat all varieties of VASPs, regardless of business model, on an equal footing in regulation and supervision when they provide fundamentally similar services and pose similar risks; where risk profiles differ, treatment may differ in line with the RBA. Countries should aim to keep VASP regulation and supervision consistent with that for FIs providing the same services with similar ML/TF risks. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should treat all varieties of VASPs, regardless of business model, on an equal footing"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 26: this Guidance is non-binding, clarifies and interprets the existing Standards but does not change them, and does not overrule national authorities' purview, including their assessment and categorization of VASPs, VAs and VA activities according to prevailing ML/TF risks and other contextual factors. It draws on country and private sector experience to assist competent authorities, VASPs and relevant FIs (for example banks engaging in covered VA activities) in implementing the Recommendations using a RBA. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This Guidance is non-binding and clarifies and interprets the existing Standards, but does not change them"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Under the level-playing-field principle, it is a risk assessment based on the nature of the products and services offered, not terminology, technology or business model, that should guide countries in imposing regulation and supervision. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is an assessment of risks, based on the nature of the products and services offered, that should guide countries"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: All countries should strive to make their domestic regimes support even and efficient implementation worldwide, so as to avoid jurisdictional and supervisory arbitrage. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "all countries should strive to ensure their domestic regimes contribute to even and efficient implementation globally"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Guidance notes that nothing stops countries from adding requirements beyond the FATF Standards to respond to their own risks or policies. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is no impediment to countries imposing additional requirements that go beyond the FATF Standards"</sub>

## Structure

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 27: the Guidance is organised as Section II (scope of FATF Recommendations for VA activities and VASPs), Section III (application to countries and competent authorities), Section IV (application to VASPs and other obliged entities, including banks and securities broker-dealers), Section V (examples of jurisdictional approaches to regulating, supervising and enforcing) and Section VI (Principles for International Co-operation and Information-Sharing amongst VASP Supervisors). (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Section VI sets out Principles for International Co-operation and InformationSharing amongst VASP Supervisors"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 28: Annex A sets out the updated text of Recommendation 15 and its Interpretive Note and the Glossary definitions of 'virtual asset' and 'virtual asset service provider'; Annex B sets out the changes made in the November 2021 update. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Annex B sets out the changes made to this Guidance in the November 2021 update"</sub>

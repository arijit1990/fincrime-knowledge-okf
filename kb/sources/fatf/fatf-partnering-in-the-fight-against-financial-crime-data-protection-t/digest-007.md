## Box 6.1. What should a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) include?

- **permissive / recommendation**: A DPIA could describe the specific data points/elements to be shared, pooled, analysed or processed, including whether data is pseudonymised or anonymised at any stage and, if so, when/if re-identification is possible and by which entity, with particular attention to sensitive or special category data under the relevant DPP framework. It could also address common data standards and interoperability. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "including whether data is pseudonymised or anonymised at any stage of the project"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: A DPIA could address how to ensure data quality, accuracy and adequacy and data minimisation. In line with data minimisation, initiatives may wish to consider a graduated approach from the outset while ensuring data shared is sufficient and adequate for the purpose, for example using lower-risk models or options as pilot cases and expanding as necessary and permissible based on effectiveness. Adherence to the adequacy principle with appropriate data minimisation will help ensure effective data sharing. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "initiatives may wish to consider adopting a graduated approach from the outset"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: A DPIA could state if and how sharing, pooling, analysis or processing of the data elements will occur and, where data is collaboratively pooled or shared, how security will be guaranteed from initial transfer into the pool, through its use, to deletion/destruction, and in the event of loss or unauthorised disclosure, including risk mitigation measures and notification to relevant authorities and possibly affected data subjects. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "how security of the data will be guaranteed from the initial transfer into the pool"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: A DPIA could also include mapping of data flows for clear understanding, implications for cross-border or international transfers, privacy risks arising from the arrangement and mitigation measures, and any implications for vulnerable or marginalised groups. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Mapping of data flows to ensure clear understanding."</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: A DPIA could include technical, legal and organisational mitigation measures for risks from new technology on individuals, including measures to minimise false positives, measures to mitigate adverse impacts on individuals (for example ensuring individuals are not unfairly denied access to services and have a right of appeal), and other measures to mitigate risks to data subjects' rights and freedoms. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Measures that could or will be implemented to minimise risks of false"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: A DPIA could also cover questions of transparency and providing data to individuals, including how to prevent tipping off. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Questions of transparency and providing data to individuals, including how to prevent tipping off."</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: In addition to DPIAs, entities involved in data sharing agreements could consider undertaking or adopting data sharing agreements/contracts setting out each party's responsibilities, including a clear framework for dealing with customer complaints or the exercise of individuals' rights under relevant DPP laws. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In addition to DPIAs, entities involved in data sharing agreements could consider undertaking or adopting"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Entities could also consider Human Rights Impact Assessments (HRIAs), which provide a mechanism to mitigate risks to individuals and ensure all actors comply with human rights obligations such as the right to privacy, for example for sharing initiatives involving AI surveillance. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Human Rights Impact Assessments (HRIAs), which provide a useful mechanism to mitigate the risks to individuals"</sub>

## Private sector should establish early and ongoing engagement with DPAs

- **expected / expectation**: The report's heading states that the private sector should establish early and ongoing engagement with DPAs (data protection authorities). (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Private sector should establish early and ongoing engagement with DPAs"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report says that bringing in DPP authorities from the start of an information-sharing project is often critical and beneficial to the success of a private-sector AML/CFT/CPF initiative. Regular, transparent communication with them can help work through unexpected challenges and assess new risks as they appear. Ideally engagement starts while the financial institutions are designing their approach to collaboration and improved ML/TF/PF detection, and continues through DPIA preparation and on an ongoing basis as data collection and analytics begin. Involvement of AML/CFT/CPF authorities can also be critical to success. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Involvement of DPP authorities from the beginning of any information-sharing project is often critical and beneficial"</sub>

## Private sector should adopt measures to prevent de-risking related to information sharing

- **descriptive / limitation**: Data from sharing initiatives may eventually play a part in an institution's decision to exit a relationship or not provide services. If such decisions are not taken case by case or based on additional reliable sources of information, unintended de-risking could result. ## Introduction to FATF Standards Applicable to Private Sector (Preventive Measures)

- **mandatory / requirement**: To comply with the FATF Standards, countries must impose specific obligations on the private sector (financial institutions, DNFBPs and VASPs) to mitigate ML/TF risks, collectively known as preventive measures and encompassing FATF Recommendations 9 through 23. They focus on prevention, detection and reporting of customers and transactions suspected of money laundering, associated predicate offences and terrorist financing. (p. 62)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries must impose specific obligations on the private sector"</sub>
- **descriptive / requirement**: Under R.10, preventive measures require the private sector to know their customers and monitor accounts and activities as appropriate for AML/CFT. This involves customer due diligence (CDD) to identify and verify customer identity (KYC) when establishing the relationship, understanding the purpose and intended nature of the business relationship, and ongoing due diligence and scrutiny of transactions throughout the relationship to ensure they are not misused for ML/TF. (p. 62)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "know who their customers are and monitor their accounts and activities as appropriate for AML/CFT purposes (R.10)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Annex A says that, in general, preventive measures call on the private sector to understand the nature and level of its ML/TF risks and to apply AML/CFT policies, internal controls and programmes sufficient to mitigate them (R.1). (p. 62)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "understand the nature and level of ML/TF risks and apply AML/CFT policies, internal controls, and programmes"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Under R.20, the private sector must be able to detect and report suspicious transactions. Financial institutions hold transaction data and typically have transaction monitoring systems with automatic risk indicators within their institution, but as ML/TF becomes more complex the ability of these systems to identify large-scale or sophisticated schemes is limited without digitalisation, machine learning and greater information sharing. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "As ML/TF activity becomes more complex the ability of these systems to effectively identify large-scale or sophisticated schemes is limited"</sub>
- **descriptive / requirement**: Under R.11, preventive measures require keeping records on CDD and other transaction information for at least 5 years to enable law enforcement investigations, as financial crime is often difficult to detect and investigations can take considerable time to reveal complex networks. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "keep records on CDD and other transaction information for at least 5 years (R.11)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Under R.21, customers must not be informed that an STR or related information is filed with authorities. The annex states these provisions are not intended to inhibit private sector information sharing efforts; STR confidentiality ensures potential criminals are not alerted. R.21 also provides safe harbour to financial institutions and their representatives for good faith reporting of suspicious transactions. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These provisions are not intended to inhibit private sector information sharing efforts"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: To meet AML/CFT obligations the private sector must collect, store and share relevant data to identify and report suspected ML/TF to competent authorities, noting AML/CFT and DPP authorities have duties to ensure this data satisfies both sets of requirements. Data must be stored properly and shared securely with public sectors (supervisors and law enforcement, domestically and occasionally internationally) and private sectors (within group/foreign branches, and with other FIs or DNFBPs in the country) to allow timely and effective disruption. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the private sector must collect, store and share relevant data and information to identify and report suspected ML/TF activities"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Annex A says the FATF has recently clarified that information sharing is especially needed within group-wide ML/TF programmes, so that sophisticated professional money laundering networks that spread their activity across entities and jurisdictions, facilitating corruption, drug trafficking and tax evasion, can be detected and reported. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "information sharing is particularly necessary in context of group-wide programmes against ML/TF"</sub>

## Table A1. Types of information shared within financial groups for AML/CFT/CPF purposes

- **descriptive / data or feature**: Table A1 lists customer information shared within groups to manage customer and geographical risks, identify global risk exposure from on-boarding of the same customer by multiple group entities, and make record-keeping more efficient. Examples include identification and contact information; for legal persons and arrangements, nature of business, ownership and control structure, legal form and proof of existence, registered office and principal place of business addresses, and LEI information; financial assets, tax and real estate records; source of funds and wealth; economic/professional activity; account files; PEP status (including close associates or family); targeted financial sanction information; adverse information from public sources or internal ML/TF investigation; and customer risk categorisation. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Customer identification and contact information (name and identifier), in case of legal persons and arrangements"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Table A1 lists beneficial owner information shared within groups to manage beneficial owner and geographical risks, identify the same beneficial owner across multiple group entities, and make record-keeping more efficient. Examples include beneficial owner identification and contact information, real estate holdings, source of funds and wealth, economic/professional activity, account files, PEP status and other relevant elements from documents collected at on-boarding or record updates. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Beneficial owner identification and contact information, real estate holdings, information on source of funds and wealth"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Table A1 lists account information (bank/other account details, including the intended purpose of the account, expected location of transactions/activity as expressed by the customer, and business correspondence) shared within groups for effective due diligence and transaction monitoring at group level, justification of transaction patterns against the financial profile, and follow-up on alerts or abnormal trading patterns across the group. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Bank/other account details, including the intended purpose of the account, expected location of transactions/activity"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Table A1 lists transaction information shared within groups for global transaction monitoring, alert processing and identifying suspicious transactions, and flagging and checking for similar behaviour across business lines. Examples include transaction records, credit and debit card records and usage, past credit history, digital footprints (IP address, ATM usage information), attempted/failed transaction information, currency transaction reports, information on account closure or termination of relationship due to suspicion, and analysis made to detect unusual or suspicious transactions. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Transaction records, credit and debit card records and usage, past credit history, digital footprints"</sub>

## Private to Private Sector AML/CFT/CPF Data Sharing

- **expected / requirement**: The FATF requires (Interpretive Note to Recommendation 18) that adequate safeguards on the confidentiality and use of information exchanged should be in place, including to prevent tipping-off, and that countries may determine the scope and extent of this sharing based on the sensitivity of the information and its relevance to AML/CFT risk management. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Adequate safeguards on the confidentiality and use of information exchanged should be in place, including to prevent tipping-off."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The annex states that the types of data shared within financial groups can also be used for sharing between financial institutions and financial groups, known as Private-to-Private Sector AML/CFT/CPF Data Sharing, and that the transnational nature of ML/TF/PF is typically more effectively identified and mitigated through information sharing and co-ordinated reviews by multiple FIs and groups. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "typically can be more effectively identified and mitigated through information sharing and co-ordinated reviews by multiple FIs"</sub>

## Public-Private Partnerships

- **descriptive / context**: Information sharing between public and private stakeholders through Public-Private Partnerships (PPPs) increases effectiveness by facilitating a more comprehensive view of transactions and customer behaviour, often in a secured environment permitting further data-mining, operational analysis and scanning by the private sector to fill intelligence gaps. PPPs enable sharing across supervisors, FIUs, law enforcement, vetted private sector participants and in some cases international partners. In July 2021 the FATF reviewed PPP information sharing mechanisms in its report on Opportunities and Challenges of New Technologies for AML/CFT. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These PPPs enable information sharing across supervisors, FIU, law enforcement, vetted participants from the private sector"</sub>

## Operational issues - Data and system readiness

- **descriptive / limitation**: Absence of structured data and differences in data format and classification, leading to a lack of interoperability, have slowed the launch of some sharing initiatives. A number of projects took three to five years to clean and match data before it was ready for sharing or analysis, and inadequate IT capacity prolonged implementation where institutions had to upgrade IT tools and staff IT skills. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A number of projects have taken three to five years to clean and match data"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: The FATF states that interested private sector entities should consider and plan ahead data interoperability projects to facilitate a successful launch of sharing initiatives. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "interested private sector entities should consider and plan ahead data interoperability projects"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Section 6 recommendations addressing these operational issues include data protection by design (paras 58-59), applying PETs (para 56), and taking steps towards data preparation (para 57). (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "applying PETs (para.56), and taking steps towards data preparation (para.57)"</sub>

## Other issues - Provision of financial services to affected customers/De-risking

- **descriptive / context**: Private sector sharing initiatives may help participating institutions identify customers or transactions for further monitoring under their AML/CFT/CPF requirements and internal policies, and could reduce the number of false positive STRs. As with any findings of problematic activity, this may eventually lead to exiting customer relationships or excluding certain individuals from certain financial services to manage risk. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These initiatives could reduce the number of false positive STRs"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Quoting the Phase 1 Stocktake Report, the FATF notes that sharing has the possibility of exacerbating defensive STR filing behaviour. Overreliance on a sharing system could lead an FI to regard a customer as suspicious solely on third party information that may be inaccurate or whose grounds for suspicion were ultimately rejected by the FIU, with the unintended and unethical impact of denying a legitimate customer access to the financial system or subjecting customers to further clarifications that delay services. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this has the possibility of exacerbating defensive STR filing behaviour"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: De-risking can occur even without private sector information sharing; the additional concern is the potential for multiple effects or over-reliance on sharing initiatives. Conversely, information sharing may increase the accuracy and reliability of information, thereby improving risk understanding and decision-making and reducing de-risking. A Financial Stability Institute paper (September 2020) noted improved information sharing could help reduce unwarranted de-risking, supporting financial inclusion. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "information sharing may increase the accuracy and reliability of information"</sub>

## Other issues - Competition

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Quoting the Phase 1 Stocktake Report, the FATF notes that processing large sets of customer information between FIs could raise competition concerns, and could result in selective sharing with only a small group of trusted participants and an uneven sharing framework. ML/TF risks might then transfer from FIs with sharing mechanisms to those lacking them, as bad actors thwarted by the former gravitate to the latter, so FIs or sectors without sharing mechanisms may face additional ML/TF risks and may need additional risk mitigation. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there might be a transfer of ML/TF risks from FIs that have information sharing mechanisms to those lacking such arrangements"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The FATF states that sharing initiatives should be limited to the sharing or accessing of data that is required for AML/CFT/CPF purposes. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The initiatives should be limited to the sharing or accessing of data that is required for AML/CFT/CPF purposes."</sub>

## Other issues - Cross-border sharing

- **descriptive / limitation**: DPP rules applicable in a jurisdiction or region shape the form and scope of a sharing initiative there, including the type of data, the purpose, and the specific legal grounds for sharing. As a result, initiatives implemented to date are seldom replicable or scalable in other jurisdictions, which limits their value, restricts projects to a single jurisdiction and makes cross-border expansion difficult, for example hindering identification of more complex ML via correspondent banking networks or trade finance. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the sharing initiatives implemented to-date, despite extensive resources allocated, are seldom replicable or able to be scaled-up in other jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Institutions involved in these initiatives are generally large international or regional banks that obtain data from several jurisdictions within their group. Involvement in a sharing platform in one jurisdiction may raise compliance concerns for another part of the institution operating under different DPP standards or interpretations of standards. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the institutions that are involved in these initiatives are large international or regional banks"</sub>

## Other issues - Competition

- **expected / expectation**: Quoting the Phase 1 Stocktake Report, the report says that access to and exchange of data among a limited number of FIs should not give those FIs an unfair advantage. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Access and exchange of data amongst a limited number of FIs should not provide them with an unfair advantage"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: Quoting the Phase 1 Stocktake Report, the report says competition law concerns may also be relevant when assessing an AML/CFT data sharing arrangement, to keep a level playing field and avoid exclusionary conduct by potential competitors. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "competition law concerns may also have a place in the assessment of an AML/CFT data sharing arrangement"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Quoting the Phase 1 Stocktake Report, the report states that where data access is warranted, it must be provided on fair, reasonable and non-discriminatory terms and in a way that does not enable or facilitate collusion. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "when data access is warranted it must be granted on fair, reasonable and non-discriminatory terms"</sub>

## Other issues - Cross-border sharing

- **descriptive / context**: Section 6 recommendations addressing these other issues include adopting measures to prevent de-risking (para 62) and active facilitation by the public sector (paras 51-52). (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "adopting measures to prevent de-risking (para.62)"</sub>

## SECTION SIX: What are the key recommendations for effectively implementing a private sector information-sharing initiative for AML/CFT/CPF purposes while complying with DPP rules?

- **recommended / recommendation**: Overarching recommendation 1: prepare a data protection impact assessment (DPIA) to clearly define the purpose and objectives of the information sharing, the data to be processed and why it is necessary and reasonable/proportional to achieve the stated purpose, and the legal basis and safeguards to be applied. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "To prepare a data protection impact assessment (DPIA) to clearly define the purpose and objectives of the information sharing"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Overarching recommendation 2: engage with applicable DPP authorities from the beginning of the sharing project, at the design stage. Because DPP authorities may lack resources to engage with individual organisations, general engagement could occur through a sector-wide or industry group-led approach, which may also communicate lessons more quickly and ensure lessons learned are not treated as proprietary information. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "To engage with applicable DPP authorities from the beginning of the sharing project, at the design stage."</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Overarching recommendation 3: consider safeguards to adequately protect customer data, including PETs and anonymisation/pseudonymisation where useful. Footnote 39 notes that DPP requirements differ depending on the extent of anonymisation; under the GDPR, anonymous data (no personal reference and not relatable to a person) is not subject to GDPR requirements, unlike pseudonymised data. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "To consider safeguards to adequately protect customer data, including PET and anonymisation/pseudonymisation"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FATF states there is no one-size-fits-all solution addressing all AML/CFT/CPF and DPP objectives for all financial institutions globally; initiatives differ in objectives, DPP legal requirements, technologies and modes of operation, and so rely on different legal bases and mitigation measures. A key element is to involve a range of stakeholders, take account of local regulation and context, take a phased approach and build public trust and understanding. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There is no one-size-fits-all solution that addresses all AML/CFT/CPF and DPP objectives for all financial institutions globally."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Section 6 recommendations are in addition to the Suggested Actions to Support the Use of New Technologies for AML/CFT identified in the first phase of the project, and apply to public and private sector entities interested in developing private sector information sharing initiatives. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The recommendations below are in addition to the Suggested Actions to Support the Use of New Technologies for AML/CFT"</sub>

## Recommendations relevant to the public sector - Public sector should consider taking an active facilitation role

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Focus group discussions found that having the national AML/CFT/CPF co-ordinating agency, a supervisor or an FIU take an active role in spearheading sharing initiatives generally helps private sector institutions overcome challenges, particularly legal ones, and may facilitate engagement of other authorities (DPP authorities, consumer protection, competition) and co-ordination with international counterparts on cross-border initiatives. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "take an active role in spearheading information sharing initiatives generally helps private sector institutions overcome a number of challenges"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: The report stresses that none of this is required by the current FATF Standards, but says authorities wishing to facilitate private sector information sharing could, for example, consider updating existing legal or supervisory instruments to permit sharing or to create an exception or exemption to sharing restrictions, in jurisdictions with no specific legislation or supervisory instruments on the lawfulness of such sharing for AML/CFT/CPF purposes. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Consider updating existing legal or supervisory instruments, either to permit sharing or to provide an exception/exemption to restrictions on sharing."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report describes primary legislation as the firmest legal basis for allowing data sharing and processing for AML/CFT/CPF purposes while supplying the needed DPP safeguards. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Such primary legislation provides the strongest legal basis to allow data sharing and processing for AML/CFT/CPF purposes"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Convergence in the DPP regimes of different jurisdictions, for example through efforts in multilateral fora, can facilitate the design of cross-border initiatives that respect DPP rules, but active government engagement in these efforts is described as vital. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "but active government engagement in these efforts is vital"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FATF states that the facilitation actions below are in no way a requirement under the current FATF Standards, and that authorities interested in facilitating private sector sharing (whether or not AML/CFT/CPF competent authorities) could take them. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "While in no way a requirement under the current FATF Standards"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Examples cited for primary legislation: the IAS case (Box 4.4) shows the DPP authority regularly certifying the initiative in the Netherlands; in Singapore, MAS has prepared the draft regulatory and legislative framework for sharing between financial institutions under COSMIC and conducted public consultations before launch; the Netherlands Ministry of Finance has taken the initiative to introduce a legislative amendment enabling full-scale collective transaction monitoring for TMNL; and the U.S. 314(b) case also demonstrates the utility of primary legislation. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Ministry of Finance of the Netherlands has taken the initiative to introduce a legislative amendment"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Pilot programmes allow initial, more limited sharing to measure anticipated benefits and assess whether expansion is necessary. The TriBank pilot (Box 4.1) followed an initial regulatory sandbox by the data processor and the UK DPP authority that assessed key data protection issues. The safeFBDC and EuroDaT cases reflect inclusion of the AML/CFT use case in broader German government pilots, and Estonia's AML Bridge included government involvement to set and reform government data management frameworks. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The TriBank pilot (Section 4, Box 4.1) was established following an initial regulatory sandbox"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Authorities could devise a national AML/CFT/CPF information sharing strategy highlighting priority areas of financial crime or typologies, or key data types that would benefit most from sharing (for example with input from law enforcement and the FIU), to guide private sector initiatives to align with national strategy or national risk assessment results. Singapore's COSMIC targets three priority risk areas from its national risk assessment, and the Netherlands' TMNL aligns with the National Action Plan against Money Laundering. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Devise a national AML/CFT/CPF information sharing strategy, highlighting the priority areas of financial crime or typologies"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Among the things the report says authorities could do, though not required by the FATF Standards, is to use regulatory sandboxes, pilot programmes or other mechanisms to test information-sharing initiatives, particularly ones using new technologies, in a controlled way. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Make use of regulatory sandboxes, pilot programmes or other mechanisms to test information-sharing initiatives"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report says that, because several regulatory frameworks intersect, joint sandboxes, for example with AML, DPP and competition authorities involved, are a particularly useful platform for testing AML/CFT/CPF data sharing initiatives. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "joint sandboxes provide a particularly useful platform for testing AML/CFT/CPF data sharing initiatives"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The report states that AML/CFT authorities should engage with other stakeholders, including authorities for innovation, technology or digital policy and academics or experts, to learn about the latest developments and encourage broader information sharing pilot programmes to consider including AML/CFT use-cases. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AML/CFT authorities should engage with other stakeholders"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Authorities could identify a leading or co-ordinating agency and establish a contact point on private sector information sharing, for example through an existing forum such as a public-private partnership (PPP). The contact point should consider establishing contact and maintaining dialogue with DPP and other government departments (such as digital innovation) to allow consistent advice, and close engagement helps identify whether an initiative is creating risk displacement to non-involved institutions so government can respond (for example by encouraging participation from all regulated entities). (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The responsible contact point should consider taking a role in establishing contact and maintaining dialogue with DPP"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The degree of public sector involvement varies: in Singapore's COSMIC the supervisor MAS led development; in the UK case the DPP authority (the ICO) took an active role engaging the proponent; in Estonia's AML Bridge the DPP authority, FIU and Financial Supervision Authority participate in the Steering Committee and/or act in an advisory capacity. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Singapore’s COSMIC case study (Section 4, Box 4.2) illustrates how the AML/CFT/CPF competent authority"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Where the legal framework permits, authorities could consider going beyond guidance to certify specific data processing arrangements; the Netherlands' IAS operates under certification from the data protection authority, and the Canadian Consumer Privacy Protection Act gives the Privacy Commissioner the ability to approve a Code of Practice for a data processing arrangement. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "authorities could also consider going beyond guidance to certify specific data processing arrangements"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Another item the report lists for authorities is to provide guidance, checklists or other reference materials on the legal and supervisory provisions governing information exchange, including the scope of exemptions or exceptions, for example how far institutions permitted to share to detect fraud may share information on related ML, or how far, where sharing is permitted for ML, they may share on predicate offences. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Provide guidance, checklists, or other reference materials setting out the relevant legal/supervisory provisions governing information exchange."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Where guidance on information sharing is issued, AML/CFT/CPF authorities and DPP authorities should make sure it is consistent and collaborative rather than issued in isolation. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AML/CFT/CPF and DPP authorities should ensure that it is consistent and collaborative, and not issued in isolation"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: The report lists, among options for authorities, exploring the feasibility of building a secured platform for private sector information sharing, described as the most direct way to supply the financial and technological resources needed for sharing. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Explore the feasibility of building a secured platform for private sector information sharing."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: COSMIC and AML Bridge are secure digital platforms allowing (or that will allow) sharing among participating financial institutions, and in Germany's safeFBDC and EuroDaT authorities are involved in developing a platform or trustee. When building such platforms, governments and participants could consider using standardised or open-source technology for data security and/or technology meeting relevant public certifications to promote trust in data security. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "governments and participants could consider using standardised or open-source technology for data security"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Authorities could develop projects to promote data interoperability and consistency, such as common data standards and definitions or data cleaning/structuring initiatives for AML/CFT/CPF data or entries used in STRs, led by AML/CFT/CPF authorities (for example supervisors or the FIU) or digital innovation departments. These could shorten data preparation time; for example Singapore conducted separate exercises to harmonise data formats a few years before implementing its sharing initiative. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Develop projects to promote data interoperability and consistency."</sub>

## Public sector should ensure and promote regular dialogue between DPP and AML/CFT/CPF authorities

- **descriptive / context**: Under the Phase 1 Stocktake Report and FATF Recommendation 2, national co-operation and co-ordination for AML/CFT/CPF requires interaction between AML and DPP authorities to ensure compatibility of AML/CFT/CPF requirements with DPP rules. The dialogue is also vital for education so AML authorities understand the scope, nature and objectives of the DPP framework and vice versa. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "requires interaction between AML and DPP authorities to ensure the compatibility of AML/CFT/CPF requirements with DPP rules"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: International collaboration and co-ordination, bilateral and multilateral, can help share lessons, encourage discussion and promote consistency in cross-border sharing in line with required data protection. Government outreach could also extend to relevant civil society groups (for example NPOs or advocacy organisations with a DPP focus or working on financial exclusion) to provide assurance that DPP principles will be upheld and address concerns about financial exclusion. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Collaboration and co-ordination at the international level (both bilateral and multilateral) can help share lessons"</sub>

## Recommendations relevant to the public sector - Public sector should consider taking an active facilitation role

- **descriptive / context**: The report says such a platform may keep sharing initiatives accessible and affordable for smaller institutions, including those outside the financial sector, which reduces the risk of risk displacement when sharing is limited to major institutions. It adds that a platform could in the long run pave the way for national public-private or cross-border sharing, which are not currently FATF Standards requirements except for group-wide information sharing. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This approach may also help ensure data sharing initiatives are accessible and affordable for smaller institutions"</sub>

## Public sector should ensure and promote regular dialogue between DPP and AML/CFT/CPF authorities

- **expected / expectation**: The report's heading states that the public sector should ensure and promote regular dialogue between DPP authorities and AML/CFT/CPF authorities. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Public sector should ensure and promote regular dialogue between DPP and AML/CFT/CPF authorities"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In many of the more advanced sharing initiatives, the lead AML/CFT/CPF authorities have involved their DPP counterparts in regular meetings to discuss solutions and measures that facilitate private sector sharing and to spot potential inconsistency or lack of clarity. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the leading AML/CFT/CPF authorities have engaged or included their DPP counterparts in regular meetings"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Among practical suggestions for how authorities could interact, AML/CFT/CPF and DPP authorities could consider issuing joint guidance, joint statements or other communications to achieve greater policy harmonisation. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AML/CFT/CPF and DPP authorities could consider joint guidance or statements or other communications"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: If countries pursue legislative gateways for sharing, AML authorities should work closely with DPP authorities to ensure that laws and requirements are coherent. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AML authorities should work closely with DPP authorities to ensure coherence of laws and requirements"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: AML authorities should facilitate industry engagement with DPP authorities as appropriate; the report adds it may be useful to include DPP authorities in private sector consultation or sector-wide engagement. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AML authorities should facilitate industry engagement with DPP authorities as appropriate."</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Practical suggestions for authorities include regular forums bringing together AML/CFT/CPF authorities, DPP authorities and private sector institutions to discuss policy and operational issues, and joint initiatives such as joint regulatory sandboxes or joint guidance on how DPP and AML/CFT/CPF legislation interacts. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Regular forums that bring together AML/CFT/CPF authorities, DPP authorities, and private sector institutions"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: AML/CFT/CPF authorities could co-ordinate with DPP authorities to devise a strategy on information sharing and promote private sector sharing protected with proper safeguards for digital security and data protection/privacy. DPP authorities could provide guidance or support, such as on technological solutions that reduce DPP risks (minimising personal data shared or pseudonymising) or sector-wide engagement. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "To encourage industry initiatives, AML/CFT/CPF authorities could co- ordinate with DPP authorities"</sub>

## Recommendations relevant to the private sector - Private sector should consider the application of PETs

- **recommended / recommendation**: The FATF states that PETs can help support DPP compliance, though they are not a 'silver bullet': they can reduce or eliminate data movement, minimise personal data shared, and pseudonymise, anonymise or encrypt shared data. AML/CFT/CPF experts, technology providers and DPP experts should be engaged in discussions so the technical design and output match the objectives, the PET is fit-for-purpose and data security risks are managed. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "PETs can help support compliance with DPP requirements, even if they are not a"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report says that when PETs and AI technology are used properly and consistently with DPP rules and obligations, they have the potential to make processing more accurate, reliable, objective and secure. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "PETs and AI technology have the potential to enable more accurate, reliable, objective and secure processing"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: PETs also need to be accessible to smaller institutions, which form the majority of obliged entities, and attention should be paid to interoperability of different technologies (for example by using technologies conforming to accepted standards) to allow broader engagement. The UK's FutureFlow platform uses pseudonymisation to remove/clean personal data in transaction data (such as account identifiers, transaction values, transaction IDs and timestamps) before exchange, to minimise re-identification risk. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "PETs also need to be accessible to smaller institutions, which form the majority of obliged entities."</sub>

## Private sector should take steps towards data preparation

- **descriptive / context**: New data-sharing or data-pooling technologies, especially advanced analytics, work best with common data standards and formats, and interoperable formats help improve data accuracy and reliability. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "New data-sharing or data-pooling technologies, especially advanced analytics, work best with common data standards and formats."</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Private sector institutions could consider strategies including using existing data prepared in structured format (for example data fields used in SWIFT), introducing data cleansing initiatives prior to sharing, or assigning technology providers (particularly if they are a participant) to plan and implement data cleansing/structuring. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Private sector institutions could consider a number of strategies"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In the UK's TriBank Initiative, participating FIs spent significant time in early stages ensuring all participants could share data seamlessly via the digital platform, and Estonia's AML Bridge also noted significant time and effort spent resolving less visible challenges such as data preparedness. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "participating financial institutions had to spend a significant amount of time during the early stages of the project"</sub>

## Private sector should pursue data protection by design

- **expected / expectation**: The report's heading states that the private sector should pursue data protection by design. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Private sector should pursue data protection by design"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report describes taking DPP principles into account during the design phase of an information sharing initiative as key to its success, and says privacy risk assessments or DPIAs offer an analytical framework to assess DPP compliance and to identify and mitigate DPP risks. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Considering DPP principles in the design phase of an information sharing initiative is key to its success."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Based on the advanced initiatives it describes, the report says the legal basis for sharing, processing and storing personal data, and the mitigation measures adopted, should be tailored to the project, its objectives and the way data is used or processed; where applicable a proper assessment of suitable legal bases should also be undertaken. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should be tailored to the project, its objectives and the use/processing of data"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Taking data protection into account at the beginning of a project also allows participants to pivot projects to align with DPP requirements, saving resources. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "allows participants to pivot projects to align with relevant DPP requirements, thereby saving resources"</sub>

## Box 6.1. What should a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) include?

- **permissive / recommendation**: The exact DPIA requirements depend on the relevant DPP legislation and features of the sharing initiative, but a DPIA could include the specific purposes and goals of the project. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The exact requirements for a DPIA will depend on the relevant DPP legislation"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: A DPIA could include identification of the legal authority/basis that authorises or compels the parties to engage in the ML/TF/PF detection and investigation arrangements, with in-depth verification that the conditions of the legal basis are met, and an analysis of whether other arrangements could achieve similar results or the intended benefits. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Identification of a legal authority/basis that authorises or compels the parties to engage in these ML/TF/PF detection"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: A DPIA could clarify which parties undertake key data protection roles (controller, joint controller and/or processor), noting any contracts between controllers and processors, and describe how the parties will collect, use, disclose/share, store, and later delete or destroy the data. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Clarify which parties are undertaking key data protection roles in respect of the data utilised"</sub>

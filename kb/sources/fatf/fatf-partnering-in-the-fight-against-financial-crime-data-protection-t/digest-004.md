## Box 4.3 TMNL (Netherlands)

- **descriptive / method**: In the TMNL initiative, a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) is conducted as part of a 'privacy by design' approach by the banks (the data controllers) and TMNL (the data processor) to assess the key GDPR principles. Data privacy and security measures are formally agreed in the user agreement between TMNL and the banks, and privacy considerations are separately made, aligned and documented for each analytical model. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) is conducted by the banks (being the data controllers) and TMNL (data processor)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The starting point for the TMNL DPIA is the legitimate interest of the participating banks, as controllers, in strengthening their role as gatekeeper to the financial system and acting on legal AML requirements. The considerations also rest on the necessity of processing data for better multibank detection (described in the National Action Plan against Money Laundering) and on testing confirming that TMNL could enhance AML effectiveness. Proportionality of the processing was carefully considered, with purpose limitation and data minimisation described as critical principles. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the legitimate interest of the participating banks as controllers to enhance their formal role as gatekeeper"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: TMNL applies data quality analysis and remediation processes, performed both by banks and at TMNL level, to assess whether data items are suitable for use in analytical models. The data cannot leave the TMNL IT platform to any other external party. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The data itself cannot leave the TMNL IT platform to any other (external) party."</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The TMNL IT platform is subject to the highest IT security standards, including assessment and continuous monitoring and testing of security measures. The working of the analytical models is governed by a model risk framework aimed at ensuring valid, reliable, fair and transparent models. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "governed by a model risk framework aimed at ensuring valid, reliable, fair and transparent analytical models"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: For further steps towards a full transaction monitoring utility, restrictions and uncertainties from AML and privacy law are described as blocking. More legal clarity and certainty on data sharing, and on the tension between AML and privacy legislation, are said to be required for utilities like TMNL to reach full effectiveness. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "restrictions and uncertainties from AML and privacy law are blocking"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The case study states that if AML standards described more precisely what information could or should be shared for both AML detection and review processes, this could enable deeper analysis with better outcomes, as well as less data processing and less impact on low risk actors. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this could enable both deeper analysis with better outcomes, as well as less data processing"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: To operationalise TMNL, the Dutch banks are in close dialogue with a number of public partners, and authorities are currently involved in developing legislation to further support the initiative. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Authorities are currently involved to develop legislation to further support this initiative."</sub>

## Box 4.3. TMNL (Netherlands)

- **descriptive / limitation**: In the TMNL case study, the Dutch experience is described as showing that the existing legal framework allows some data sharing but carries significant restrictions and uncertainties; TMNL's current operating model stays within those limits and is cautious about the uncertainties, yet it falls short of enabling full AML effectiveness. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "does not yet enable full AML effectiveness"</sub>

## Box 4.4 Incident Alert System (IAS) for Financial Institutions (Netherlands)

- **descriptive / context**: The Incident Alert System (IAS) is a post-suspicion sharing mechanism relating to fraud. It shares identifying information on persons involved in fraud incidents, based on an initial hit/no-hit alert system, to detect potential customer risks. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Post-suspicion sharing of identifying information on persons involved in fraud incidents"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The IAS was established in 1997 so that participating financial institutions (FIs) can help each other identify and prevent fraud, alerting each other to customers, employees or other persons involved in fraud incidents. Since 1990 FIs' Security Departments have recorded fraud incidents on an internal incidents register; from 1997 certain elements of those registers are included on an external register accessible to other participating FIs on a hit/no-hit basis. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The IAS was established in 1997 to allow participating financial institutions (FIs) to help each other identify and prevent fraud."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The IAS is open to all FIs that are members of a recognised trade association (Dutch Banking Association; Association of Insurers; Association of Financing Companies in the Netherlands; Mortgage Fraud Prevention Foundation; or Health Insurers Netherlands). Banks not in the Dutch Banking Association and insurers not in the Association of Insurers can be admitted as participants case by case. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The IAS is open to all FIs that are members of a recognised trade association"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Each participating FI in the IAS remains the data controller. The IAS is managed by the Credit Registration Bureau (BKR) for data from other financial institutions and by the Central Information System Foundation (CIS) for data from insurance entities; these entities manage the external register and act as data processors. A Guidance Committee of representatives from some participating institutions oversees the register to ensure uniform application and respect for the rules. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Each participating FI remains the data controller."</sub>

## Box 4.4. Incident Alert System (IAS) for Financial Institutions (Netherlands)

- **descriptive / requirement**: A person is entered on the IAS external register only if three conditions are met: they broke or tried to break the law in a way that threatens the FI's customers or employees, the FI, or the wider financial sector; there are enough grounds to tie them to the matter that a criminal complaint or report is, or will be, made (or would be, were that not disproportionate to the offence or harmful to law enforcement); and proportionality is respected. (p. 32)[^original]

## Box 4.4 Incident Alert System (IAS) for Financial Institutions (Netherlands)

- **descriptive / method**: The IAS allows participating FIs to search the external register on a hit/no-hit basis to be alerted to fraud incidents involving the searched person. It also provides for retrospective hits: where a query results in a hit in the subsequent two months, the consulting FI is alerted to the new entry. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "where a query results in a hit in the subsequent two months, the consulting FI will be alerted"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Where an IAS hit is received, an FI can request additional data to understand why the customer was entered on the register. The requested FI considers these requests case by case, and any exchange must be adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary for the purpose of access. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These requests are considered on a case-by- case basis by the requested FI."</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: The IAS external register contains identifying data on individuals involved in the fraud incident, for example name, address, date of birth, nationality and IBAN. Initial consultation is hit/no-hit, so no identifying data is shared on first consultation; if there is a hit, the FI's Security Department must determine whether to seek further information, taking DPP requirements into account. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "meaning no identifying data is shared upon first consultation"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Data sharing under the IAS is permitted under the GDPR for the legitimate interest of detecting and preventing fraud (art.6(1)(f)). The Dutch General Data Protection Act (UAVG) requires certification of any processing of personal data; the IAS has been certified, which required the data protection authority (DPA) to assess the system for compliance with DPP requirements. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Dutch General Data Protection Act (UAVG) requires the certification of any processing of personal data."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: On proportionality, IAS sharing is limited to a narrowly defined group of FIs and is initially hit/no-hit only. Additional sharing occurs only when necessary and case by case, and the requested FI's Security Department is required to consider whether it is proportionate. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Data sharing under the IAS is limited to a narrowly-defined group of FIs."</sub>
- **descriptive / requirement**: Participating FIs in the IAS are obliged to delete data from the register where it is no longer relevant (for example where inclusion is no longer necessary to prevent fraud) or within 8 years. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Participating FIs are obliged to delete data from the register where it is no longer relevant"</sub>
- **descriptive / requirement**: On data quality and accuracy, participating FIs in the IAS are obliged to correct, remove or supplement data on the external register as necessary to ensure accuracy, and any information entered must be traceable and documented. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Participating FIs are obliged to correct, remove or supplement data on the external register as necessary to ensure accuracy."</sub>
- **descriptive / requirement**: Under the IAS, the data subject is required to be notified of their inclusion on the register, unless notification would be against the interest of preventing, detecting and prosecuting criminal offences or the protection of the data subject. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The data subject is required to be notified of their inclusion on the register"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: On fairness in automated decisions, the IAS external register may be queried automatically, but each hit must be reviewed at both ends (the requesting FI and the FI that entered the data) to ensure it is not a false match. Any request for information beyond a hit must be considered manually by the Security Department of both the requesting and the requested FI. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "each hit must be reviewed at both ends"</sub>

## Box 4.4. Incident Alert System (IAS) for Financial Institutions (Netherlands)

- **descriptive / context**: Where the accuracy or legitimacy of data registered in the IAS is disputed, the individual concerned can first turn to the board or management of the relevant FI, and if the matter stays unresolved can go to an outside body such as the Financial Services Complaints Institute, the DPA or the competent court. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the data subject can approach the board/management of the relevant FI"</sub>

## Box 4.4 Incident Alert System (IAS) for Financial Institutions (Netherlands)

- **descriptive / requirement**: Under the IAS the data subject has the right to inspect, correct, delete and object to data on the register. In most cases such requests must be answered within one month with full reasoning; where confirmation and access cannot be granted (for example where necessary in the interests of preventing, detecting and prosecuting criminal offences), the internal decision must be recorded. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In most cases, such requests must be responded to within one month with full reasoning."</sub>
- **descriptive / requirement**: IAS register data can only be processed for the specific reasons of preventing and detecting fraud set out in the IAS operating protocol. Participating FIs each commit to ensure the data cannot be further processed or used in additional ways or in any way incompatible with this purpose. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Participating FIs each commit to ensure that the data cannot be further processed or used in additional ways"</sub>
- **descriptive / requirement**: Only designated authorised officers can access the IAS to query the register, and each must be subject to a duty of confidentiality. Beyond the initial hit/no-hit query, any exchange of identifying information takes place exclusively between the Security Departments of the participating FIs. Each participating FI commits to maintaining data security and evaluating applicable security measures every two years, and FIs must put in place a procedure for data leaks consistent with the GDPR. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Only designated authorised officers are able to access the IAS to query the register."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: On financial inclusion, inclusion on an FI's internal incident register may coincide with a decision not to offer services or to exit a relationship, and authorities are sensitive to the possibility that inclusion on the external register may lead to similar decisions. To prevent financial exclusion, FIs have committed to continue providing access to services concerning the basic needs of the individual (for example basic bank accounts or basic insurance). (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs have committed to continue to provide access to services concerning the basic needs of the individual"</sub>
- **descriptive / requirement**: In deciding whether to provide services to a customer, FIs can only take into account IAS information after receiving advice from the FI's security department (which manages any data exchange). FIs cannot act solely on the basis of a 'hit' without checking the reason for inclusion. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs cannot act solely on the basis of a"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The IAS project was assessed by the DPA for compliance with DPP requirements and approved, and its certification was renewed in 2021. Participating FIs are obligated to re-evaluate the protocol every two years, taking into account developments in DPP regulation and jurisprudence; if re-evaluation warrants an amendment, the DPA has to be consulted for renewed approval. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "was assessed by the DPA for compliance with DPP requirements, and was approved"</sub>

## Box 4.5 Section 314(b) (U.S.)

- **descriptive / definition**: Section 314(b) of the USA PATRIOT Act is a legal gateway allowing information sharing between financial institutions, under a safe harbour offering protection from liability, in order to better identify and report activities that may involve ML or TF. It covers both pre- and post-suspicion private-private sharing. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Legal gateway allowing information sharing between financial institutions, under a safe harbour"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: FinCEN data from 2017-2019 shows that on average 15 900 suspicious activity reports per year reference the use of section 314(b) sharing. Filers had either sent a 314(b) request to support their own enquiry or received a 314(b) request that prompted their own analysis and a SAR. Over 2017-2019 there is a trend towards an increasing number of institutions referencing 314(b) sharing in their reports. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "on average 15 900 suspicious activity reports per year reference the use of section 314(b) sharing"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Participation in section 314(b) sharing is voluntary and open to all financial institutions subject to an AML program requirement under FinCEN regulations, and any association of such institutions. Entities wishing to share must be registered with FinCEN. At the end of 2019 there were 7 000 participating institutions, mostly banks and credit unions but also casinos, securities firms, insurance companies and money service businesses. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Engaging in sharing under section 314(b) is voluntary"</sub>

## Box 4.5. Section 314(b) (U.S.)

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Section 314(b) information sharing lets financial institutions collect more information on customers or transactions that may relate to ML/TF, including accounts, activities and linked entities or individuals not previously known to them. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Gather additional information on customers or transactions potentially related to ML/TF"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Sharing under section 314(b) helps institutions follow complex financial trails that are layered across many institutions, entities and jurisdictions, and build a fuller and more accurate picture of a customer's possible ML/TF activity for due diligence and transaction monitoring decisions. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Shed more light on overall financial trails, especially if they are complex"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Section 314(b) sharing lets participating institutions alert one another to a customer whose suspicious activity they were unaware of, and supports filing more comprehensive suspicious activity reports than would otherwise be filed. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Alert other participating financial institutions to a customer of whose suspicious activities"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Section 314(b) sharing aids detection of ML/TF methods and schemes, and supports efficient SAR decisions, for instance when a fuller picture obtained through voluntary sharing leads an institution to conclude no SAR is needed for transactions that first looked suspicious. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Identify and aid in the detection of ML/TF methods and schemes."</sub>

## Box 4.5 Section 314(b) (U.S.)

- **descriptive / context**: All participating institutions are covered by regulations that provide a safe harbour for sharing and require relevant controls, which are periodically tested by supervisors. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "All participating institutions are covered by regulations which provide a safe harbour for sharing"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Section 314(b) allows information to be shared one-to-one or one-to-many between organisations registered with FinCEN, in writing, verbally or using available technologies. Organisations sharing under 314(b) must maintain adequate procedures to protect the security and confidentiality of all information shared. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Information may be shared on a one-to-one or a one-to-many basis between organisations registered with FinCEN under 314(b)"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Participating institutions may share information on individuals, entities, organisations and countries to identify and, where appropriate, report activities that may involve possible terrorist activity or money laundering. Section 314(b) and its regulations impose no limitation on sharing personally identifiable information provided sharing is otherwise consistent with them, and do not restrict the type or medium of information (for example video surveillance footage or IP addresses may be shared). (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "impose no limitations on the sharing of personally identifiable information"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Section 314(b) provides the lawful basis for sharing, protected by a safe harbour from liability for sharing consistent with it. Sharing is appropriate when the institution or association has a reasonable basis to believe the information relates to activities that may involve money laundering or terrorist activity, and shares it for an appropriate purpose under 314(b). (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "has a reasonable basis to believe the information shared relates to activities that may involve money laundering or terrorist activity"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: On proportionality, the sharing institution must have a reasonable basis to believe the information relates to activities (for example fraudulent transactions or cyber events) that may ultimately be related to ML/TF. The case study says sharing within the 314(b) parameters is implicitly reasonable and proportionate to the purposes of detecting potential ML/TF-related activity and protecting the public from financial crime and terrorism. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the sharing institution must have a reasonable basis to believe that the information relates to activities"</sub>

## Box 4.5. Section 314(b) (U.S.)

- **descriptive / limitation**: Section 314(b) gives no authority for a participating institution to share a suspicious activity report itself, or any information that would disclose that such a report exists; institutions that share information can nonetheless collaborate on a joint report. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "section 314(b) does not authorise a participating financial institution to share a suspicious activity report itself"</sub>
- **descriptive / requirement**: Information received through section 314(b) sharing may be used only for the purposes set by the section and its implementing regulations. As far as the excerpt goes, these include identifying and, where appropriate, reporting activities that may involve ML/TF, determining whether to open or maintain an account or enter a transaction, and assisting AML compliance. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Data shared pursuant to Section 314(b) may only be used for the purposes laid out in the section"</sub>

## Box 4.5 Section 314(b) (U.S.)

- **descriptive / context**: Financial institutions using 314(b) may improve the accuracy and integrity of their information, including information that may need to be reported to FinCEN in a SAR. The case study says the sharing is done with human oversight, which helps ensure fairness to the individuals involved. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "engaging in information sharing, with human oversight"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Financial institutions must establish and maintain adequate procedures to protect the security and confidentiality of all information shared under section 314(b), including designating a point of contact for receiving and providing information. Before sharing, they must take reasonable steps to verify that the receiving institution is registered for 314(b) sharing; FinCEN provides a list of participants for this purpose. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial institutions must establish and maintain adequate procedures to protect the security and confidentiality"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Financial institutions wishing to use section 314(b) are required to register with FinCEN and to update their registration annually. Government agencies including FinCEN are not involved with and do not see specific information shared under the 314(b) safe harbour unless the sharing is referenced in a SAR. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "are required to register with FinCEN and to update their registration on an annual basis"</sub>

## Box 4.6 AML Bridge (Estonia)

- **descriptive / method**: AML Bridge is a secure digital platform provided by an independent third-party company. It allows member banks to exchange pseudonymised data (largely transaction data) with one or more other banks in end-to-end encrypted format. Each member defines the list of receiving institutions for each exchange, and information is shared near real time to pursue collaborative investigations. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AML Bridge is a secure digital platform provided by an independent 3rd party company."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: As of March 2022, AML Bridge had seen 1200 private-private collaborative investigations completed since its establishment in July 2021, roughly 150 cases per month. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AML Bridge has seen 1200 private-private"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Half of AML Bridge cases (over 600) involved ML investigations, which has increased the quality of STRs submitted, including by promoting joint STRs, and has helped clear non-suspicious customers more quickly (reducing false positives). (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Half of these cases (over 600) involved ML investigations"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: One third of AML Bridge cases (over 400) relate to 'scam fraud', generally a form of authorised push payment (APP) fraud. Approximately EUR 3 million has been recovered from criminals and returned to victims with AML Bridge's help, and most urgent fraud cases can be resolved in under 15 minutes. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Approximately EUR 3 million has been recovered from criminals and returned to victims"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Collaborative investigations related to sanctions evasion were initially a small category but, following Russia's invasion of Ukraine in March 2022, accounted for the majority of AML Bridge usage, with weekly volumes quadrupling during March 2022. The dominant use case is quickly clearing new exact-match false positives, and the network is observing opportunities to share information about close associates and owned companies of sanctioned persons and entities. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The dominant use case is quickly clearing new exact-match false positives"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: AML Bridge was first launched between four of Estonia's largest banks (collectively 90% of transactions) and has since expanded to all 10 Estonian banks and several non-banks. The platform provider is a data processor under the GDPR. Roles include CEOs (executive support), MLROs (Steering Committee members), Data Protection Officers (advisors), information security teams, and bank project leads; end-users are the banks' crime-fighting teams (AML/CFT/CPF, sanctions screening, transaction monitoring, anti-fraud). (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "has since expanded to include all 10 Estonian banks and several non-banks in the country"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Data shared on AML Bridge is mostly transaction data, shared near real time, and its structure is configurable according to the suspected offence and network members. 'Scenario templates' let participants define the input fields the recipient needs to identify the subject (for example customer name, account number, transaction ID) and the specific data requested (for example full name, date of birth, source of wealth, payment reason, risk level, potential red flags, copies of documents). Sharing covers enquiries about potential ML, sanctions evasion, fraud and related incidents. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "allows participants to define the input fields required for the recipient in order to identify the subject"</sub>

## Box 4.6. AML Bridge (Estonia): post-suspicion private-private information sharing initiative

- **descriptive / method**: AML Bridge runs an information security management system covering strict access control (Multi-Factor Authentication and IP whitelisting), encryption in transit and at rest, disaster recovery with backups and regular testing, and audit logs. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The AML Bridge has an information security management system in place which includes strict access control"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Security documentation for AML Bridge, including audit reports, is open to all participants. A qualified third party tests the platform's security at least once a year, and every participating institution has the right to run its own penetration testing; one bank has done so and passed the results to other participants. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The security of the platform is tested at least annually by a qualified third party"</sub>

## Box 4.6 AML Bridge (Estonia)

- **descriptive / context**: As controllers, each bank must have a legal basis to share through AML Bridge; in most cases this is 'compliance with the law' or 'legitimate interest' (GDPR, Article 6.1(e)). Estonia's ML/TF Prevention Act allows financial institutions to share personal data for collaboration purposes (section 16) and allows certain data subject privacy rights to be restricted on public interest grounds (section 48). Each participating bank signs a Data Processing Addendum with the platform provider. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "financial institutions are allowed to share personal data for collaboration purposes (section 16)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The extent and amount of data shared, its geographical scope and retention periods are defined by the banks using AML Bridge. The platform design limits the amount and type of data shared to help institutions minimise sharing, and it has extensive audit logging to help banks conduct reviews and quality assurance checks and identify unreasonable actions. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the platform has extensive audit logging to help banks conduct reviews and quality assurance checks"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Data Processing Addendums stipulate that the platform provider will give the controller bank all reasonable assistance in meeting data subject rights requests, and any such requests received by the provider are forwarded to the bank with all relevant information. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they are forwarded to the bank with all relevant information"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: AML Bridge uses end-to-end password-based encryption. All messages are encrypted with a private plus public key pair; to decrypt messages a user must access their private key by entering a password different from their main login password, and neither the platform provider nor any other party has access to this key. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AML Bridge uses end to end password-based encryption."</sub>

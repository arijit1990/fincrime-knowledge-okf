## SECTION ONE: Introduction

- **permissive / context**: In line with the applicable legal framework, countries or entities may decide that private sector information sharing is required to effectively mitigate ML/TF/PF risks, in particular to identify suspicious transactions. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries or entities may decide that private sector information sharing is required to effectively mitigate"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Based on FATF discussions with private and public sectors, it is increasingly difficult for a single private sector entity to identify suspicious transactions in complex schemes designed to avoid detection. Intricate ML/TF/PF schemes involving complex legal arrangements and transaction patterns are difficult or impossible to detect without information from counterparty banks or other banks serving the same customer or associates. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "difficult or impossible to detect without information from counterparty banks or other banks providing services"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: As transaction numbers grow, it may become more complicated for transaction monitoring systems to pinpoint suspicious activity. Without access to and processing of additional information among private sector entities, these systems may capture irrelevant transactions and report false positives; appropriately tailored sharing of data among FIs or with FIUs can improve detection and reduce false positives, so legitimate customers and transactions would no longer be flagged as suspicious. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is a risk that these systems may be capturing transactions which are not relevant, and reporting false positives"</sub>
- **permissive / finding or result**: Where entities investigate potentially suspicious clients or activity in silos, more data has to be collected and recorded because each entity investigates separately. Information sharing among private sector entities may have the potential to reduce data collection at different points and pinpoint suspicious activity more accurately, leading to better outcomes and customer experiences if proper DPP safeguards are in place, and may reduce operational burden of analysing large amounts of low quality data. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "every individual entity has to perform investigations of its own"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: One use case for private sector information sharing is identifying crime typologies: the aim is to spot emerging criminal typologies faster and more accurately, put safeguards in place, and share the findings with other institutions and the public sector. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to more rapidly and accurately identify emerging criminal typologies and implement safeguards"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Another use case is intelligence-driven inquiries: the aim is to line up pro-active inquiries into potentially suspicious transactions and reach firmer conclusions that support FIU or law enforcement investigations. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to align pro-active inquiries into potential suspicious transactions and reach more definitive conclusions"</sub>

## SECTION THREE: What are data protection and privacy requirements and objectives?

- **descriptive / context**: A right to privacy is enshrined in the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and the ICCPR (UDHR art.12; ICCPR art.17). It would generally include freedom from interference or intrusion and the ability to control who can see or use personal information. Although the UN has called on jurisdictions to put in place a legal framework, no single inter-governmental organisation with global membership sets specific international standards for DPP laws, and requirements have evolved differently across jurisdictions. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is no single inter-governmental organisation with a global membership that establishes specific international standards for DPP laws"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In many jurisdictions DPP legislation generally requires transparency about data collection, uses and sharing; restricts disclosure of records to situations where the individual has given free and informed consent or another lawful basis applies; and gives individuals a right of access to their data, a right to amend or suppress incorrect data, and a right to redress for privacy violations, with certain limited exceptions (e.g. law enforcement and national security). Free consent would generally require a free choice and ability to refuse or withdraw without disadvantage. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "restricts disclosure of records to situations where an individual has given free and informed consent"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Multilateral background: the UN developed the ICCPR (1976); the Global Privacy Assembly first met in 1979 and connects more than 130 data protection and privacy authorities; the OECD adopted Privacy Guidelines in 1980 (revised 2013) and is convening experts to develop principles for government access to personal data held by the private sector; the Council of Europe adopted Convention 108 in 1981 (now 55 signatories across three continents, updated to Convention 108+) and is preparing draft guidelines on data protection implications of AML/CFT/CPF exchanges. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Convention now has 55 signatories spanning three continents, and was recently updated to Convention 108+."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: European framework: Article 8 of the 1950 European Convention on Human Rights bars public authority interference with private and family life except in accordance with law and where necessary in a democratic society. The 2000 EU Charter of Fundamental Rights protects privacy (Article 7) and personal data (Article 8), requires limitations to be provided by law and only if necessary and genuinely meeting objectives of general interest (Article 52), and enshrines proportionality. In 2016 the EU adopted the directly applicable General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "limitations may only be made if they are necessary and genuinely meet the objectives of general interest"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The GDPR is supervised and enforced by data protection authorities (DPAs) in each EU and EEA Member State; the European Data Protection Board ensures consistent application, and the EDPS ensures the EU Data Protection Regulation is implemented for EU institutions. A footnote notes the Data Protection Directive (EU) 2016/680 applies to processing by competent authorities for law enforcement and so is not relevant to private-to-private information sharing. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "ensures that the GDPR is applied consistently throughout the EU"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In the Americas, the Organisation of American States Principles of Privacy and Protection of Personal Data (2021) and the Ibero-American Data Protection Network Personal Data Protection Standards (2017) identify basic elements of effective protection and common principles. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Principles of Privacy and Protection of Personal Data (Organisation of American States, 2021)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Approximately 145 jurisdictions have some type of data protection legislation. EU member states are subject to the GDPR; the UK GDPR and Data Protection Act 2018 provide a comparable UK framework. In the U.S., the Constitution prohibits unreasonable searches and seizures, and the Privacy Act of 1974 (reflecting fair information practice principles) governs federal agencies' collection, maintenance, use and dissemination of personal information, requiring transparency and remedies. Later US federal and state privacy laws are sector-specific, for example the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, which imposes privacy rules on the financial sector. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Approximately 145 jurisdictions have some type of legislation in place to ensure the protection of data and privacy."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Review of laws and discussions with DPP authorities show that frameworks, though differing in type and scope, often follow similar general principles and establish oversight and accountability mechanisms. The principles listed are: type of data, lawful authority, purpose and use, necessity/reasonableness/proportionality/minimisation, quality and integrity, fairness including automated decisions, transparency, data transfers/disclosures, data security, access/correction/other data subject rights, accountability/oversight, redress, and impact assessments. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these frameworks often follow similar general principles, and establish oversight and accountability mechanisms"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: DPP principle, type of data: protections generally apply to personal data relating to natural persons, often defined by identifiability (e.g. through a name, number, location or combination of identifying factors); different categories of personal data may exist. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "DPP protections generally apply to personal data relating to natural persons"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: DPP principle, lawful authority: in certain jurisdictions (e.g. EU) a legal basis is needed to process personal data. The GDPR has a finite list: data subject's free consent for specific purposes; processing necessary for a contract; compliance with a legal obligation; a task in the public interest or exercise of official authority; or a legitimate interest (unless there is good reason to protect the data), subject to necessity and proportionality. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Under the GDPR there is a finite list of legal bases for using/processing personal data"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: DPP principle, purpose and use: data should be processed in line with the specified, legitimate purpose and not used for incompatible purposes; it should be processed for a specific, well-defined purpose and not further processed for additional purposes unless permitted and/or compatible with the original purpose. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The data should be processed/used in line with the specified, legitimate purpose"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: DPP principle, necessity, reasonableness, proportionality and minimisation: even with a lawful basis, in certain jurisdictions processing should be necessary and reasonable or proportionate; depending on the jurisdiction data should be retained only as long as necessary, collected information should be reasonable and not excessive, or there should be no other reasonable and less intrusive way to achieve the purpose. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "data should be retained only for as long as necessary"</sub>
- **descriptive / requirement**: Under the lawful authority (lawful basis) principle, relying on the 'legitimate interest' ground generally means the data controller, being the person or entity that determines the purposes and means of processing, has to identify a legitimate interest, show that the processing is necessary to achieve it, and weigh it against the individual's interests, rights and freedoms. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "would generally need to identify a legitimate interest, show that processing is necessary to achieve it"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Institutions should consider to what extent their aims can be achieved without sharing personal data, for example by using available anonymisation technologies. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Institutions should consider to what extent their aims can be achieve without the sharing of personal data"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: DPP principle, quality and integrity: personal data should be maintained so it is accurate, reliable, complete, consistent and in context, which could include an obligation to keep data as up to date as necessary and appropriate for its purposes. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Personal data should be maintained in such a manner that the data is accurate, reliable, complete, consistent and in context."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: DPP principle, transparency: data subjects should be informed about, among other things, how and by whom their data will be processed, for which purpose and to what extent; individuals and relevant authorities should be notified of data breaches. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Data subjects should be informed about, among other things, how and by whom the data will be processed"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: DPP principle, data transfers/disclosures: in certain jurisdictions transfers or disclosures to another organisation can only occur with prior free consent, which can be withdrawn at any time; other jurisdictions may permit them under a specific legal basis (regardless of consent) and in accordance with legal restrictions ensuring continued protection, necessity and proportionality. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "transfers or disclosures of data with another organisation can only occur with prior free consent"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: DPP principle, data security: personal data must be processed using appropriate physical, technical and organisational measures to protect it, particularly against unauthorised or unlawful processing and accidental loss, destruction or damage, and records (e.g. computer audit logs) should be maintained to demonstrate how personal data is accessed, used and disclosed. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Personal data must be processed using appropriate physical, technical, and organisational measures"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: DPP principle, access, correction and other data subject rights: procedures should exist for individuals to be informed, to obtain access, and to request correction of data they assert is inaccurate, or erasure in certain jurisdictions, subject to reasonable restrictions (e.g. law enforcement and national security). Depending on the framework, rights may also include suppression or erasure, restriction of processing, and data portability (receiving personal data in a structured, commonly used, machine readable format). (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There should be procedures in place for individuals to be informed and to seek and obtain access to the data"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Under the DPP principle of fairness, including in automated decisions, processing must be fair and lawful. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Processing must be fair and lawful."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Under the same principle, decisions that can significantly adversely affect an individual's interests should not be made solely on the basis of automated processing of personal data, unless domestic law authorises it and appropriate safeguards apply. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should not be made solely on the basis of the automated processing of the personal data"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Depending on the legal framework, the restriction on solely automated decisions may apply only to the extent that the adverse impacts relate to the purpose for which the data is processed, or may apply more broadly. In some jurisdictions automated decision-making is prohibited unless specific conditions are met. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this may apply only to the extent that the adverse impacts are related to the purpose"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: DPP principle, accountability/oversight: the use and processing of data should be reviewed by one or more bodies exercising functionally independent and effective oversight, alone or cumulatively. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should be reviewed by one or more bodies that exercise functionally independent and effective oversight"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: DPP principle, redress: there should be appropriate and effective mechanisms for individuals to submit complaints and seek redress, including receipt and investigation of complaints about access, correction or alleged improper processing, subject to reasonable domestic restrictions; in certain jurisdictions this may include judicial redress before courts. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There should be appropriate and effective mechanisms to enable an individual to submit complaints and to seek redress."</sub>
- **descriptive / requirement**: DPP principle, impact assessments: in many jurisdictions laws, regulations or policies require an institution to assess DPP risks to individuals from proposed collection, use, dissemination or other processing of personal data and implement ways to minimise them; under the GDPR this is a documented 'data protection impact assessment' (DPIA) (art. 35), while US federal agencies must complete a 'privacy impact assessment'. Additional impact assessments (e.g. Human Rights Impact Assessments, Legitimate Interest Assessments) may help identify other risks. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "assess the data protection and privacy risks to individuals from the proposed collection, use, dissemination"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: DPP laws generally contain exceptions, exemptions, restrictions or limitations to privacy rights in certain situations, including prevention, investigation, detection or prosecution of criminal offences, which may apply only to specific offences such as fraud or terrorism. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "exceptions, exemptions, restrictions, or limitations to privacy rights in certain situations"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Even where such exceptions apply, as a general rule stakeholders should, to the extent applicable, be encouraged to bear in mind the DPP principles when designing private sector information sharing initiatives, which is likely to reduce DPP risks, particularly for entities active in multiple jurisdictions, and build greater public trust. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "stakeholders should, to the extent applicable, be encouraged to bear in mind the DPP principles"</sub>

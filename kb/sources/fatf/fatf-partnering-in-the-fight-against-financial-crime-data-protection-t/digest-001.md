## Executive Summary

- **descriptive / context**: The report states that countering ML, TF and proliferation financing (PF) and data protection and privacy (DPP) are both significant public interests serving important objectives, including upholding human rights and protecting the public from criminal activity. They are not in opposition nor inherently mutually exclusive, and an effective AML/CFT/CPF regime requires public and private sectors to pursue both sets of objectives. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These interests are not in opposition nor inherently mutually exclusive."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report aims to help jurisdictions considering enhancing information exchange among private sector entities to design and implement such initiatives responsibly and in accordance with DPP rules, so that risks of increased personal data sharing are appropriately taken into account. The FATF consulted data protection authorities, academics, technology providers and the private sector. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "design and implement such initiatives responsibly, in accordance with data protection and privacy rules"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A single financial institution has only a partial view of transactions, and criminals exploit this information gap by using multiple financial institutions within or across jurisdictions to layer illicit flows. By using collaborative analytics, bringing data together or developing other responsible sharing initiatives, financial institutions seek a clearer picture to better understand, assess and mitigate ML/TF risks. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A single financial institution has only a partial view of transactions"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The report states that collecting and using personal data for these purposes can trigger DPP concerns, and that misuse, unnecessary sharing or lack of protections may negatively affect individuals not engaged in malicious activity. Relevant data and systems must be managed and designed in accordance with applicable DPP rules. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Relevant data and systems must be managed and designed in accordance with applicable DPP rules."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Where the legal framework requires, it is important that initiatives are necessary, reasonable and proportionate in relation to the purposes of processing (AML/CFT/CPF), and designed and implemented responsibly and effectively. In general the risks of increased sharing of personal data need to be outweighed by the public benefits of combating financial crime. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these risks need to be outweighed by the public benefits of combating financial crime"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The experiences indicate AML/CFT/CPF private sector information sharing can be achieved in compliance with DPP rules and obligations, subject to key tests and requirements. Technology can play an enabling role in balancing policy objectives and reducing privacy risks, but adequate governance and legal frameworks are key to success; more quantitative data will be available to assess whether, when and how sharing enhances effectiveness as initiatives mature. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "adequate governance and legal frameworks are key to the success of these initiatives"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report draws on experiences from FATF members and its Global Network of increasing private sector information sharing within the legal requirements of their domestic data protection and privacy (DPP) frameworks. It says each initiative has to be assessed individually, based on its own characteristics and the DPP requirements that apply. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Each of these information sharing initiatives needs to be considered on a case-by-case basis"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: The report says the public sector should consider actively facilitating private sector information sharing initiatives. Examples it lists: updating laws or supervisory instruments where necessary; using regulatory sandboxes and pilot programmes; pointing out areas, typologies or data types that would gain from sharing; naming a lead agency or contact point to promote collaboration and co-ordination; providing guidance or checklists; building secure platforms for sharing and oversight; and running projects to harmonise and standardise data. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The public sector should consider taking an active facilitation role in private sector information sharing initiatives"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: This is a non-binding report; its recommendations to jurisdictions considering enhancing private sector information exchange reflect observations and lessons across the FATF global network. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This is a non-binding report."</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: The public sector should ensure and promote regular dialogue between DPP and AML/CFT authorities, consistent with FATF Recommendation 2, as well as internationally, for example through regular forums, a joint strategy, joint guidance or sector-wide engagement, assistance to industry initiatives, and joint initiatives such as regulatory sandboxes or technology sprints. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should ensure and promote regular dialogue between DPP and AML/CFT authorities, consistent with the FATF Recommendation 2"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: The private sector should consider applying privacy-enhancing technologies where fit for purpose; taking steps towards data preparation; pursuing data protection by design; establishing early and ongoing engagement with DPP authorities; developing indicators and metrics to measure success; and adopting measures to prevent de-risking related to information sharing. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The private sector should consider the application of privacy-enhancing technologies where they are fit for purpose"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 1: the right to privacy is enshrined in international instruments including the 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights, the 1950 Council of Europe Convention and the 1966 ICCPR; AML/CFT/CPF regimes help prevent criminal activity that violates fundamental human rights (e.g. human trafficking, corruption, terrorism) and contribute to sustainable development objectives (e.g. UN SDG target 16.4). (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AML/CFT/CPF regimes help prevent criminal activity that violates fundamental human rights"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 2 lists consulted stakeholders: data protection bodies (EDPB Financial Matters Expert Subgroup, Council of Europe, Global Privacy Assembly, OECD Working Party) and national DPAs (including Canada, Jersey, Luxembourg, Mexico INAI, Norway and the UK ICO); financial institutions and associations (Commerzbank, Lloyds, Santander, EBF, IIF, Wolfsberg Group); technology providers (e.g. Ant Group, Deloitte, FutureFlow, Duality, Elucidate, HAWK AI, Salv, TMNL); and academics and experts. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Wolfsberg Group and financial institutions involved in UK, Singapore and Estonian focus groups"</sub>

## SECTION ONE: Introduction

- **descriptive / context**: In July 2021 the FATF published a Stocktake on Data Pooling, Collaborative Analytics and Data Protection (the Stocktake Report), which recognised that privacy-enhancing and other technologies could support information sharing while protecting privacy and personal data. These technologies have not currently been adopted at scale and their application needs case-by-case consideration. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these technologies have not currently been adopted at scale and their application will need to be considered on a case-by-case basis"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Competent authorities and financial institutions need access to certain information about customers and their transactions to protect individuals from fraud, protect the public and markets, and accomplish AML/CFT/CPF objectives, including detecting, investigating and prosecuting or sanctioning persons for ML/TF. Feedback from FATF mutual evaluations suggests users of financial services are generally aware the private sector uses personal data under legal obligations (e.g. to verify identity). (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "users of financial services are generally aware that the private sector accesses and uses personal data"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Footnote 1 clarifies that this refers to broad trends in societal expectations and does not override the legal right in many jurisdictions (e.g. the EU) to be informed of any such use of personal data. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "does not override the legal right in many jurisdictions to be informed of any such use of their personal data"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FATF's July 2021 Stocktake Report on data pooling, collaborative analytics and data protection pointed to a need for clearer regulation, enabling environments, data standardisation and governance, and prevention of bias in artificial intelligence, so that AML/CFT/CPF information sharing can be more effective within a suitable DPP framework. The present report builds on this by sharing lessons from the Global Network on how some countries have tackled these issues. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "highlighted the need for greater regulatory clarity, promotion of enabling environments, data standardisation and governance"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A range of international conventions, treaties, laws and frameworks grant individuals data protection and/or privacy rights, which are the foundation for democracy and rule of law and essential to exercising other rights such as freedom of expression, association, religion and assembly. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "data protection and privacy rights are essential for the effective exercise of other human rights"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Where one institution might struggle to identify a complex suspicious transaction pattern or network, information from other institutions may complete the picture; conversely, additional information may help an institution determine that an initially unusual transaction was not suspicious. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "additional information may help an institution determine that a transaction that initially appeared unusual, was not suspicious"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Specific DPP requirements, including exceptions and exemptions, differ between jurisdictions; the report cites that in 2021, 145 jurisdictions had some type of data protection and privacy legislation (Greenleaf, 2021). It builds on the Phase 1 Stocktake and explores through case studies how both sets of objectives could be fulfilled. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In 2021, 145 jurisdictions have some type of legislation in place to ensure the protection of data and privacy."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The case studies are tailored to detecting suspicious activity: participating FIs would analyse certain personal customer data and related transactions for possible criminal activity and, after detection, the relevant FI follows its usual policies, including follow-up investigation, contacting the customer with questions and/or submitting a suspicious transaction report (STR). Many projects are at an early stage but designed to comply with DPP requirements while improving AML/CFT/CPF outcomes; information was collected through multi-stakeholder country-level focus groups. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "following up and investigating the situation, contacting the customer with questions, and/or submitting a suspicious transaction report (STR)"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The report states it is imperative that any exchange of personal data among private sector entities (such as customer data or personally identifiable transaction data) is limited to what is necessary, reasonable and proportionate, in line with the applicable legal frameworks. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is imperative that any such exchange of personal data"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The report says that where personal data is shared, DPP considerations and objectives should be built into the information sharing initiative. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Where personal data is shared, DPP considerations and objectives should be built into the sharing initiative."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Data sharing initiatives should be put in place case by case, through policies, regulations, procedures or other arrangements aimed at legitimate public interests in shielding society from criminal activity, including terrorism. The report says this policy calibration, often helped by enabling technology, should try to strike a balance between disrupting criminal activity and not undermining the policy objectives of national and international DPP laws and frameworks. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Data sharing initiatives should be implemented on a case- by-case basis through policies, regulations, procedures"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report states that detecting, investigating and prosecuting individuals for ML/TF while protecting individuals' data and privacy is not optional: both must be achieved to protect public safety, global markets and national security, democracy and the rule of law. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "while protecting individuals’ data and privacy is not optional"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: To develop private-to-private AML/CFT/CPF data sharing compliant with DPP laws, regulations and principles, the report states it is important that each project is developed case by case, consistent with applicable requirements and the particularities of the initiative, while potentially borrowing elements from the use cases; the report outlines what has and has not worked, to help detect, report and disrupt professional money laundering networks operating across entities and jurisdictions. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is important that each project is developed on a case-by-case basis"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: The report says it is worth exploring whether information sharing initiatives with appropriate safeguards could advance DPP objectives by making STRs more accurate, cutting false positives and the related investigations, or lowering the amount of personal data shared with authorities. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "improving the accuracy of STRs, reducing false positives and related investigations"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The UK Information Commissioner's Office (the UK DPP authority) said that a collaborative approach to fighting financial crime opens up the prospect of higher detection rates with fewer false positives, while lessening the burden of scrutiny on each individual and business consumer. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "opens up the prospect of higher detection rates with lower false positives"</sub>

## SECTION TWO: What are the global requirements on AML/CFT/CPF and to what extent does private-to-private information sharing contribute to their effective implementation?

- **descriptive / context**: The FATF Standards set the global requirements for AML/CFT/CPF systems, operationalising obligations under the 1988 UN Drug Convention, 2000 UN Convention against Transnational Organised Crime, 2003 UN Convention against Corruption and the 1999 Terrorist Financing Convention, and implementing UN Security Council Resolutions. Over 200 countries have committed to meeting them and undergo detailed evaluations. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Over 200 countries have committed to meeting the FATF Standards and undergo detailed evaluations"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The FATF Standards set out mandatory requirements that countries must impose on their private sector (through national law, regulations and other measures), collectively called 'preventative measures', which include collecting and retaining personal data (e.g. for identity verification). Specifically on information sharing, the Standards currently require private sector sharing in the context of correspondent banking, processing wire transfers, relying on third parties and implementing group-wide AML/CFT programmes, in some cases automated and in others manual. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF Standards set out a range of mandatory requirements that countries must impose on their private sector"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: There are no other mandatory FATF obligations for private-to-private information sharing in other circumstances, but jurisdictions may implement additional sharing initiatives to better deploy resources in a risk-based manner and develop innovative techniques to combat ML, TF and PF. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions may implement additional information sharing initiatives in order to better deploy resources in a risk-based manner"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report focuses on information sharing to support submission of suspicious transaction reports (STRs, sometimes called SARs or UTRs depending on national legislation) to the FIU; other relevant requirements are in Annex A. A footnote notes that paragraphs 77 to 79 of the Phase 1 Stocktake Report explain how STR confidentiality rules can create challenges for private-to-private sharing. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This report focuses on information sharing to support the submission of suspicious transaction reports"</sub>

## STR requirements

- **expected / requirement**: FATF Recommendation 20 (R.20) stipulates that if a financial institution suspects or has reasonable grounds to suspect that funds are proceeds of criminal activity or related to TF, it should be required to report its suspicions promptly to the country's FIU. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "if a financial institution suspects or has reasonable grounds to suspect that funds are the proceeds of a criminal activity"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Under R.20 the reporting requirement must be a direct, mandatory obligation; any indirect or implicit obligation to report suspicious transactions is not acceptable. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The reporting requirement must be a direct, mandatory obligation"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The private sector is also required to identify and verify customer identity and undertake ongoing monitoring of transactions/circumstances to ensure activities match what has been reported and to have a basis to determine whether transactions may be suspicious. It uses transaction monitoring systems, including common risk indicators (from FATF, government authorities or commercial providers), to identify potential suspicious activity across crime types, designed to ensure the financial sector is not used to finance crime, terrorism, or evade related financial sanctions. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The private sector is also required to identify and verify the identity of customers and undertake ongoing monitoring"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: As outlined in the Phase 1 Stocktake Report, information sharing (both private-to-private and private-to-public) is critical to fight ML/TF/PF, because multinational criminal schemes do not respect national boundaries and criminals do not exploit only one institution. The Phase 1 report sets out objectives and preconditions for private sector information sharing and analysis. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "information sharing (both private-to- private and private-to-public) is critical to fight ML/TF/PF"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The report states that the private sector must take a risk-based approach to mitigating the ML/TF/PF risks it faces. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The private sector must take a risk-based approach to mitigating the ML/TF/PF risks it faces."</sub>
- **permissive / context**: As an illustration of the risk-based approach, institutions may direct extra resources at the customers, services, products, locations and so on that they have identified as higher risk, in order to identify potentially suspicious activity. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "institutions may direct additional resources at those areas (customers, services, products, locations, etc.)"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The report states that, to comply with its requirements under R.20, the private sector must collect personally identifiable information and share it with the FIU. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In order to comply with its requirements under R.20, the private sector must collect and share personally identifiable information"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Existing FATF requirements cover information sharing between private sector entities in the context of correspondent banking (Recommendation 13), processing of wire transfers (Recommendation 16) and implementing AML/CFT measures within a financial group (Recommendation 18). (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "correspondent banking (Recommendation 13), processing of wire transfers (Recommendation 16)"</sub>

## Box 2.1. Potential use cases that could support the fight against ML, TF and PF

- **descriptive / context**: Box 2.1: per the Phase 1 Stocktake Report, private-to-private data sharing outside financial groups is perceived as restricted in many jurisdictions because of DPP requirements and/or jurisdiction-specific fundamental rights. Depending on national legislation and AML/CFT/CPF risk mitigation needs, FIs may wish to share data within and outside groups and across jurisdictions; listing a use case does not presume it meets DPP assessments, tests or thresholds. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Their inclusion does not presume that these cases meet or comply with the relevant assessments, tests, or thresholds under DPP rules."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Box 2.1 use case, customer identification/verification: to verify customer identity; to identify whether a natural or legal person has previously raised flags or concerns; and to verify customer risk ratings by checking for similar customer behaviour across business lines. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to identify if a natural or legal person has previously raised flags or concerns"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Box 2.1 use case, transaction monitoring: to detect layering by examining a customer's transaction pattern to assess the financial profile; to follow up abnormal activity within and across institutions; to better identify suspicious activity (or activity that is unusual but not suspicious); and to apply transaction thresholds. A footnote defines layering as occurring after illegal proceeds have been placed into the legitimate financial system, with funds further legitimised and distanced from criminal origin through additional transactions or instruments (layers). (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to detect layering11 by examining the transaction pattern of a customer"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Box 2.1 use case, sanctions or other screening: to screen customers and counterparties against UN and domestic sanctions lists (including TF and PF), and against PEP lists or other lists provided by commercial service providers. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to screen customers and counterparties in transactions against United Nations and domestic sanctions lists"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Box 2.1 use case, risk understanding and management of a business relationship: to update customer information on an ongoing basis; identify global risk exposure from onboarding the same customer across multiple institutions; and enable dynamic risk management reflecting new information or changes in customer behaviour. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identify global risk exposure as a result of on-boarding of the same customer across multiple institutions"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Box 2.1 use case, identification of the beneficial owner: to enhance accuracy of beneficial owner identification; identify the same beneficial owner across institutions; enhance detection of shell companies; or develop more efficient record-keeping of beneficial owner information. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to identify the same beneficial owner across institutions; to enhance the detection of shell companies"</sub>

## GENERAL GLOSSARY 186

- **descriptive / definition**: For Recommendations 6 and 7, the Glossary says the term third parties includes, but is not limited to, financial institutions and DNFBPs. (p. 189)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "includes, but is not limited to, financial institutions and DNFBPs"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The terms trust and trustee should be understood as in Article 2 of the Hague Convention on the law applicable to trusts and their recognition; trustees may be professional if paid to act in the course of business, or non-professional, such as a person acting without reward for family. (p. 189)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Trustees may be professional (e.g. depending on the jurisdiction, a lawyer or trust company)"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: A virtual asset is a digital representation of value that can be digitally traded or transferred and used for payment or investment purposes; it excludes digital representations of fiat currencies, securities and other financial assets already covered elsewhere in the FATF Recommendations. (p. 189)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A virtual asset is a digital representation of value that can be digitally traded, or transferred"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Article 2 of the Hague Convention describes a trust as legal relationships created inter-vivos or on death by a settlor when assets are placed under a trustee's control for a beneficiary or specified purpose, with characteristics that the assets are a separate fund, title stands in the trustee's name or another's on the trustee's behalf, and the trustee has the power and duty, for which he is accountable, to manage or dispose of the assets under the trust terms and legal duties. (p. 189)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the assets constitute a separate fund and are not a part of the trustee's own estate"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The Hague Convention text states that the settlor's reservation of certain rights and powers, and the trustee's own rights as a beneficiary, are not necessarily inconsistent with the existence of a trust. (p. 189)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "are not necessarily inconsistent with the existence of a trust"</sub>

## GENERAL GLOSSARY 187

- **descriptive / definition**: A virtual asset service provider is any natural or legal person not covered elsewhere in the Recommendations who as a business conducts for or on behalf of another person: exchange between virtual assets and fiat currencies, exchange between forms of virtual assets, transfer of virtual assets, safekeeping or administration of virtual assets or instruments enabling control over them, or participation in and provision of financial services related to an issuer's offer or sale of a virtual asset. (p. 190)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "safekeeping and/or administration of virtual assets or instruments enabling control over virtual assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: In the virtual asset context, transfer means conducting a transaction on behalf of another natural or legal person that moves a virtual asset from one virtual asset address or account to another. (p. 190)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "moves a virtual asset from one virtual asset address or account to another"</sub>
- **expected / definition**: In both cases, without delay should be interpreted in the context of the need to prevent flight or dissipation of funds or other assets linked to terrorists, terrorist organisations, terrorist financiers and WMD proliferation financing, and the need for global, concerted action to interdict and disrupt their flow swiftly. (p. 190)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "prevent the flight or dissipation of funds or other assets which are linked to terrorists"</sub>

## DNFBPS AND VASPS

- **expected / requirement**: All requirements for financial institutions, DNFBPs or VASPs should be introduced either (a) in law, as specifically required for Recommendations 10, 11 and 20, or (b) for all other cases, in law or enforceable means, at the country's discretion. (p. 191)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should be introduced either (a) in law (see the specific requirements in Recommendations 10, 11 and 20 in this regard)"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: In Recommendations 10, 11 and 20, law refers to legislation issued or approved through a Parliamentary process or equivalent under the constitutional framework, imposing mandatory requirements with sanctions for non-compliance that should be effective, proportionate and dissuasive (Recommendation 35); it also encompasses binding and authoritative judicial decisions imposing relevant requirements in all parts of the country. (p. 191)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "which imposes mandatory requirements with sanctions for noncompliance"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Enforceable means refers to regulations, guidelines, instructions or other documents or mechanisms setting out enforceable AML/CFT requirements in mandatory language with sanctions for non-compliance, issued or approved by a competent authority, with sanctions that should be effective, proportionate and dissuasive. (p. 191)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "set out enforceable AML/CFT requirements in mandatory language with sanctions for non-compliance"</sub>
- **expected / method**: In judging whether a document or mechanism amounts to enforceable means, there must be a document or mechanism setting out clearly stated requirements understood as such, addressing the issues in the FATF Recommendations; wording using shall or must should be considered mandatory. (p. 191)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "if particular measures use the word shall or must, this should be considered"</sub>
- **expected / method**: For enforceable means, the document or mechanism must be issued or approved by a competent authority. (p. 191)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The document/mechanism must be issued or approved by a competent authority."</sub>
- **expected / method**: For enforceable means, there must be sanctions for non-compliance, which need not be in the same document provided there are clear links between the requirement and the available sanctions, and which should be effective, proportionate and dissuasive, including an adequate range of such sanctions if persons fail to comply. (p. 191)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "sanctions need not be in the same document that imposes or underpins the requirement"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: When deciding whether a document or mechanism amounts to enforceable means, measures worded with 'should' could be mandatory if both the regulator and the regulated institutions demonstrate that the actions are directly or indirectly required and are being implemented. (p. 191)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this could be mandatory if both the regulator and the regulated"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Wording such as measures 'are encouraged', 'are recommended' or institutions 'should consider' is less likely to be regarded as mandatory; wherever weaker language is used, the presumption is that it is not mandatory unless the country can demonstrate otherwise. (p. 191)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In any case where weaker language is used, there is a presumption"</sub>
- **expected / method**: Sanctions should be directly or indirectly applicable for failure to comply with an AML/CFT requirement; where no sanction is attached directly, sanctions for violating broader requirements such as proper systems and controls or safe and sound operation are satisfactory provided that, at a minimum, failure to meet AML/CFT requirements could be (and has been as appropriate) sanctioned without proving additional unrelated prudential failures. (p. 192)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "without a need to prove additional prudential failures unrelated to AML/CFT"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: One of the issues to consider on sanctions for enforceable means is whether there is satisfactory evidence that effective, proportionate and dissuasive sanctions have been applied in practice. (p. 192)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "whether there is satisfactory evidence that effective, proportionate and"</sub>

(p. 193)[^original]

## EVALUATION REPORT TEMPLATE

- **expected / method**: The KRA Roadmap identifies each country's highest priority follow-up actions and is the basis for follow-up or ICRG process, so assessors should draft KRAs to be practical, achievable, measurable, precise and clear, without being overly prescriptive. (p. 193)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "practical, achievable, measurable, precise and clear, without being overly prescriptive"</sub>
- **expected / method**: The Executive Summary is the basis for Plenary discussion of each Mutual Evaluation and for conclusions for ministers and policymakers, and should not exceed five pages. (p. 193)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it does not exceed five pages"</sub>
- **expected / limitation**: A completed MER, excluding the KRA Roadmap, Executive Summary and TC Annex, is ideally 100 pages or less, with a TC annex of 60 pages or less; there is no predetermined length limit per chapter, but assessors should ensure the MER does not become excessively long. (p. 193)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is expected to be ideally 100 pages or less (together with a TC annex of 60 pages or less)"</sub>
- **expected / method**: Assessors should aim to summarise technical compliance with each Recommendation in one or two paragraphs totalling at most half a page, and may be very brief where there is little to report; a single sentence would be enough for Recommendations rated compliant. (p. 193)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "aim to summarise technical compliance with each Recommendation in one or two paragraphs"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Assessors are urged to include statistics and case studies where relevant, in the format shown at the end of the template. (p. 193)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Assessors are urged to include statistics and case studies where relevant."</sub>

## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

- **descriptive / method**: The Executive Summary is a separate document prepared after the face-to-face meeting (UPs, para.101), and summarises the AML/CFT measures in place as at the date of the on-site visit, analysing compliance with the FATF 40 Recommendations and effectiveness, with recommendations on strengthening the system. (p. 194)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Executive Summary is a separate document that is prepared after the face-to-face meeting (UPs, para.101)."</sub>
- **expected / method**: The Executive Summary Key Findings should be a short summary of the most important positive and negative findings given the country's risk profile; the first should be a very brief overview of the AML/CFT situation based on compliance and effectiveness. (p. 194)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The first key finding should be a very brief overview of the AML/CFT situation in the country"</sub>
- **expected / method**: Assessors should note progress since the last MER, highlighting significant changes and outstanding key issues, focusing on 5-7 points rather than summarising findings for every IO or chapter. (p. 194)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The focus should be on 5-7 points raised in the report"</sub>

## ANNEX I: MUTUAL EVALUATION REPORT TEMPLATE

- **descriptive / context**: The template links effectiveness outcomes to technical compliance: IO.1 with R.1 and R.2; IO.2 with R.36-R.40; R.33 as cross-cutting; IO.3 with financial sector and virtual asset supervision and preventive measures; IO.4 with R.22 and R.23; and IO.5 for transparency and beneficial ownership. (p. 195)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial sector and virtual asset supervision and preventive measures IO.3"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The template also links IO.6 (financial intelligence) with R.29, IO.7 (ML investigations and prosecutions) with R.3, IO.8 (asset recovery), IO.9 (TF investigations and prosecutions) with R.5, IO.10 (TF preventive measures and financial sanctions), and IO.11 (proliferation financing financial sanctions) with R.7. (p. 196)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Proliferation financing financial sanctions IO.11 R.7"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Effectiveness ratings are High (HE), Substantial (SE), Moderate (ME) or Low (LE); technical compliance ratings are C (compliant), LC (largely compliant), PC (partially compliant) or NC (non-compliant). (p. 196)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Technical compliance ratings can be either a C – compliant, LC – largely compliant, PC – partially compliant or NC – non-compliant."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The note says the table illustrates the main technical compliance findings specific to each immediate outcome even though findings such as R.1 or R.40 can be relevant across outcomes, and refers to paragraphs 53 and 54 of the 2022 Methodology for links between effectiveness and technical compliance ratings. (p. 196)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "See also paragraphs 53 and 54 of the FATF 2022 Methodology for links between effectiveness and technical compliance ratings."</sub>

## Risks and General Situation

- **expected / method**: The Executive Summary risks section should briefly (1-2 paragraphs) summarise the country's ML/TF risk situation and context, focusing on exposure to domestic and international risks and the issues and sectors presenting greatest risk, and should note areas where assessors identified material risks not considered in the country's own risk assessment or consider the risk level significantly different. (p. 197)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Assessors should note any areas where they have identified material risks which were not considered in the country’s own risk assessment"</sub>

## SYSTEMS

- **expected / method**: For each chapter of the main report, assessors should set out main findings, highlighting high-risk or significant contextual issues, areas of particularly good performance including unusual or innovative mechanisms, significant failures of effectiveness and important technical non-compliance, with a brief summary of conclusions on compliance and effectiveness, key findings for each IO and actions required. (p. 197)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "areas where the country performs particularly well both on effectiveness and technical compliance, highlighting unusual or innovative mechanisms"</sub>
- **expected / limitation**: The Executive Summary description should give enough detail for readers to understand conclusions but should not include full analysis, defend assessors' conclusions or anticipate and rebut objections; additional information belongs in the main report body. (p. 197)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should not defend assessors’ conclusions or anticipate and rebut objections"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Executive Summary template headings run by chapter: assessment of risk, coordination and policy setting (Chapter 1; IO.1, R.1, 2, 33 and 34); international co-operation (Chapter 2; IO.2; R.36-40); financial sector and virtual asset supervision and preventive measures (Chapter 3; IO.3, R.9-21, 26, 27, 34 and 35); non-financial sector (Chapter 4; IO.4, R.22, 23, 28, 34 and 35); and transparency and beneficial ownership (Chapter 5; IO.5, R.24 and 25). (p. 197)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial sector and virtual asset supervision and preventive measures (Chapter 3; IO.3, R.9-21, 26, 27, 34 & 35)"</sub>

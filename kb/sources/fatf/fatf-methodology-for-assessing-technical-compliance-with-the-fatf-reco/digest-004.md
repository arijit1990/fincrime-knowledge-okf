## LINKS TO TECHNICAL COMPLIANCE

- **mandatory / requirement**: Paragraph 52: assessors must assess all eleven Immediate Outcomes. Before the on-site visit they should conduct a scoping exercise in consultation with the assessed country, taking account of the risks and factors in paragraphs 5 to 16, identify higher risk issues to be examined in more detail and reflected in the final report, and seek to identify lower/low risk areas that may not need the same level of detail. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Assessors must assess all eleven of the Immediate Outcomes."</sub>
- **expected / method**: As the assessment continues, assessors should keep engaging the country and review their scoping based on initial effectiveness findings, to focus on areas with the greatest scope to improve effectiveness in addressing key ML/TF risks. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "review their scoping based on their initial findings about effectiveness"</sub>
- **expected / method**: Paragraph 53: the level of technical compliance contributes to the effectiveness assessment and should be considered in scoping. Technical compliance reviews whether legal and institutional foundations of an effective system are present; a country with a low level of technical compliance is unlikely to have an effective system, though a technically compliant country cannot be assumed effective, and in many cases the main reason for poor effectiveness will be serious deficiencies in implementing the technical elements. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is unlikely that a country that is assessed to have a low level of compliance with the technical aspects"</sub>
- **expected / method**: Paragraph 54: assessors should consider the impact of technical compliance when explaining why a country is or is not effective and in recommending improvements. In exceptional circumstances a low level of technical compliance may coexist with a certain level of effectiveness, for example due to low risks or other structural, materiality or contextual factors, particularities of the country's laws and institutions, or compensatory AML/CFT/CPF measures not required by the FATF Recommendations. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "low level of technical compliance but nevertheless a certain level of effectiveness"</sub>

## LINKS TO TECHNICAL COMPLIANCE

- **mandatory / requirement**: In such cases assessors should pay particular attention in the MER and must fully justify their decision, explaining in detail the basis and specific reasons for their effectiveness conclusions despite lower technical compliance. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "must fully justify their decision, explaining in detail the basis and the specific reasons for their conclusions on effectiveness"</sub>
- **expected / method**: Paragraph 55: an effectiveness assessment should consider each of the eleven Immediate Outcomes individually but does not directly focus on the Intermediate or High-Level Outcomes. For each Immediate Outcome there are two overarching questions: (a) to what extent is the outcome being achieved, and (b) what can be done to improve effectiveness. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there are two overarching questions which assessors should try to answer"</sub>
- **expected / method**: On question (a), assessors should assess whether the country is achieving the results expected of a well-performing system (see Characteristics of an Effective System), basing conclusions principally on the core issues, supported by examples of information and specific factors, and taking into account technical compliance, risk, materiality and contextual factors. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "They should base their conclusions principally on the core issues, supported by the examples of information"</sub>

## USING THE EFFECTIVENESS METHODOLOGY

- **expected / method**: On question (b), assessors should understand why a country may not have reached a high level of effectiveness and recommend how to improve, basing analysis on the Characteristics of an Effective System, core issues and examples of specific factors (activities, processes, resources, infrastructure), and considering the effect of technical deficiencies and contextual factors. Where an outcome is achieved to a high degree they need not consider in detail what can be done to improve, though they are free to identify good practices, potential improvements or efforts needed to sustain effectiveness. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they would not need to consider in detail what can be done to improve effectiveness"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Paragraph 56, Characteristics of an Effective System: the boxed text at the top of each Immediate Outcome describes the main features and outcomes of an effective system, setting out the situation in which a country is effective and providing the benchmark for assessment. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "provides the benchmark for the assessment"</sub>
- **expected / method**: Paragraph 57, core issues: these are the mandatory questions assessors should seek to answer to get an overview of how effective a country is under each outcome. Conclusions should be based on an overview of each outcome, informed by the core issues assessment and taking into account the Characteristics of an Effective System. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The core issues are the mandatory questions which assessors should seek to answer"</sub>
- **expected / method**: Paragraph 58: assessors should examine all core issues listed for each outcome but may vary the degree of detail to reflect the risk and materiality associated with each issue in the country. In exceptional circumstances they may consider additional issues they regard as core to the outcome (for example alternative measures reflecting specificities of the country's system), and should make clear when and why any additional issues were used. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Assessors should examine all the core issues listed for each outcome."</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Paragraph 59: the Examples of Information set out the types and sources of information most relevant to understanding the extent to which an outcome is achieved, including data points assessors might look for. Supporting information and data can test or validate assessors' understanding of the core issues and provide a quantitative element. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The supporting information and other data can test or validate"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 60: the supporting information and data listed are neither exhaustive nor mandatory; available data and statistics vary considerably between countries, and assessors should use whatever information the country can provide to reach their judgement. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The supporting information and data listed are not exhaustive and not mandatory."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 61: assessment of effectiveness is not a statistical exercise. Assessors should use data, statistics and qualitative information to reach an informed judgement but interpret data critically in the country's circumstances; the focus should not be on raw data, which can be interpreted in many ways and even with contradictory conclusions, but on information and analysis indicating whether the objective is achieved. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Assessment of effectiveness is not a statistical exercise."</sub>
- **expected / limitation**: Assessors should be particularly cautious about using data from other countries as a comparison point for judging effectiveness, given significant differences in country circumstances, AML/CFT/CPF systems and data collection practices, and should be aware that a high level of outputs does not always contribute positively towards achieving the desired outcome. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a high level of outputs does not always contribute positively towards achieving the desired outcome"</sub>

## Examples of specific factors that could support the conclusions on core issues

- **descriptive / method**: Paragraph 62: the factors section gives examples of elements normally involved in delivering each outcome, as a non-exhaustive aid for assessors considering why a country may or may not achieve an outcome (for example a breakdown in one factor). In most cases assessors will need to refer to the factors to reach a firm conclusion on the extent an outcome is achieved. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These are not an exhaustive list of the possible factors"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The activities and processes listed do not imply a single mandatory model for organising AML/CFT/CPF functions, only the most commonly implemented administrative arrangements; reasons for ineffectiveness are not limited to the factors listed; and assessors need to focus on the qualitative aspects of the factors, not the mere underlying process or procedure. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "do not imply a single mandatory model for organising AML/CFT/CPF functions"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Paragraph 63: assessors do not have to review every one of the listed factors in every case. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Assessors are not required to review all the factors in every case."</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Where a country is demonstrably not effective and the reasons are fundamental (for example major technical deficiencies), there is no need for assessors to undertake further detailed examination of why the outcome is not being achieved. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is also no need for assessors to undertake further detailed examination of why the outcome is not being achieved"</sub>
- **expected / method**: Paragraph 64: assessors should be aware of outcomes depending on a sequence of steps or a value chain (for example Immediate Outcome 7: investigation, prosecution and sanctioning, in order); an outcome may not be achieved because of failure at one stage even though other stages are effective. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an outcome may not be achieved because of a failure at one stage of the process"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 65: assessors should consider contextual factors, which may influence which issues are material or higher risk and where attention is focused, and may be an important part of explaining performance and of recommendations, but should not be an excuse for poor or uneven implementation of the FATF Standards. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they should not be an excuse for poor or uneven implementation of the FATF Standards"</sub>

## CROSS-CUTTING ISSUES

- **expected / method**: Paragraph 66: Immediate Outcomes are not independent, and factors assessed under Immediate Outcomes 1 (risk assessment and risk-based approach implementation) and 2 (international co-operation) may have far-reaching effects, for example risk assessment affects risk-based measures under IOs 3 and 4 and resource deployment across outcomes, and international co-operation supports domestic ML investigations and confiscation under IOs 7 and 8. Assessors should consider how IO 1 and 2 findings positively or negatively affect other IOs; these cross-cutting issues appear in each IO's Note to Assessors. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "how their findings for Immediate Outcomes 1 and 2 may have a positive or negative impact"</sub>
- **expected / method**: Paragraph 67: where possible assessors should avoid duplication by presenting analysis of an issue once in the most relevant MER section and cross-referencing it elsewhere. In determining ratings the issue should get most weight in the IO where it is most relevant, and may be considered, with less weight, in rating other IOs. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should give the issue the most weight when rating the IO where they consider the issue is most relevant"</sub>

## CONCLUSIONS ON EFFECTIVENESS

- **expected / requirement**: Paragraph 68: for each Immediate Outcome assessors should reach conclusions on the extent to which a country is effective; where a high level of effectiveness is not reached they should also recommend the reasons and the measures the country should take to improve. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "assessors should reach conclusions about the extent to which a country is (or is not) effective"</sub>
- **expected / method**: Paragraph 69: effectiveness is assessed differently from technical compliance, on an overall understanding of the degree to which the outcome is achieved. Core issues should not be treated as a checklist of criteria but as questions aiding understanding; they are not equally important and their significance varies with each country's ML/TF risks and structural factors, so assessors need to be flexible and use judgement and experience. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The core issues should not be considered as a checklist of criteria"</sub>
- **expected / method**: Paragraph 69: assessors' conclusions for each Immediate Outcome should clearly explain how much weight was given to each core issue, based on the country's risk, context and materiality and on the nature of that core issue. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "clearly explain the weight given to each core issue based on the country’s risk, context and materiality"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 70: conclusions should reflect only the degree to which the outcome is achieved; assessors should not be unduly influenced by their own national approach, and should avoid basing conclusions on the number of problems or deficiencies, since a country may have several weaknesses that are not material or are offset by strengths and still achieve a high overall level of effectiveness. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "avoid basing their conclusions on the number of problems or deficiencies identified"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 71: effectiveness conclusions should be primarily descriptive, setting out the extent to which the outcome is achieved overall, noting variation (areas of higher or lower effectiveness), and explaining the basis of the judgement (responsible deficiencies, most significant core issues and information, how data and indicators were understood, and weight given to aspects). Assessors should also identify particular strengths or good practice. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "conclusions on the level of effectiveness should be primarily descriptive"</sub>
- **expected / method**: Paragraph 72: to ensure clear and comparable decisions, assessors should summarise each Immediate Outcome conclusion as one of four effectiveness ratings, based on the extent to which core issues and characteristics are addressed: High, Substantial, Moderate or Low level of effectiveness. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "High level of effectiveness; Substantial level of effectiveness; Moderate level of effectiveness; and Low level of effectiveness"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Effectiveness ratings: High level means the Immediate Outcome is achieved to a very large extent and minor improvements are needed; Substantial means achieved to a large extent with moderate improvements needed; Moderate means achieved to some extent with major improvements needed; Low means not achieved or achieved to a negligible extent with fundamental improvements needed. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Immediate Outcome is achieved to a very large extent. Minor improvements needed."</sub>

## RECOMMENDATIONS ON HOW TO IMPROVE THE AML/CFT/CPF SYSTEM

- **expected / requirement**: Paragraph 73: assessors' recommendations are a vitally important part of the evaluation and should propose measures to improve the system, covering both effectiveness and technical compliance. Assessors should determine whether recommendations are key and, if so, note them separately as Key Recommended Actions (KRAs); there should not normally be more than 2-3 KRAs per Immediate Outcome, including any KRA on a related Recommendation, and in exceptional cases a limited number of KRAs on contextual factors. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There should not normally be more than 2-3 KRAs per Immediate Outcome"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The report should prioritise recommendations for remedial measures taking account of risks, context, level of effectiveness and weaknesses identified. Recommendations should not simply address each deficiency but add value by identifying and prioritising specific, targeted measures to mitigate risks most effectively, for example because they offer the greatest and most rapid practical improvements, have the widest-reaching effects, or are easiest to achieve. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should add value by identifying and prioritising specific and targeted measures"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 18: KRAs should only relate to IOs rated ME or LE, or Recommendations rated PC or NC that relate to any IO rated ME or LE; normally no more than two to three KRAs per IO, including technical compliance KRAs for Recommendations relating primarily to that IO; and there may be one KRA for each of R.3, R.5, R.6, R.10, R.11 and R.20 rated NC or PC where these do not pertain to any IO rated ME or LE. Footnote 19: KRAs on contextual factors should be linked to an explanation in the MER of the grounds and intended impact. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "one KRA for each of R.3, R.5, R.6, R.10, R.11 and R.20 that is rated NC or PC"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 74: assessors should consider the country's circumstances, context and legal and institutional system when making recommendations, noting there are several ways to achieve an effective system and their own preferred model may not be appropriate, and should consider structural or contextual factors affecting compliance or effectiveness (see paragraph 16). (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "their own preferred model may not be appropriate in the context of the country assessed"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 75: assessors should work with the country to identify needed measures so meaningful recommendations can be made. Recommendations, particularly KRAs, should be practical, achievable, precise and clear without being overly prescriptive, measurable and time-bound so progress can be benchmarked, and outcome oriented and targeted to increase effectiveness. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should be measurable and time-bound, so that the progress achieved can be benchmarked"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 76: to help the country develop an action plan, assessors should clearly indicate where a specific action is required and where there is flexibility in how a priority objective is achieved, and avoid unnecessarily rigid or overly detailed recommendations (for example on scheduling of measures or prosecution of specific persons), so countries can adapt recommendations to local circumstances. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Assessors should avoid making unnecessarily rigid or overly detailed recommendations"</sub>

## SECTION II – GUIDANCE FOR SUPERVISORS

- **descriptive / context**: In supervision, the RBA applies to how supervisory authorities allocate resources and to supervisors carrying out their functions in a way that supports banks' application of an RBA. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this applies to the way supervisory authorities allocate their resources"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Recommendation 26 requires countries to subject banks to adequate AML/CFT regulation and supervision; INR 26 requires supervisors to allocate resources to areas of higher ML/TF risk, based on understanding country risk and having onsite and off-site access to all information relevant to a bank's risk profile. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "INR 26 requires supervisors to allocate supervisory resources to areas of higher ML/TF risk"</sub>

## Report by the European Supervisory Authorities

- **descriptive / context**: In October 2013 the European Supervisory Authorities (EIOPA, EBA, ESMA) published a Preliminary report on AML/CFT risk-based supervision, setting out what RBA supervision entails and listing self-assessment questions for supervisors. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In October 2013, the European Supervisory Authorities"</sub>

## BCBS Guidelines

- **descriptive / context**: In January 2014 the BCBS published 'Sound management of risks related to money laundering and financing of terrorism', describing how banks should include ML/TF risk management in their overall risk management; it supports but does not modify (strengthen or weaken) the FATF standards, and contains more detailed RBA guidance for supervisors. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In no way should they be interpreted as modifying the FATF standards"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should understand the ML/TF risks of the banking sector and of individual banks and banking groups (consistent with BCP 8), drawing on a variety of sources. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisors should draw on a variety of sources to identify and assess ML/TF risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Sources for sectoral ML/TF risk include national risk assessments, domestic or international typologies, supervisory expertise and FIU feedback. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "domestic or international typologies and supervisory expertise, as well as Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU) feedback"</sub>

## UNDERSTANDING ML/TF RISK

- **expected / data or feature**: For individual banks, supervisors should consider inherent risk factors: nature and complexity of products and services, size, business model, corporate governance, financial and accounting information, delivery channels, customer profiles, geographic location and countries of operation. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the nature and complexity of the bank’s products and services, their size, business model"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should also assess a bank's controls, including the quality of the risk management policy and functioning of internal oversight functions. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the quality of the risk management policy, the functioning of the internal oversight functions"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: Some risk information comes from prudential supervision; AML/CFT-relevant information such as fit and properness of management and the compliance function (BCP 5) requires information-sharing between prudential and AML/CFT supervisors, especially when they are separate agencies. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "information-sharing and collaboration between prudential and AML/CFT supervisors"</sub>
- **permissive / data or feature**: Information from other supervisors, the FIU and law enforcement may help determine how well a bank manages its ML/TF risk. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "other supervisors, the FIU and law enforcement agencies may also be helpful"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should review their sector and bank ML/TF risk assessments periodically and whenever a bank's circumstances change or relevant new threats emerge. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "review their assessment of both the sector’s and banks’ ML/TF risk profile periodically"</sub>

## MITIGATING ML/TF RISK

- **mandatory / requirement**: The FATF Recommendations (in line with BCP 9) require supervisors to allocate more resources to higher ML/TF risk areas, setting frequency and intensity of assessments by risk level and prioritising higher risk areas where detailed supervision of all banks is not feasible. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "require supervisors to allocate more supervisory resources to areas of higher ML/TF risk"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should adjust AML/CFT supervision frequency to risk, combining periodic reviews with ad hoc supervision triggered e.g. by whistleblowing, law enforcement information, prudential findings or thematic review samples. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "as a result of whistleblowing, information from law enforcement, or other supervisory findings"</sub>
- **expected / method**: Supervisors should set the scope or level of assessment in line with risk; more intensive supervision may include detailed testing of systems and files on risk assessment, CDD, reporting and record keeping, internal audit, interviews with staff, senior management and the Board, and line-of-business AML/CFT assessment. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "detailed testing of systems and files to verify the implementation and adequacy of the bank’s risk assessment"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should use findings to update ML/TF risk assessments, reconsider whether their supervisory approach, rules and guidance remain adequate, and where appropriate and confidentiality allows, communicate findings to banks. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisors should use their findings to review and update their ML/TF risk assessments"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: Supervisors can adjust the level of information they require in their authorisation checks, which aim to prevent criminals or their associates from holding a significant or controlling interest in a bank: where sector ML/TF risk is low they may decide to base approvals on a review of documentation, and where it is high they may ask for additional information. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to prevent criminals or their associates from holding a significant or controlling interest in a bank"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should always have both on-site and off-site access to all relevant risk and compliance information. To the extent permitted by their regime, supervisors can determine the correct mix of on-site and off-site supervision of banks, but off-site supervision alone may not be appropriate in higher risk situations. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to the extent permitted by their regime, supervisors can determine the correct mix of on-site and off-site supervision of banks"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Under Recommendation 26 and relevant Basel Core Principles (BCBS Principles 1-3, 5-9, 11-15, 26 and 29), banking supervisors should use other prudential supervision results in AML/CFT supervision and ensure broader prudential findings are informed by AML/CFT findings. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Principles 1-3, 5-9, 11-15, 26, and 29"</sub>

## GENERAL APPROACH

- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should ensure their staff can assess whether a bank's policies, procedures and controls are appropriate to its risk assessment and risk appetite, that the bank adheres to its own policies, and that decisions use sound judgment. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisors should make sure that the bank adheres to its own policies, procedures and controls"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should clearly articulate and communicate expectations so supervisory actions are mostly predictable, consistent and proportionate, with training and communication being key. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supervisory actions are in most cases predictable, consistent and proportionate"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: Supervisors could compare banks' AML/CFT programmes to inform judgments on individual control quality, but should recognise valid reasons for differences under the RBA, especially across differing operational complexity. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "carrying out comparisons between banks’ AML/CFT programmes could be considered"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should thoroughly understand higher and lower risk lines of business and engage in dialogue with individual banks about their AML/CFT controls. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "have a thorough understanding of higher and lower risk lines of business"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The general supervisory principles apply to international banking groups, though application is more complex given multiple jurisdictions' legal frameworks, risks and supervisors. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "also apply to international banking groups"</sub>

## TRAINING

- **expected / requirement**: INR 26 provides that supervisory staff should understand the discretion a bank has in assessing and mitigating ML/TF risks, and supervisors should check staff are trained to assess the quality of bank risk assessments and the adequacy, proportionality and effectiveness of AML/CFT policies and controls. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should understand the degree of discretion a bank has in assessing and mitigating its ML/TF risks"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Supervisory training should enable sound judgments on adequacy and proportionality of controls and achieve national consistency where there are multiple supervisory authorities. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "achieving consistency in the supervisory approach conducted at national level"</sub>

## GUIDANCE

- **expected / expectation**: Supervisors should communicate expectations of bank compliance (R. 34), after considering consultation, through outcome-based requirements, risk-based rules, interpretive information or detailed control guidance, and should consider guidance that fosters financial inclusion. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "in a way that fosters financial inclusion"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Industry guidance may be useful where supervisory guidance is high-level, but banks should ensure private sector guidance they use is consistent with national legislation, international standards and authority guidelines. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the private sector guidance they take into consideration should be consistent with national legislation"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Supervisors should consider liaising with other relevant domestic regulatory and supervisory authorities to secure a coherent interpretation of legal obligations and minimise disparities; multiple guidance should not create opportunities for regulatory arbitrage, loopholes or unnecessary confusion among banks, and authorities should consider preparing joint guidance when possible. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Multiple guidance should not create opportunities for regulatory arbitrage, loopholes or unnecessary confusion among banks"</sub>

## SECTION III – GUIDANCE FOR BANKS

- **descriptive / context**: For banks, the RBA applies to allocation of compliance resources, organisation of internal controls and structures, and policies to deter and detect ML/TF, including at group level. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the way banks allocate their compliance resources, organise their internal controls"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Banking lines with different ML/TF risks include retail banking, corporate and investment banking, investment services or wealth management (sometimes called private banking), and correspondent services provided by a correspondent bank to a respondent bank; banks should be mindful of these differences. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "banking services are provided by one bank (the “correspondent bank”) to another bank"</sub>

## A. RISK ASSESSMENT

- **expected / expectation**: A bank's risk assessment is the basis of its RBA, should show how and how far it is vulnerable to ML/TF, often yields a stylised risk categorisation, and should always be properly documented, maintained and communicated to relevant personnel. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It should always be properly documented, maintained and communicated to relevant personnel"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: A bank's risk assessment should be commensurate with its nature and size: simple for smaller or less complex banks, more sophisticated where products are complex, there are multiple subsidiaries or branches, or the customer base is diverse. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A bank’s risk assessment need not be complex, but should be commensurate"</sub>
- **expected / data or feature**: Factors banks should consider in assessing ML/TF risk include nature, scale, diversity and complexity of business; target markets; number of customers already identified as high risk; and transaction volume and size relative to usual activity and customer profiles. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The number of customers already identified as high risk"</sub>
- **expected / data or feature**: Banks should consider jurisdictional exposure through own or customer activities, especially to jurisdictions with higher corruption or organised crime, deficient AML/CFT controls, and listed by FATF. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions with relatively higher levels of corruption or organised crime"</sub>
- **expected / data or feature**: Banks should consider distribution channels, including how far they deal directly with customers, rely on third parties for CDD, and use technology, as well as internal audit and regulatory findings. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "relies (or is allowed to rely on) third parties to conduct CDD and the use of technology"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Banks should supplement their risk assessment with internal and external sources such as heads of business, relationship managers, national risk assessments, international and government lists, mutual evaluation reports and typologies, and review it periodically and when circumstances change or new threats emerge. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "heads of business, relationship managers, national risk assessments"</sub>

## Box 2. Examples of ML/TF risk associated with different banking activities

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Retail banking ML/TF risks include services to cash-intensive businesses, transaction volume, high-value transactions and diversity of services. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "provision of services to cash-intensive businesses, volume of transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Wealth management ML/TF risks include a culture of confidentiality, difficulty identifying beneficial owners, concealment via offshore trusts, banking secrecy, product complexity, PEPs, high value transactions and multiple jurisdictions. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "culture of confidentiality, difficulty to identify beneficial owners, concealment (use of offshore trusts)"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Investment banking ML/TF risks include layering and integration, transfer of assets between parties for cash or other assets, and the global nature of markets. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "layering and integration, transfer of assets between parties in exchange for cash"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Correspondent banking ML/TF risks include high value transactions, limited information on the remitter and source of funds especially with banks in jurisdictions not or insufficiently complying with FATF Recommendations, and possible PEP involvement in bank ownership. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "limited information about the remitter and source of funds"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The categorisation of banking activities is purely indicative and the list of risks illustrative and non-exhaustive. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the list of identified risks is illustrative and non-exhaustive"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The bank risk assessment should be approved by senior management, underpin ML/TF mitigation policies reflecting risk appetite and the acceptable risk level, be regularly reviewed and updated, and mitigation controls should be consistent with it. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The risk assessment should be approved by senior management"</sub>

## IDENTIFICATION, VERIFICATION AND THE PURPOSE AND INTENDED NATURE OF THE BUSINESS RELATIONSHIP

- **expected / expectation**: CDD processes should help banks understand who customers are, what they do and why they need banking services; initial CDD should help assess relationship risk, determine CDD level and deter persons seeking relationships for illicit activity. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "deter persons from establishing a business relationship to conduct illicit activity"</sub>
- **expected / method**: Banks should prepare customer risk profiles from a holistic view of CDD information, determining the level and type of ongoing monitoring and supporting decisions to enter, continue or terminate relationships; profiles may apply to groups of customers with homogenous characteristics, particularly in retail banking. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "where groups of customers display homogenous characteristics"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Initial CDD comprises identifying the customer and any beneficial owner, verifying identity using reliable and independent information at least to the extent legally required, and understanding the purpose and intended nature of the relationship, obtaining further information in higher risk situations. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Verifying the customer’s identity on the basis of reliable and independent information"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Banks should comply with national and international sanctions legislation by screening customer and beneficial owner names against UN and other relevant sanctions lists. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "screening the customer’s and beneficial owner’s names against the UN and other relevant sanctions lists"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: As a general rule, CDD measures have to apply in all cases, but their extent may be adjusted, to the extent permitted or required by regulatory requirements, in line with the ML/TF risk of the individual business relationship; the amount and type of information obtained and the extent of its verification must be increased where risk is higher, and may be simplified where risk is lower. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to the extent permitted or required by regulatory requirements, in line with the ML/TF risk"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Banks have to draw up, and periodically update, customer risk profiles, which help them apply the appropriate level of CDD. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Banks therefore have to draw up, and periodically update, customer risk profiles"</sub>

## Box 3. Examples of Enhanced Due Diligence/Simplified Due Diligence measures (see also INR 10)

- **descriptive / method**: EDD examples: obtaining additional identifying information from wider or more robust sources, additional searches such as verifiable adverse media, commissioning an intelligence report on customer or beneficial owner, verifying source of funds or wealth, and seeking more information on relationship purpose. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "commissioning an intelligence report on the customer or beneficial owner"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: SDD examples: obtaining less information (e.g., no address or occupation), less robust verification of identity and relationship purpose, and postponing identity verification. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "not requiring information on the address or the occupation of the potential client"</sub>

## Box 4. CDD and financial inclusion considerations

- **expected / expectation**: A risk-based approach to CDD can support financial inclusion by flexible CDD for some products or customers, but financial exclusion is not itself an indicator of low risk and banks must decide on exemptions or SDD from a holistic risk assessment. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "financial exclusion in itself is not an indicator of low ML/TF risk"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Under Recommendation 10, where banks cannot apply the appropriate level of CDD, they must not enter into, or must terminate, the business relationship. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Recommendation 10 requires that banks do not enter into the business relationship"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The BCBS Sound management guidance gives detailed guidance on ML risk in correspondent banking and where banks rely on third parties for initial CDD. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "risk in correspondent banking and in situations where banks rely on third parties"</sub>

## ONGOING CDD/MONITORING

- **descriptive / definition**: Ongoing monitoring means scrutinising transactions for consistency with the bank's knowledge of the customer and the product and relationship, and identifying and updating changes to customer profile, which may require new CDD; it is essential for identifying potentially suspicious transactions. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Ongoing monitoring means the scrutiny of transactions to determine whether those transactions are consistent"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Monitoring may be continuous or triggered by specific transactions and can compare a customer's activity with a peer group. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It could also be used to compare a customer’s activity with that of a peer group"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Monitoring need not be electronic, though automated systems may be the only realistic option for high-volume activity; where used, banks should understand their operating rules, regularly verify their integrity and check they address identified risks. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "banks should understand their operating rules, verify their integrity on a regular basis"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Banks should adjust monitoring extent and depth to institutional risk assessment and customer risk profiles, with enhanced monitoring for higher risk and possible reduced frequency and intensity for lower risk, and regularly review monitoring adequacy. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Enhanced monitoring should be required for higher risk situations"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Banks should document clearly the criteria and parameters for customer segmentation and risk-level allocation per customer cluster, and make monitoring frequency and intensity criteria transparent. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "document and state clearly the criteria and parameters used for customer segmentation"</sub>

## Box 5. Examples of monitoring in high/lower risk situations

- **descriptive / method**: High risk monitoring examples: daily and manual transaction monitoring, frequent analysis, considering destination of funds, red flags based on typologies reports, and reporting results to senior management; lower risk: thresholds, low frequency, automated systems. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "daily transaction monitoring, manual transaction monitoring, frequent analysis of information"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Per BCBS guidance (Section II 1 (d)), a bank's monitoring system should be adequate for its size, activities, complexity and risks; for most banks, especially internationally active ones, effective monitoring likely requires automation. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "effective monitoring is likely to necessitate the automation of the monitoring process"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Banks should document, retain and communicate to relevant personnel monitoring results and queries raised and resolved. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the results of their monitoring as well as any queries raised and resolved"</sub>

## REPORTING

- **mandatory / requirement**: Recommendation 20 requires countries to mandate that a bank suspecting, or having reasonable grounds to suspect, that funds are criminal proceeds or TF-related report promptly to the FIU. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it shall report its suspicions promptly to the relevant FIU"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Banks should be able to flag unusual movements of funds or transactions and have case management systems ensuring timely scrutiny and determination of suspicion. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Banks should have appropriate case management systems"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Suspicious funds or transactions should be reported promptly to the FIU in the manner set by authorities; processes leading to suspicion may be risk-sensitive, but once ML/TF suspicion forms the bank should report. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a bank should report once ML/TF suspicion has formed"</sub>

## INTERNAL CONTROLS

- **expected / expectation**: Adequate internal controls are a prerequisite for mitigating ML/TF risk, including governance with clearly allocated AML/CFT responsibility, staff integrity controls, and compliance controls testing overall effectiveness of risk policies. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Adequate internal controls are a prerequisite for the effective implementation of policies"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Larger banking groups should have controls ensuring a consistent group-wide AML/CFT approach; BCBS guidance covers consolidated risk management, home/host differences and intra-group information sharing to strengthen group-wide risk assessment. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there should be controls in place for a consistent approach to AML/CFT controls across the group"</sub>

## GOVERNANCE

- **descriptive / case example**: Examples of senior management promoting compliance include product development and commercial campaigns in strict compliance with national AML/CFT law and senior management involvement in staff AML/CFT training. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "To involve senior management in AML/CFT training of staff."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF paragraph 75 states that successful implementation and effective operation of a risk-based approach to AML/CFT depends on strong senior management leadership and oversight of how the approach is developed and implemented across the bank. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "depends on strong senior management leadership and oversight of the development and implementation of the RBA"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Among the ways senior management should consider to support AML/CFT initiatives (paragraph 76) is implementing adequate internal communication mechanisms on actual or potential ML/TF risks; these mechanisms should link the board, the AML/CFT chief officer, any relevant or specialised committee (e.g., the risks or ethics/compliance committee), the IT division and each business area. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "link the board of directors, the AML/CFT chief officer, any relevant or specialised committee within the bank"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Among the ways senior management should consider to support AML/CFT initiatives (paragraph 76) is deciding on the measures needed to mitigate the identified ML/TF risks and on how much residual risk the bank is prepared to accept. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the extent of residual risk the bank is prepared to accept"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Among the ways senior management should consider to support AML/CFT initiatives (paragraph 76) is adequately resourcing the bank's AML/CFT unit. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "adequately resource the bank’s AML/CFT unit"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: FATF paragraph 77 says that this would require senior management to receive sufficient, regular and objective information giving an accurate picture of the ML/TF risk to which the bank is exposed through its activities and individual business relationships. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "receives sufficient, regular and objective information to get an accurate picture of the ML/TF risk"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: FATF paragraph 77 says senior management would also need sufficient and objective information (without a stated regularity) to understand whether the bank's AML/CFT controls are effective, for example information from the Chief Compliance Officer on control effectiveness or audit reports; and processes should be in place to escalate important decisions that directly affect the bank's ability to address and control risks. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "receives sufficient and objective information to understand whether the bank’s AML/CFT controls are effective"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Responsibility for consistency and effectiveness of AML/CFT controls should be clearly allocated to a sufficiently senior individual, including appointment of a skilled compliance officer at management level (INR 18). (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "clearly allocated to an individual of sufficient seniority within the bank"</sub>

## ENSURING AND MONITORING COMPLIANCE

- **expected / expectation**: A bank's internal control environment should assure staff integrity, competence and compliance, and AML/CFT controls should be consistent with broader controls for business, financial and operating risks. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "conducive to assuring the integrity, competence and compliance of staff"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Banks should check staff have integrity and adequate skills, knowledge and expertise, especially those implementing AML/CFT controls. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Banks should check that staff they employ have integrity and are adequately skilled"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Staff vetting should reflect the ML/TF risks individual staff are exposed to, not just senior roles; conflicts of interest for AML/CFT staff should be managed and their remuneration aligned with BCBS principles on compliance function independence. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "not focus merely on senior management roles"</sub>

## TRAINING AND AWARENESS

- **expected / expectation**: Bank AML/CFT training should be high quality, relevant to the bank's risks and current legal obligations, obligatory for all relevant staff, and tailored to particular lines of business. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Obligatory for all relevant staff"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: In line with INR 18, AML/CFT training of bank staff should be ongoing: regular and relevant, and not a one-off exercise at the time staff are hired; it should also be complemented by AML/CFT information and updates disseminated to relevant staff as appropriate. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "not be a one-off exercise when staff are hired"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: FATF paragraph 83 says that, overall, training should also seek to build up a working behaviour in which compliance is embedded in the activities and decisions of all of the bank's staff. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "build up a working behaviour where compliance is embedded in the activities and decisions"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: AML/CFT training should be effective, checked e.g. by staff passing tests or monitoring compliance levels, with measures where staff lack expected knowledge. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "requiring staff to pass tests or by monitoring levels of compliance"</sub>

## ASSESSMENT OF CONTROLS

- **expected / expectation**: Banks should be satisfied AML/CFT controls are adhered to and effective, with ongoing monitoring by the compliance officer and review by an audit function. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "their controls should be monitored on an ongoing basis by the bank’s compliance officer"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The compliance officer should have independence, authority, seniority, resources and expertise, including access to all relevant internal information across business lines, foreign branches and subsidiaries. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the necessary independence, authority, seniority, resources and expertise"</sub>

## Box 7. Examples of internal controls to encourage compliance

- **descriptive / method**: Internal controls to facilitate suspicious transaction reporting include staff training on detecting unusual transactions, adequate channels to report to the Compliance Officer, and confidentiality for reporting staff. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Ensure confidentiality to staff reporting suspicious transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Other control examples include letting staff report unclear or ineffective policies, ongoing consultation channels with consistent answers, and conducting AML/CFT so staff see it as supporting service quality and bank integrity. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Establish ongoing consultation channels for staff concerning AML/CFT issues"</sub>

## ASSESSMENT OF CONTROLS

- **mandatory / requirement**: Recommendation 18 requires countries to require banks to appoint a compliance officer at management level. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Recommendation 18 requires countries to require banks to appoint a compliance officer at management level"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Beyond advising relevant staff how to meet their obligations, the role of the bank's compliance officer should be to monitor and assess ML/TF risks across the bank and the adequacy and effectiveness of the measures the bank has put in place to mitigate those risks (FATF paragraph 85). (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "their role should be to monitor and assess ML/TF risks across the bank"</sub>

## Box 7. Examples of internal controls to encourage compliance

- **expected / expectation**: The findings of the independent audit function should inform senior management's view of the design and implementation of the bank's AML/CFT framework (FATF paragraph 86). (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The findings should inform senior management’s view of the design and implementation"</sub>
- **permissive / data or feature**: FATF paragraph 87 says other sources of information that compliance and audit functions can use include training pass rates, compliance failures, and analysis of questions received from staff. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Other sources of information can include training pass rates, compliance failures, and analysis of questions received from staff"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The audit function should examine the adequacy of all risk determinations and not focus exclusively on higher risks. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should therefore not focus exclusively on higher risks"</sub>

## EXAMPLES OF COUNTRIES’ SUPERVISORY PRACTICES FOR THE IMPLEMENTATION OF THE RISK-BASED APPROACH TO THE BANKING SECTOR

- **descriptive / limitation**: The annex country supervisory practices are examples only, had not been assessed for compliance in the 4th Round of mutual evaluations at publication, and are not FATF endorsements. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should not be considered as an endorsement by FATF"</sub>

## AUSTRALIA

- **descriptive / case example**: AUSTRAC, Australia's AML/CFT regulator and FIU, supervises banks risk-based at corporate group level, applying more regulatory effort to group entities providing services with higher ML/TF exposure and vulnerability. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "applies a riskbased approach to the supervision of the banking sector at a corporate group level"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: AUSTRAC uses four factors for an RE Group's ML/TF risk profile: operating in an industry identified as a major or significant ML channel in the National Threat Assessment; exposure to ML/TF risk; specific interest from AUSTRAC intelligence; and specific interest from other competent authorities. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Four factors are taken into account in determining the ML/TF risk profile"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: AUSTRAC uses entity size and/or volume and value of transaction reports lodged as proxies for ML/TF exposure; large RE Groups, especially those lodging many reports, receive more supervision. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the size of the entity and/or the volume and value of transaction reports lodged with AUSTRAC"</sub>

## GERMANY

- **descriptive / method**: BaFin rates an institution's abstract risk on 5 criteria: location, scope of business, product structure, customer structure and distribution structure, with customer structure weighted more because ML is committed by customers. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "based on 5 essential risk criteria (location, scope of business, product structure, customer structure and distribution structure)"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Where an institution's score is at the boundary between low and enhanced risk categories, BaFin has discretion to categorise it based on its past AML/CFT history. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the supervisor has discretion to decide which risk category the institution will fall in"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: BaFin also rates quality of AML/CFT preventive measures (compliance officer task fulfilment, IT monitoring, KYC, group-wide compliance) using annual audit reports and external auditor assessments. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The ratings are based on information from the annual audit reports"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: BaFin combines potential ML/TF threat (3 high, 2 medium, 1 low) with prevention quality (A high to D low) in a 12 cell-matrix that sets AML/CFT supervision intensity. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The end result is a 12 cell-matrix"</sub>

## MEXICO

- **descriptive / method**: Mexico's CNBV sets monitoring strategies from inherent risks, considering products or services, customer types, flow of funds and geographic area, to choose which institutions to visit in its annual program; inspection results produce a risk rating that sets future inspection periodicity. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "decide which financial institutions have to be visited during the year (annual program)"</sub>

## THE NETHERLANDS

- **descriptive / method**: The Dutch Central Bank (DNB) analyses integrity risks at macro (country and global), meso (sector) and micro (institution-specific) levels and focuses supervision on the highest risk institutions. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "DNB analyses integrity risks on different levels, namely on a macro, meso and micro level"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: DNB identifies ML/TF risks from typologies, intelligence, international bodies and supervisors, supervisory visits, questionnaires to selected institutions, and a trend analysis function monitoring open sources. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "DNB has set up a trend analysis function which monitors open sources of information"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: DNB's institution risk profile has two dimensions: inherent ML/TF risk (geographical scope, customer base, products and services, distribution channel) and control level (governance, compliance function adequacy, compliance history and incidents, preventive measures). (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "two main dimensions, the ML/TF risk level and control level"</sub>

## SINGAPORE

- **descriptive / method**: MAS assesses FIs annually under its Framework for Impact and Risk Assessment on impact (systemic importance to Singapore's financial system, economy and reputation) and risk (inherent risks including ML/TF and proliferation financing, controls, governance and financial resources). (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "used to assess FIs on two aspects annually"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: MAS risk assessments consider intra-group linkages and are subject to peer comparison, challenge and review by experienced supervisors or senior panels for key FIs. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "subjected to a process of peer comparison, challenge and review"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: MAS assigns FIs to one of four supervisory significance buckets, with impact weighted more; Bucket 1 is supervised most intensely and Buckets 1 and 2 get more resources, more frequent inspections and more senior approval of risk assessments. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "with Bucket 1 FIs supervised most intensely"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: MAS off-site supervision monitors financial soundness and risk indicators, business and home country developments, reviews returns and audit reports, and holds regular meetings with management, auditors and home supervisors. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "MAS’ off-site supervision involves ongoing monitoring of an FI’s financial soundness and risk indicators"</sub>

## SOUTH AFRICA

- **descriptive / case example**: SARB's AML Review Team uses a risk-based approach because limited resources prevent inspecting all areas of a bank or all banks within a calendar year, and always requests the bank's own AML risk assessment. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "nor will it be possible to inspect all banks within a calendar year"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Under SARB's methodology, the team requests a list of products and services by business unit, assesses whether the bank's ML risk assessment adequately identifies risk, and performs its own assessment if the bank's is absent or inadequate. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In the absence of an ML risk assessment of a particular bank"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: SARB benchmarks risk factors against FATF Recommendations, Basel Core Principles and reputable typologies, segments activities, products, locations and client types into high, medium and low risk, and scopes inspections around identified high risk activities. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should be segmented between high, medium and low risk"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: FATF requires countries to identify and assess national ML/TF risks on an ongoing basis to inform regime changes, allocate and prioritise AML/CFT resources, and make information available for bank risk assessments. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "make information available for ML/TF risk assessments conducted by banks"</sub>

## UNITED KINGDOM

- **descriptive / method**: The UK FCA classifies firms by risk to its objectives and separately by money laundering risk, because ML risk does not necessarily correlate with firm size; a lower conduct risk firm may get relatively more AML/CFT attention. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "money laundering risk does not necessarily correlate to the size of a firm"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: FCA ML risk classification considers nature of business, products and services, and jurisdictions of location or operation; criteria are regularly reviewed and firms can be reclassified without delay. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "firms can be reclassified without delay as appropriate"</sub>

## UNITED STATES

- **descriptive / method**: US Federal Banking Agencies assess whether depository institutions have risk-based BSA/AML processes to identify and report suspicious activity with enough detail to be useful to law enforcement, following the FFIEC Bank Secrecy Act/Anti-Money Laundering Examination Manual. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Bank Secrecy Act/Anti-Money Laundering Examination Manual"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The FFIEC Manual lets examiners tailor scope to institution risk profile and gives the private sector a clear road map of expectations; it incorporates law enforcement information such as the U.S. National Money Laundering Strategy and U.S. Money Laundering Threat Assessment. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the U.S. National Money Laundering Strategy and the U.S. Money Laundering Threat Assessment"</sub>

## AUSTRALIA

- **descriptive / method**: AUSTRAC uses data-mining across the entire regulated population to identify reporting entities whose behaviour is an outlier versus peers, targeting further engagement, particularly in lower risk sectors. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "whose behavioural characteristics are outliers to that of their peers"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: AUSTRAC defines compliance risk as the risk an RE Group is non-compliant with legislative obligations, distinct from ML/TF risk: a high ML/TF risk entity with a strong regulatory approach can have low compliance risk. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Compliance risk is defined as the risk that an RE Group is non-compliant with its legislative obligations"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: AUSTRAC compliance techniques range from low intensity 'engagement' (mailouts, forums, guidance), moderate 'heightened' (remitter registration, desk and themed reviews, cohort transaction monitoring) to high intensity 'escalated' (on-site assessments, dedicated management). (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "High intensity or ‘escalated’ activities such as on-site assessments"</sub>

## GERMANY

- **descriptive / method**: German on-site inspections include regular annual inspections by external auditors and special/targeted inspections by auditors or the supervisor; supervisory staff join all special inspections, and the Banking Act allows thematic priorities in regular inspections. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the supervisory authority is able to decree thematic priorities"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: In German off-site supervision, low risk institutions may receive only a 'quick check' of the annual report annex, with an 'intensive check' of the full report if marks are bad; supervisors may request information, consult the compliance officer or mandate an external expert. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "only a “quick check” of this annex is done"</sub>

## MEXICO

- **descriptive / method**: Mexico's CNBV risk-rates institutions using periodic regulatory data in a risk matrix to set visit frequency, focuses on-site strategy on higher risk factors, confirms the risk level during inspections, and may issue observations, recommendations, corrective actions or sanctions, updating the off-site matrix. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "including the issuance of observations and recommendations, as well as the implementation of corrective actions"</sub>

## HONG KONG, CHINA

- **descriptive / method**: The HKMA integrates risk-based AML/CFT on-site and off-site supervision into broader banking supervision, linking frequency, intensity and scope to impact and risk level, with annual training forums sharing best practices. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "takes into account both impact to the financial system and risk level"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In 2012 the HKMA ran STR thematic reviews of 9 banks; in Q1 and Q2 2013 a further 107 banks had high-level desk reviews focused on post-reporting risk mitigation; 26 were selected for intensive desk reviews, followed by 4 thematic on-site examinations. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a further 107 banks were subject to high-level desk-based reviews over STRs"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: HKMA STR review findings were shared in training seminars in October 2012 and April 2013 and in a guidance paper developed with the Joint Financial Intelligence Unit issued on 16 December 2013. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "developed in collaboration with the Joint Financial Intelligence Unit, issued on 16 December 2013"</sub>

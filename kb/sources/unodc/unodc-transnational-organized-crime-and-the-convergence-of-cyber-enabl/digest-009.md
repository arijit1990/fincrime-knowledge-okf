## Gamification of money laundering and attractiveness of casinos, junkets and online gambling platforms

- **descriptive / case example**: In 2022 the Supreme People's Procuratorate of China described a case in which a man repeatedly bought and sold USDT on online platforms, claiming he was moving bricks for commissions while actually laundering money for cyber-scam operations. (p. 77)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "he was laundering money for cyber- scam operations"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Underregulated casinos, junkets and illegal online gambling platforms have surged across East and Southeast Asia over the past decade and form a critical part of underground banking and money laundering infrastructure. (p. 77)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "has surged across East and Southeast Asia over the past decade"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Casinos and junkets are high-volume cash businesses capable of processing vast amounts of money, with direct links to syndicates engaged in cyber-enabled fraud, trafficking for forced criminality and drug trafficking. (p. 77)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Casinos and junkets represent high- volume cash businesses capable of processing vast amounts of money"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Cash-in cash-out is the simplest typical casino laundering method: criminals exchange cash for chips and convert them back to cash, so dirty money can appear to be casino winnings; splitting money across betting accounts can make players appear less suspicious. (p. 77)[^original]

## Gamification of money laundering and attractiveness of casinos, junkets and online gambling platforms

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Collusion between gamblers (intentional losses) moves proceeds into casinos and deliberately loses them to an accomplice, evading AML policies triggered only by successful bets against the casino. (p. 78)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "proceeds of crime are brought into either physical or online casinos and deliberately lost"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Junket financing lets a client deposit money in one jurisdiction and access credit to gamble in another, with debits and credits offsetting wins and losses, allowing value to move informally below tax and law enforcement radar; junkets may pay high interest to those storing funds. (p. 78)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Gambler/client deposits money into junket account in one country or stakes other assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Offsetting arrangements or mirror transactions transfer value between jurisdictions through credit and debit relationships between entities, similar to hawala, and are increasingly used by Asia Pacific money laundering organisations linked to drugs, precursor chemicals and cybercrime. (p. 78)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Offsetting through the use of casinos and junkets as well as more traditional trade-based arrangements has been reported"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Operators provide 'safekeeping' transactions in which players, including those linked to organised crime, deposit casino chips for later cash-out. (p. 78)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "players, including those with clear links to organized crime, have been permitted to deposit casino chips for safekeeping"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Some major junket operators offer 'investment' arrangements paying investors 5 to 7 per cent per month on deposits used to capitalise the junket, with little to no due diligence on source of funds; these have been provided to illegal online casinos linked to cyber-enabled fraud. (p. 78)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "whereby 'investors' have been reported to earn between 5 to 7 per cent per month on deposited funds"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Misuse of gambling accounts for illegal transactions between players lets buyers and sellers of illegal items pay through gambling accounts and cash out the seller's credit as a claimed successful gamble. (p. 79)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Once the seller's gambling account is credited, the money can be cashed out, claiming it was a successful gamble."</sub>

## Evolution and impact of widespread underregulated and illegal online gambling

- **descriptive / limitation**: Anonymous payment methods, unlicensed MSBs, cryptocurrency, multiplier betting, multi-party transactions and cash deposits, together with authorities' limited view of gambling accounts, make it difficult to verify source of funds or whether accounts are used for legitimate gambling or crime. (p. 79)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it can be difficult to verify source of funds and whether an account is used for legitimate gambling or criminal activity"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The offshore online gambling industry has rapidly proliferated in several high-risk Southeast Asian jurisdictions, especially the Mekong, and is reported as a growing concern by authorities. (p. 79)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The rapid proliferation of the offshore online gambling industry in several high-risk jurisdictions in Southeast Asia"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Junket operators and associates drove the online gambling growth, accelerated by enhanced enforcement in mainland China and COVID-19 mobility restrictions that curbed VIP gambler travel. (p. 79)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "together with mobility restrictions brought on by the COVID-19 pandemic which curbed travel for VIP gamblers"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Junket-run online gambling platforms (e-junkets) served as credit settlement channels between junkets and clients and could disguise proceeds of organised crime as legitimate betting profits; most of the largest junket operators had such operations before displacement from Macau SAR. (p. 79)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "providing additional utility in being able to effectively disguise proceeds of organized crime as legitimate online betting profits"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Online gambling is characterised by non-face-to-face business, minimal if any compliance staff, and large complex international transaction volumes, with limited legislative harmonisation creating enforcement gaps and grey zones. (p. 79)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The online gambling sector is characterized by a non- face-to-face element, minimal, if any, compliance staff"</sub>

## 8. Identification of PEPs: Commercial and In-House Databases

- **descriptive / context**: The obligation to identify PEPs has prompted many banks to use commercial databases and similar platforms compiling publicly available information; some larger banks have in-house databases that include black-list business information on higher-risk individuals. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The obligation to identify PEPs has prompted many banks to use resources such as commercial databases"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Advantages of commercial databases include wide reach across jurisdictions and languages, large data sets, user-friendly output, flexible subscription options from manual to batch screening, and fuzzy matching with category filters. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The advantages are their wide reach across a large number of jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Databases do not contain all information because some regions have less news and internet coverage, and they produce large volumes of hits and false positives, for example 5,000 names at a 10 percent match rate yields 500 people and 10 million names yields 1,000,000 people. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "searching 5,000 names with a 10 percent match rate = 500 people"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Many databases lack identifiers such as date of birth or sex, which contributes to false positives; providers are adding identifiers but some are limited and do not draw on other public sources such as birth registries for privacy reasons. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the databases do not always have identiﬁ ers for the names, such as date of birth or sex"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The full package of database, software and staff to review hits can be quite costly. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the entire package of services—the database, software, and staff to review the hits—can become quite a costly venture."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Narrowing database search parameters can reduce false positives, but calibrating screening rules too narrowly increases the risk that PEPs are not identified; banks must use caution. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "calibrating screening rules too narrowly increases the risk that PEPs are not identiﬁ ed"</sub>
- **recommended / expectation**: Banks using automated PEP screening should consider whether screening rules are calibrated appropriately for their business nature, risk attitude and customer list, since potential matches may otherwise be missed. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Banks using automated systems for PEP screening should consider whether the screening rules"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Regulatory authorities should review the settings of PEP screening databases and the specific reliance placed on database results. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Regulatory authorities should review the settings of such databases"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Where applicable, the regulatory authority should include in onsite inspections a review of the PEP identification database, including commercial database parameters, sample transaction testing and overall database management practices. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the regulatory authority should include, as part of the onsite inspection"</sub>

## Good Practice

- **descriptive / method**: One regulatory authority assesses PEP search performance in onsite examinations, reviewing database search parameters, monitoring algorithms and in-house search feeds. (p. 67)[^original]

## 9. Identifying and Verifying Source of Wealth and Source of Funds

- **descriptive / requirement**: Understanding source of wealth and source of funds is an important component of the EDD obligations banks must apply to PEP customers and forms the baseline for enhanced ongoing monitoring. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Understanding a customer’s source of wealth and source of funds is an important com"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Legislation and regulation do not always require source of wealth and funds to be established, and FATF and FSRB evaluations found confusion over whether both concepts are in scope, causing inconsistent application. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "legislation and regulation do not always require that it be done."</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Regulatory authorities should provide guidance ensuring both source of wealth and source of funds provisions are addressed and suggest ways banks can apply them. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "regulatory authorities should take steps to provide guidance to ensure both provisions"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Source of wealth describes the activities that generated the customer's total net worth; source of funds describes the origin and means of transfer of funds accepted at account opening. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "“Source of wealth” describes the activities that have generated the total net worth"</sub>

## 9. Identifying and Verifying Source of Wealth and Source of Funds

- **mandatory / requirement**: FATF Recommendation 6(c) requires financial institutions to take reasonable measures to establish the source of wealth and the source of funds. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "take reasonable measures to establish the source of wealth and the source of funds."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: For higher-risk PEP relationships, banks should take additional steps to corroborate source of wealth information, such as using an external investigation company or a site visit. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "For particularly higher-risk PEP relationships, banks should take additional steps"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Asset and income declarations filed by many PEPs in their home jurisdictions can help determine source of wealth and build the KYC profile. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "One helpful tool in determining the source of wealth and source of funds"</sub>
- **expected / method**: It is critical that banks use a variety of tools to establish source of wealth and funds; these may include customer information corroborated by documents, internet searches and in-country referrals, and some jurisdictions require sworn statements at account opening. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The tools may include obtaining information directly from the customer"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: If a bank knows a PEP is from a jurisdiction requiring asset and income declarations, it should request a copy regardless of public availability, as the onus is on the customer. (p. 69)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the bank should request a copy. This request should be made regardless of whether the declaration is publicly available"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Legitimate reasons for refusing or not filing declarations exist, such as privacy concerns, government prohibitions or safety concerns; banks should ask the reason and decide on a risk-sensitive basis whether to continue. (p. 69)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In all cases, the bank should inquire about the reason for the refusal"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Sources for identifying jurisdictions requiring declarations include the Djankov et al. paper with its appendix A country list, and the World Bank Public Accountability Mechanisms portal covering about 75 countries. (p. 69)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "provides legislative information from approximately 75 countries on ﬁ lings"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Analyses raise issues about the quality of many asset declarations; they may provide complementary but not definitive information. (p. 69)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It should be noted that analyses of international practices on asset declarations raise issues"</sub>

## Identifying and Verifying Source of Wealth and Source of Funds

- **recommended / recommendation**: A public official should be asked to provide a copy of any asset and income declaration filed, plus updates; if refused, the bank should assess reasons and decide on a risk-based approach whether to proceed. (p. 69)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A public official should be asked to provide a copy of any asset and income declaration"</sub>

- **descriptive / finding or result**: In case of doubt about source of funds or wealth information, the banks visited would not open the account or would terminate an existing relationship. (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the banks visited indicated they would not open the account or—in the case of an existing customer—would terminate"</sub>

  <sub>anchor: "Once the source of wealth and source of funds are established, banks will need to analyze"</sub>

- **descriptive / recommendation**: Banks set guidelines for establishing source of wealth and funds that include verification and escalation requirements where there is lack of clarity or suspicion. (p. 70)[^original]

## 10. PEP Approval by Senior Management

- **mandatory / requirement**: The FATF standard requires banks to obtain senior management approval for establishing a business relationship with a PEP and for continuing a relationship with a customer who is subsequently found to be or becomes a PEP. (p. 71)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF standard requires banks to obtain senior management approval"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The meaning of senior management approval is unclear; some jurisdictions gave no interpretation or said approval may be at compliance, branch or board level, and in some the AML/CFT compliance officer is also a senior manager. (p. 71)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The exact meaning of the term, “senior management approval” is unclear."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The group compliance officer is well placed to say a person should not be accepted regardless of account size and has broader information on the customer base, group STRs and refused or terminated customers, which prevents customer shopping across locations. (p. 71)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an AML/CFT ofﬁ cer is often in the best position to say that a person should not be accepted"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In most banks visited, members of a group can share information with the group office but not with other group members, which limits information sharing. (p. 71)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the individual members of the group can share information with the group ofﬁ ce"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The group AML/CFT compliance officer (where existing) should be involved in the PEP approval process, at least in higher-risk cases. (p. 71)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the group AML/CFT compliance ofﬁ cer (where existing) should be involved in the PEP approval process"</sub>

## Recommendation

- **expected / recommendation**: In higher-risk cases, the group AML/CFT compliance officer (where existing), in addition to senior management, should be involved in the decision to accept or continue a relationship with a PEP. (p. 71)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In higher risk cases, the group AML/CFT compliance ofﬁ cer (where exist- ing)"</sub>

## 10. PEP Approval by Senior Management

- **expected / expectation**: Internal bank policies should set out who is involved in PEP decisions and how, for example unanimous agreement or a tiered decision-tree, and the policy should be in writing with decisions documented. (p. 72)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "internal bank policies should outline who is involved and how"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Most banks visited had multilayered senior management approval, often requiring separate approvals from business and compliance heads; one bank had about eight units reviewing a new PEP client, including board sign-off. (p. 72)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "At one bank, there appeared to be about eight different units that were required to review"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Other banks had fewer layers but built in escalation to separate committees or units when a PEP posed higher risk or resided in a sensitive jurisdiction. (p. 72)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Other banks had fewer layers, but built in various opportunities for escalation"</sub>

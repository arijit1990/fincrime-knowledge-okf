## 3. Applying a Risk-Based Approach

- **descriptive / context**: Many jurisdictions studied and visited apply a risk-based approach to AML, which the FATF guidance on the risk-based approach outlines; the paper does not explain it in detail. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Many of the jurisdictions studied and visited apply a risk-based approach to anti-money laundering (AML)."</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: A risk-based approach should be applied throughout the PEP process, from core CDD measures to risk management systems that identify and verify PEPs, up to enhanced ongoing monitoring. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a risk-based approach is to be applied throughout the process, from the core customer due diligence (CDD) measures"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The two successive steps are customer identification and verification, which can be risk-based, and then graded enhanced due diligence within the high-risk PEP category to reflect the real risk of each PEP. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Second is the ability to have gradation in the enhanced due diligence (EDD) inside the high-risk PEP category"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: FATF Recommendation 6(a) requires appropriate risk management systems to determine whether the customer is a PEP. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FATF Recommendation 6(a) requires “appropriate risk management systems to determine whether the customer is a PEP.”"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Some banks were reported to abuse the flexibility of the risk-based approach by tailoring it to their business model rather than their risk model, or by box-checking, so not all PEP risks are accounted for. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it was suggested that some banks were abusing its ﬂ exibility"</sub>

## Box 2.1 Applying a Risk-Based Approach in Banks

- **descriptive / definition**: For banks, a risk-based approach means focusing resources where the greatest AML risks lie, as an alternative to prescriptive approaches with uniform requirements or evenly spread resources. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "For banks, a risk-based approach to AML means focusing resources on where the greatest risks lie."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A risk-based approach assumes the bank has performed a thorough analysis of its AML risks given its business profile, products and customer base. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A risk-based approach assumes that a bank has performed a thorough analysis of the AML risks it faces"</sub>
- **descriptive / requirement**: Under a risk-based approach the bank is responsible for identifying its AML risks and, using informed judgment, designing policies and procedures to mitigate them, referring to public authority information to complement its own sources. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Under a risk-based approach, a bank is responsible to identify the AML risks it faces"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: A poorly or partially applied risk-based approach leaves control gaps, makes the system vulnerable to abuse, and is likely to produce inferior results compared with more prescriptive alternatives. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a poorly or partially applied risk-based approach will leave gaps in controls"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Applied correctly, a risk-based approach is flexible, effective and proportionate in addressing PEP risks, since PEP risk varies across customers, jurisdictions, products, channels and time. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is ﬂ exible. PEP risk varies across customers, jurisdictions, products and delivery channels, and over time."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: A risk-based approach is neither cheap nor easy and presupposes good up-to-date information, sufficient resources and technical expertise at banks and regulators, and adequate resources to mitigate risks. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Using a risk-based approach is neither cheap nor easy"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Countries should carefully consider whether a risk-based approach will produce the best results, considering availability of qualitative information, the regulator's ability to supervise and guide, and banks' resources and expertise. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Countries should carefully consider whether a risk-based approach will pro- duce the best results."</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Where a risk-based approach is applied, regulatory authorities need to make efforts to ensure the entire sector understands and applies it correctly, including for PEP systems and controls. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "regulatory authorities need to make efforts to ensure that the entire sector understands the approach"</sub>

## 4. Who Is a PEP?

- **descriptive / context**: Identifying PEPs who pose increased risk of laundering corrupt funds is an important part of a bank's AML controls, and a definition of PEPs is essential to identification. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Identifying customers, such as PEPs, who pose an increased risk of laundering corrupt funds is an important part"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: There is no internationally agreed definition of PEPs, making who counts as a PEP a difficult and politically sensitive topic. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is no internationally agreed-upon deﬁ nition of PEPs."</sub>

## Box 2.1 Applying a Risk-Based Approach in Banks

- **descriptive / limitation**: A risk-based approach might not be the best solution where the conditions it presupposes cannot be met. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A risk-based approach might, therefore, not be the best solution in jurisdictions or ﬁ nancial institu- tions where these conditions cannot be met."</sub>

## 4. Who Is a PEP?

- **descriptive / definition**: Standard setters generally agree PEPs are individuals who are, or have been, entrusted with prominent public functions, such as heads of state or government. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Standard setters generally agree that PEPs are individuals who are, or have been, entrusted with prominent public functions"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: Standard setters and many jurisdictions expect financial institutions to treat a prominent public official's family and close associates as PEPs. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "expect ﬁ nancial institutions to treat a prominent public ofﬁ cial’s family and close associates as PEPs."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Some standard setters limit the PEP definition to exclude domestic PEPs, extended family and junior or middle-ranking PEPs, and some countries issue limited lists of positions. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "some stan- dard setters limiting the scope of the PEP deﬁ nition to exclude domestic PEPs"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Core definitions that are too restrictive, such as including only immediate family and close associates, are likely to create loopholes, as corruption cases show. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "core deﬁ nitions that are too restrictive (for example, including only immediate families and close associates) are likely to create loopholes"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Most banks visited had developed a PEP definition extending to a broader group than required by standard setters, national law or regulation. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "most of the banks visited have developed a deﬁ nition that extends to a broader group"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Stakeholders said the lack of clarity in international definitions confuses and frustrates implementation, and anecdotal evidence suggests it may be used as an excuse for inaction. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the lack of a clear deﬁ - nition may also be used as an excuse for failing to take any efforts"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: FATF and UNCAC should align the definition of PEPs, and national standard setters and key stakeholders should adopt it. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FATF and UNCAC should align the deﬁ nition of PEPs."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A good PEP definition balances comprehensiveness and workability, acts as a guide rather than inflexible rules, and lets banks focus on real risks; the paper outlines core elements without proposing a definition. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A good deﬁ nition will strike a balance between being comprehensive and being workable."</sub>

## Domestic versus Foreign PEPs

- **descriptive / context**: Standard setters are inconsistent on whether PEP standards apply to foreign PEPs, domestic PEPs or both; FATF 40+9 applies PEP provisions to foreign PEPs only, with an interpretive note encouraging extension to domestic PEPs. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF 40+9 Recommen- dations apply the PEP provisions to foreign PEPs only"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: UNCAC does not distinguish between foreign and domestic PEPs, so States Parties must require financial institutions to apply EDD to both. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "which has the effect of requiring that States Parties mandate the application by ﬁ nan- cial institutions of EDD to both foreign and domestic PEPs."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: UNCAC had been signed by 140 States Parties, none of which made a reservation to Article 52(1). (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "UNCAC has been signed by 140 States Parties, none of which have made a reservation to Article 52(1)"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Under the Third EU Directive, firms must apply EDD to PEPs residing in another Member State or a third country, so EDD is not required for PEPs residing inside the jurisdiction. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "requires that ﬁ rms identify and apply EDD to PEPs who reside outside the jurisdiction"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF Recommendation 6 covers those entrusted with prominent public functions in a foreign country regardless of residence; the location of the function and residence of the holder are distinct, so the EU and FATF approaches cannot be equated. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the location of the public function associated with a PEP is separate and distinct from the residency of the function holder."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Illustration: a child of a country X minister of natural resources studying or working in country Y would not require EDD under the Third EU Directive but would under the FATF definition. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Third EU Directive would not require banks in country Y to perform EDD on the child’s accounts."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Of 82 FATF and FSRB mutual evaluation reports reviewed, 55 referred to laws, regulations or guidance on foreign or domestic PEPs; 30 countries impose requirements only on foreign PEPs and 24 on both. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "55 of the reports referred to laws, regulations, or guidance addressing foreign or domestic PEPs or both"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: The paper advocates removing the foreign/domestic distinction; laws and regulations should make no such distinction and standards should require similar EDD for both. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Laws and regulations should make no distinction between domestic and for- eign PEPs."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Legal and reputational risks are the same for domestic and foreign PEPs, since domestic politicians face the same pressures; corrupt money may enter via a victim-country bank then move through correspondent relationships. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the legal and reputational risks remain the same, whether the PEP is domestic or foreign."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The Third EU Directive's literal residency-based wording does not capture all PEPs that FATF Recommendation 6 captures, and the residency requirement is overly restrictive, excluding PEPs that can be a significant risk to the banking sector. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this residency require- ment is overly restrictive and results in the exclusion of PEPs"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Most legislators have decided not to classify domestic office holders as PEPs, although banks in some jurisdictions are expected to consider domestic PEP customers on a risk-sensitive basis. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "most legislators have made a po- litical decision not to classify domestic ofﬁ ce holders as PEPs"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 41 lists jurisdictions with requirements for foreign and domestic PEPs, including Antigua and Barbuda, Argentina, Mexico and South Africa; Brazil also imposes requirements for both; one country imposes requirements for domestic PEPs only. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Mutual evaluation reports from ju- risdictions with laws, regulations, or guidance addressing foreign and domestic PEPs"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Covering domestic PEPs would increase credibility of governments' anti-corruption and AML commitments, particularly for UNCAC States Parties treating both equally. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "such efforts would increase the credibility of the governments’ commitment to fighting corruption"</sub>

## Family Members and Close Associates

- **descriptive / context**: Corrupt officials increasingly use family members and close associates to disguise ownership of corrupt assets, so the PEP definition should include them. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Corrupt public ofﬁ ce holders appear to be increasingly employing strategies to disguise their ownership"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: UNCAC includes as close associates persons and companies related to the official; the FATF 40+9 Recommendations are silent on close associates; the Third EU Directive adds joint beneficial ownership of legal entities or arrangements. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "UNCAC includes as close associates both persons and companies that are related to the individual"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: UNCAC and FATF do not limit the degree of family relationship, whereas the Third EU Directive focuses on immediate family, which may be insufficient where extended families are very close. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Third EU Directive focuses on immediate family members, which may not be sufﬁ cient"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: FATF and UNCAC refer to family members and close associates in the PEP definition and require the same EDD as for the office holder, but literal wording differs, which one regulator read as a possible distinction. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the literal terminology differs slightly because of the sentence structure."</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: FATF should clarify the PEP definition so it includes family members and close associates along with holders of prominent public functions. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FATF should clarify the deﬁ nition of PEPs to ensure that it includes family members and close associates"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Jurisdictions should clarify the PEP definition so it includes family members and close associates along with holders of prominent public functions. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Jurisdictions should clarify the deﬁ nition of PEPs to ensure that it includes family members"</sub>

## Other Categories

- **descriptive / context**: UNCAC lists no categories of PEPs whereas FATF does; some jurisdictions set out specific position categories and some banks add categories beyond legal requirements, such as senior decision makers of international organisations, media, religious organisations and public enterprises. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "UNCAC does not list categories of PEPs, however FATF does."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Wolfsberg Group additional categories include senior armed forces officers, members of ruling royal families with governance responsibilities, senior executives of state-owned enterprises and senior officials of major political parties. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Wolfsberg Group has out- lined a number of additional categories"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Heads of supranational bodies, members of parliament, senior diplomatic corps members and central bank board members may also be considered within the definition but may be excluded on a risk-based approach. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may also be considered to fall within the deﬁ - nition, but may be excluded on a risk-based approach."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Categories can be too restrictive; limiting judicial officials to supreme court judges is problematic because corruption in lower courts rarely reaches the supreme court. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "limiting “judicial ofﬁ cials” to supreme court judges is problematic in practice"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Where subnational or state political officials have exposure comparable to national positions, the definition should encourage institutions to treat them as PEPs on a risk-sensitive basis. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the deﬁ nition should encourage ﬁ nancial institutions to treat those customers as PEPs on a risk-sensitive basis."</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: If a country has adopted a risk-based approach, it is important that banks decide for themselves whether additional categories are relevant, and where risk is higher the net will be cast wider. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Therefore, if a country has adopted a risk-based approach, it is important that banks themselves decide whether additional categories are relevant for their purposes."</sub>

## 5. How Long Is a PEP Considered a PEP?

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Neither FATF 40+9 nor UNCAC impose or recommend time limits on PEP status after leaving office ('once a PEP, always a PEP'). (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Neither the FATF 40+9 Recommendations nor UNCAC impose or recommend any time limits"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Applying EDD indefinitely to all former office holders and their families and associates would be disproportionate, though indefinite treatment may be appropriate for some, such as some heads of state. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Applying EDD measures to all former ofﬁ ce holders—and their families and close associates—for an inﬁ nite time would be disproportionate."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Some jurisdictions introduced time limits after which banks need not automatically treat former PEPs as high risk; one jurisdiction requires EDD only for current officials and their families and associates. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "One jurisdiction only requires EDD for current ofﬁ cials and their family members and close associates"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Time limits are artificial and can create a false sense of security; corrupt PEPs continue moving proceeds after leaving office, and the shorter the period, the greater the problem. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Time limits are always artiﬁ cial and pose problems"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Banks should be encouraged to consider ongoing PEP status case by case using a risk-based approach, and regulators should provide guidance on this. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Banks should, therefore, be encouraged to consider the ongoing PEP status of their customer on a case-by-case basis"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In each bank visited the period treated as PEP status was likely several years, if not decades, for example for heads of state or other senior PEPs. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the period of time was likely to be several years, if not decades"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Where risk is low, banks can consider declassifying the relationship, but only after careful consideration of risks and with senior management approval. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Where risk is low, banks can consider declassifying the relationship, but only after careful consideration"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: One bank allowed declassification of a PEP after a set period without considering ongoing risk. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "One bank indicated that it allowed declassiﬁ cation of a PEP after a set period of time"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Where a person has ceased to hold a prominent public function, countries should not introduce time limits on how long the person, family member or close associate is treated as a PEP. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries should not introduce time limits on the length of time the person"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Good practice: a designated PEP committee meets regularly to discuss whether former PEP customers still pose increased money laundering risk, with unanimous decisions. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The creation of a designated PEP committee that meets regularly"</sub>

## 6. Identification of PEPs: Who to Check and When to Check

- **expected / requirement**: Identifying PEPs requires effective CDD including identifying beneficial owners, and financial institutions should have appropriate risk-management systems to determine whether a customer is a PEP. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The identiﬁ cation of PEPs requires effective CDD processes, including the identiﬁ ca- tion of beneﬁ cial owners."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: All banks interviewed applied PEP checks to all customers at account opening and periodically to existing customers. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "All banks interviewed applied PEP checks to all customers at the account opening stage"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Risk-management measures can include asking questions, database checks and reviewing public information. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "such as asking the necessary questions, performing a database check, referring to publicly available information"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Banks with lower PEP risk should focus greater effort on relationships with increased money laundering risk, since multiple checks in every case could be disproportionate; regulators should provide guidance. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "banks should use appropriate risk-management systems to focus greater efforts on the relationships"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: All jurisdictions visited said they require banks to check whether a beneficial owner is a PEP, though not always explicitly in legislation. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "All jurisdictions visited said they require banks to check whether a beneﬁ cial owner is a PEP"</sub>

## Private banking

- **descriptive / case example**: In private banking institutions visited, the PEP check was often made before the relationship was established; the account could not be opened until beneficial ownership and PEP identification investigations were done. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the account could not be opened until all relevant investigations"</sub>

## 6. Identification of PEPs: Who to Check and When to Check

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Of the 82 FATF and FSRB evaluations reviewed, a number of jurisdictions were not required by law or regulation to check whether the beneficial owner is a PEP, although the FATF Recommendation 6 Methodology lists this as an essential criterion. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a number of jurisdictions were not required, by law or regula- tion, to check if the beneﬁ cial owner was a PEP"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Law or regulation should include a requirement to determine whether a beneficial owner is a PEP in accordance with the Methodology for FATF Recommendation 6. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Law or regulation should include a requirement to determine whether a beneﬁ cial owner is a PEP"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Banks should check whether a potential customer, existing customer or beneficial owner is a PEP; in private banking this check was often made before the relationship was established. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In the private banking institutions visited, this check was often made before the establishment of a business relationship"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Because existing clients may become PEPs after entering a relationship, existing client bases should be regularly checked for changes in PEP status. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is essential that a bank’s existing client base also be regularly checked"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Banks used various triggers for rechecking existing clients: regular database screening, public information on corrupt PEPs as a trigger for ad hoc checks, and checks during KYC updates. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Others checked ex- isting customers for PEP status as part of the normal updating of know-your-customer (KYC) information."</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Once a PEP is identified, senior management should decide whether to continue the relationship and, if so, apply EDD in line with national legal and regulatory requirements. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "senior management should decide whether to continue the business relationship, and if so, apply EDD measures"</sub>

## 1. Introduction

- **descriptive / definition**: PEPs are individuals who are, or have been, entrusted with prominent public functions, together with their family members and close associates. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "PEPs—individuals who are, or have been, entrusted with prominent public functions and their family members and close associates"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: PEPs represent a greater money laundering risk because they may abuse their position and influence to carry out corrupt acts and launder the proceeds through domestic and international financial systems. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "represent a greater money laundering risk because of the possibility that such individuals may abuse their po- sition and inﬂ uence"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Most PEPs do not actually engage in corrupt activities, but all PEPs are potentially in a position to misuse their positions for personal gain, regardless of country, business activity or seniority. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Obviously, most PEPs do not actually engage in corrupt activities; however, all PEPs are potentially in a position to misuse"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Although corrupt PEPs may be a small portion of all PEPs, a single corrupt PEP can have a disproportionate impact on a country and sometimes an entire region. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "While corrupt PEPs may be a small portion of the entire number of PEPs, a single corrupt PEP’s behavior can have a disproportionate impact"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Quantifying the amount of money stolen and laundered by corrupt PEPs has proven difficult, so available estimates are only rough approximations of the order of magnitude. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Quantifying the amount of money that has been stolen and laundered"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The term corruption is meant to include the UNCAC offences in Articles 15-22, including active and passive bribery of national and foreign officials, embezzlement, trading in influence, illicit enrichment and private-sector bribery. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The term “corruption” is meant to include the offenses outlined in Articles 15-22 of UNCAC"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: StAR considers that the definitions of corruption should be aligned with UNCAC, and that this alignment should be reflected in other StAR policy papers. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "StAR considers there should be an align- ment of the deﬁ nitions of corruption with UNCAC"</sub>

  <sub>anchor: "For the purposes of this paper, PEPs are to include individuals who are, or have been, entrusted with prominent public functions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The World Bank estimates that more than $1 trillion is paid in bribes each year. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The World Bank estimates that more than $1 trillion dollars is paid in bribes each year."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The StAR Initiative estimated that corrupt money received by public officials in developing and transition countries reaches $20 billion to $40 billion per year, equivalent to 20 to 40 percent of flows of official development assistance. (p. 24)[^original]

  <sub>anchor: "This scale of corruption leads to degradation and distrust of public institutions, insufﬁ cient government revenues"</sub>

- **descriptive / context**: In 2003 FATF introduced preventive measures to identify higher-risk PEPs and improve monitoring of their transactions, set out in Recommendation 6 of the FATF 40+9 Recommendations alongside Recommendation 5 on customer due diligence. (p. 25)[^original]

- **mandatory / requirement**: UNCAC Article 52(1) requires each State Party, in accordance with domestic law, to require banks to verify customer identity, take reasonable steps to identify beneficial owners of high-value accounts, and conduct enhanced scrutiny of accounts of PEPs and their family members and associates. ## 2. General Observations and Challenges

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Compliance with FATF Recommendation 6 is poor; low compliance is not concentrated in certain regions and is relatively worse in developed countries. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "compliance rates are relatively worse in developed countries"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Of 82 mutual evaluation reports reviewed, 40 percent of jurisdictions had no enforceable legislation or regulations concerning PEPs at the time of evaluation. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "40 percent of the jurisdictions did not have enforceable legislation or regulations concerning PEPs"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Where PEP-specific provisions existed they were often not applied to all sectors within FATF 40+9 scope, and information on effective implementation was limited. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Where PEP- speciﬁ c legal provisions, regulations, or other enforceable guidance existed, they were often not applied to all sectors"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Some laws have a narrow definition of enhanced due diligence: several countries required senior management approval only for new PEP customers at account opening, not existing customers who became PEPs. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a number of countries only required senior management approval for new PEP customers identiﬁ ed during account opening"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: PEPs are a special category of customer, all designated as high risk for money laundering under the FATF 40+9 Recommendations. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "PEPs are a special category of customer, all designated as high risk for money launder- ing."</sub>

## Growing indication of generative AI use, deepfake fraud, and other malicious technology

- **descriptive / finding or result**: AI-powered chatbots and deepfake technology risk becoming a favoured criminal tool in cyberfraud, increasing automation of Southeast Asian operations; there is growing indication of deepfake fraud used both to perpetrate scams and bank fraud to bypass KYC measures, a significant threat to individuals and the banking industry prompting urgent action by regulators, law enforcement and financial institutions. (p. 72)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "deepfake fraud being deployed to both perpetrate scams and bank fraud to bypass KYC measures"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 199: deepfakes are synthetic media digitally manipulated to convincingly replace one person's likeness with another's by manipulating facial appearance through deep generative methods, using machine learning and AI to manipulate or generate visual and audio content that more easily deceives viewers. (p. 72)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Deepfakes are synthetic media that have been digitally manipulated to replace one person’s likeness convincingly"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A recent independent identity fraud study (Sumsub, 2023) noted a 1,530 per cent increase in deepfake incidents in Asia Pacific between 2022 and 2023, the second-highest region by incidents, with the report noting Indonesia, Hong Kong and Cambodia had more than a twofold increase in identity fraud percentages over two years; available data is limited. (p. 72)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a 1,530 per cent increase in deepfake incidents in the Asia Pacific region between 2022 and 2023"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The same study found the cryptocurrency industry was the main target for AI-driven deepfake fraud, at 88 per cent of all deepfake cases detected in 2023, and that Viet Nam and Japan ranked highest for deepfake fraud prevalence in 2023. (p. 73)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "representing 88 per cent of all deepfake cases detected in 2023"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Using AI to create near-indistinguishable images and voices, fraudsters run social engineering scams with high success rates, including sophisticated investment fraud and financial grooming such as 'pig butchering' and increasingly task scams, sextortion, and schemes impersonating law enforcement officers and other government officials. (p. 73)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "fraudsters can execute social engineering scams with alarming success rates, exploiting people’s trust and emotions"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 202: task scams involve online recruitment of victims for apparent remote work in which they carry out online tasks (liking posts, leaving automated reviews) for commission, but 'earning' more requires layers of cryptocurrency 'investment' into the tiered operation, which is then stolen. (p. 73)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Task scams involve the online recruitment of victims for what appears to be a remote work scheme"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Criminals exploit stolen personal data from the dark web and 'grey and black market' Telegram groups, using mined social media profile information and photos to build fake identities and 'masks' that can bypass facial recognition and KYC measures, compounding challenges around money-muling, money laundering and high-risk and underground banking. (p. 73)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to create fake identities and ‘masks’ that can bypass facial recognition systems and KYC measures"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Authorities report Southeast Asian cyberfraud operations rapidly diversifying into development of malicious mobile and web applications or malware, the broader blockchain gaming industry, online bank fraud schemes, underground cryptocurrency exchange and payment services, and a broad range of cybercrimes as a service. (p. 73)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "offering a broad range of cybercrimes as a service"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Deepfake-related cyberfraud incidents have been reported in Canada, China, India, the Philippines, Thailand, Viet Nam and the United States. In April 2023 Fuzhou (printed 'Fuzou') police and state media reported a person who got a WeChat video call from a close friend and remitted US $590,000 within 10 minutes; police froze most funds with counterparts in inner Mongolia where the recipient account was opened. That month Anhui police also detained scammers who used AI face-swapping and voice synthesis to trick a man into transferring a large, undisclosed sum to a supposed friend. (p. 74)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "received a WeChat video call from a close friend and remitted US $590,000"</sub>

  <sub>anchor: "anticipate this new modus operandi to cascade across the region"</sub>

## Developments and vulnerabilities in the metaverse

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The metaverse market was US $65.5 billion as of 2022 with over 400 million active users; research shows potential to generate up to US $5 trillion in value by 2030, which the report says is likely to attract innovative, opportunistic and increasingly high-tech organized crime groups although mass adoption has not yet occurred. (p. 74)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the market size of the metaverse207 was US $65.5 billion as of 2022"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 207: the metaverse is the emerging 3D-enabled digital space using virtual reality, augmented reality and other advanced internet and semiconductor technology to allow lifelike personal and business experiences online. (p. 74)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The metaverse is the emerging 3D-enabled digital space that uses virtual reality, augmented reality"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Because of its decentralized nature, identity and verification challenges, difficulty tracing virtual asset origins, complicated legal issues, technological advancement and lack of uniform legislation, the metaverse presents numerous AML compliance concerns; with no intermediary such as banks and no central organization monitoring and regulating transactions, applying AML requirements is difficult. (p. 74)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the metaverse presents numerous AML compliance concerns and risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: As the metaverse grows it will be increasingly hard to ensure in-game transactions follow AML rules without a centralized authority, and AML compliance mechanisms are hard to monitor and enforce; unlike banks, which are gatekeepers, participants in the decentralized metaverse are solely accountable for adhering to and enforcing AML policies and reporting suspicious activity, and participants often use aliases or stay anonymous, making true identity hard to determine. (p. 75)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "participants in the decentralized metaverse are solely accountable for adhering to and enforcing AML policies"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The metaverse is a worldwide network, so jurisdictional complexity and cross-border transactions worsen the existing difficulty of enforcing AML laws across countries, especially for cross-border payments; lack of standardized AML legislation and differing legal frameworks hinder a cohesive approach. (p. 75)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the metaverse’s decentralized and international character, especially for cross-border payments"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The metaverse develops rapidly, with financial products and digital asset types often outpacing regulatory frameworks and AML compliance procedures; the regulatory environment is hugely disjointed across jurisdictions, and businesses operating within the metaverse struggle with the absence of standardization because they must navigate varied legal obligations and compliance standards. (p. 75)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial products and digital asset types are continuously developing, frequently exceeding regulatory frameworks"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Although the metaverse is largely in its infancy, organized crime groups are increasingly targeting business projects in the broader online gaming industry, particularly blockchain gaming, and several metaverse casinos have emerged; the excerpt text on a Decentraland ICE Poker metaverse casino is fragmentary, noting other metaverse casinos use a traditional model in which players convert fiat into metaverse tokens to wager. (p. 75)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "growing indication of organized crime groups targeting business projects related to the broader online gaming industry"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: One major metaverse casino claiming registration in a Southeast Asian country reported cumulative revenue above US $40 million in the first three months of 2022; it claims to enable neither fiat nor convertible virtual currency transactions, with players buying, selling, trading and lending NFTs in a play-to-earn model, and a legal opinion on its landing page says the platform would generally not contravene local gambling laws as there is no staking or wagering of money or money's worth, posing a unique regulatory challenge. (p. 75)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "reported generating cumulative revenue exceeding US $40 million in the first three months of 2022"</sub>

## Alvin Chau Cheok-wa (周焯华) and Suncity junket

- **descriptive / case example**: In November 2021 the Wenzhou City Public Security Bureau issued an arrest warrant for Macau SAR-based junket mogul Alvin Chau, accused of running a massive illegal online and proxy betting operation, money laundering and leading an organized crime group, following an investigation begun in 2019; the activity was allegedly conducted under cover of the Suncity junket and VIP rooms, then the world's biggest junket operator, responsible for as much as 50 per cent of high-roller gambling turnover in Macau SAR. (p. 79)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "accused of running a massive illegal online and proxy betting operation as well as money laundering"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In a related case the Wenzhou Intermediate People's Court convicted 36 individuals connected to the Chau-led syndicate, finding the group provided cross-border currency exchange and settlement services and collected gambling debts through asset management companies and underground banks it set up on the mainland. (p. 79)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "convicted 36 individuals connected to the Chau-led syndicate"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Alvin Chau was convicted in Macau SAR in January 2023 and given an 18-year prison term on more than 100 counts connected with facilitating illegal bets of over US $105 billion in the period March 2013 to March 2021. Seized data also show that from 2015 to 2019 Chau and Suncity handled an estimated 300+ billion yuan (US $42 billion) in bets placed by online gamblers in China through the group's illegal offshore operations. (p. 79)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "sentenced to 18 years in prison on over 100 charges relating to facilitating illegal bets"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Court determined that between 2016 and 2021 the Suncity-linked consortium grew to more than 280 mainland Chinese shareholder-level agents, more than 38,000 gambling agents/promoters and more than 80,000 players, totalling at least US $160 million in illegal cross-border payments and transactions through Suncity's regional network of VIP rooms and associated online gambling and phone betting platforms in offshore casinos in jurisdictions including Cambodia, the Philippines and Viet Nam. (p. 80)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "more than 280 mainland Chinese shareholder-level agents, more than 38,000 gambling agents/promoters"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Suncity is no longer a licensed Macau SAR junket operator, though related entities under different names are believed still involved in the casino industry in Russia, the Isle of Man, the Philippines and Viet Nam; footnote 9 adds that Suncity was also active in Lao PDR and Myanmar, facilitating illegal gambling and payment services for organized crime. (p. 80)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Suncity is no longer a licensed Macau SAR junket operator"</sub>

## Mechanics

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Chau was found to have led a side-betting business named 'Main Camp' enabling large-scale underground banking, money laundering and tax evasion, corroborated by Suncity executives' testimony including a former accounting director; it used multiplier betting (under-the-table betting or 'tok dai') and existing Suncity corporate and technological infrastructure and resources to hide underground transactions as legitimate gambling flows. (p. 80)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Chau was found to have led a side-betting business named ‘Main Camp’"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Footnote 10 repeats the multiplier betting definition: a bet formally denominated at the casino tables represents only a fraction of a private bet between gamblers and junket operators, to avoid gaming revenue levies; it lets clients pre-negotiate payment method, currency and cash-out method, raises junket promoter commissions, and conceals the total amount an individual bettor moves through the casino. It is popular because most Macau SAR junket customers come from mainland China, cannot bring money due to capital controls and the gambling ban, and rely on junket agent credit (example: HK $1 million credit against HK $100,000 of chips, a ten times multiplier). (p. 80)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the junket agent can request the casino to provide HK $100,000 worth of chips"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The side-betting business included online and proxy betting through Suncity's regional VIP rooms, associated bank accounts and online and phone betting platforms, and informal cross-border money transfers disguised as gambling credit issued to VIPs; illegal transactions ran through Suncity VIP cash cards called 'Operation Reserve Cards', managed with IT systems called 'RollsMary' and 'SunPeople' recording client data, betting volumes, credit issuance and debts, hotel room bookings and more. (p. 81)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Illegal transactions were carried out through Suncity VIP cash cards, referred to as ‘Operation Reserve Cards’"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: To finance and expand the illegal side-betting business, Chau targeted high-net-worth VIP members in various jurisdictions, offering company equity and gaming commissions as incentive to 'invest' and provide capital and liquidity to the side-betting companies he controlled; these customers became Suncity agents. (p. 81)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "offered company equity and gaming commissions as an incentive to ‘invest’ and provide capital and liquidity"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Law enforcement and financial intelligence officials indicate Suncity offered high interest rates for storing money with the junket, and many investors preferred it to traditional financial institutions because of higher returns, greater flexibility and virtually no due diligence and screening, effectively making it an international bank for criminals needing to move money globally. (p. 81)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "higher returns, greater flexibility, and virtually no due diligence and screening"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: When Suncity VIP members wanted apparent gambling credit at an overseas operation via the side-betting business, the Suncity exchange rate department devised an offsetting arrangement by randomly selecting an agent holding both a Suncity currency account and a bank account in the destination and currency, and asking the agent to help conduct an informal money transfer by issuing the corresponding credit. (p. 81)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Suncity exchange rate department would devise an offsetting arrangement"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: In the Suncity offsetting process the recipient gambler at the destination transferred funds to the agent's preferred account while the Suncity currency exchange department transferred the corresponding funds to the player's Suncity account minus the junket fee (commission); the process was reversed to move funds the other way, and as many as 100 calls per day are understood to have been received about such cross-border transfers before Suncity junket operations closed. (p. 81)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "as many as 100 calls per day are understood to have been received"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Suncity is understood to have developed a multi-billion-dollar real estate portfolio through payment settlement arrangements devised to cover what appeared to be VIP customer gambling losses. The excerpt breaks off mid-sentence, saying that clients using this system would be able to bypass the formal financial system and informally transmit (the object is cut off). (p. 81)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "multi-billion-dollar real estate portfolio through its payment settlement arrangements"</sub>
- **expected / definition**: Footnote 15 explains that junket-based offsetting arrangements, also called mirror transactions, are a means of junket financing in which the gambler deposits money into a junket account or stakes local assets in one jurisdiction and can access this credit, minus a fee, in another. It says the model should be limited to gambling, but in practice it has become a favoured typology for underground banking and money laundering, because a system of debits and credits lets operators move money quickly and informally below the radar of tax and law enforcement agencies. Facilitators arrange for money debited from an entity in one jurisdiction to be credited to (sometimes the same) entity in a second, requiring the facilitator to have fund access in both. (p. 81)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "While this model should be limited to gambling, in practice it has become a favoured typology"</sub>

## Wan (Broken Tooth) Kuok Koi and cryptocurrency-integrated ‘e-junkets’

- **descriptive / case example**: Longtime senior 14K triad member Wan Kuok-Koi announced a 2017 Initial Coin Offering for the 'HB Token' (also 'Triad Coin') and was involved in at least two related projects, 'Dragon Coin' (DRG) and the Saixigang 'smart city' Industrial Park in Karen State, Myanmar; the report calls this among the best-known examples of criminal convergence around online casinos, e-junkets and cryptocurrency. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "longtime senior member of the 14K triad, Wan Kuok-Koi, and the announcement of his 2017 Initial Coin Offering"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Triad Coin traded exclusively on A.Top, an exchange launched by All In Group, a Hong Kong company registered in April 2018 whose sole shareholder was reported to be 3658 Investment and Management, a business partner of Internet Technology Company 1. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Triad Coin traded exclusively on A.Top, an exchange launched by All In Group"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: According to reports, Wan Kuok-Koi's 2017 Triad Coin (HB Token) ICO produced token sales of 450 million and US $750 million in proceeds, via his company World Hongmen Investment, working with Internet Technology Company 1, which was to develop and run linked online poker tournaments. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "sale of 450 million tokens, raising US $750 million through Koi’s company, World Hongmen Investment"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In practice clients could buy Triad Coin with another crypto or fiat currency and transfer it to the casino operator in exchange for casino credits logged on A.Top's 'Allchain' ledger, letting clients wager online and draw down the ledger for losses. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "casino credits logged on A.Top’s ‘Allchain’ ledger"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Koi was associated with Macau Dragon, which launched the Dragon Coin (DRG) ICO with Thailand-based Holding Company 1 in 2018; the project, since shut down, planned to integrate cryptocurrency into high-stakes international gambling through partnerships with Macau SAR junket operators. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Koi was associated with Macau Dragon"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Dragon Coin operating model: gamblers use local currency to buy DRG, travel to a participating casino, exchange DRG for Dragon Global Chips (in-game token) to play, afterwards turn chips back into DRG or other currencies, and withdrawn funds can be disbursed to gamblers in a preferred jurisdiction (source: DRG white paper). (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Gamblers use local currencyto buy Dragon Coin (DRG)"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Macau Dragon's whitepaper said the project aimed to cut the 5-7 per cent fee VIP gamblers historically incurred under junket offsetting by using blockchain; players would exchange fiat into DRG, convert it to Dragon Chip non-fungible tokens, and later swap back to DRG and cash out for fiat and luxury prizes via a smartphone wallet. The whitepaper contains no information on measures to address money laundering concerns. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the project aimed to reduce the 5-7 per cent fee historically incurred by VIP gamblers"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Both Triad Coin and Dragon Coin showed rapid price rises and equally dramatic falls ending in a stoppage of trading, which the report says indicates possible use of the crypto in money laundering and/or a 'pump and dump' investor fraud scheme. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "rapid price increases and equally dramatic decreases, culminating in a stoppage in trading activity"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: On 9 December 2020 US Treasury OFAC designated Koi as a leader of the 14K triad, described as one of the largest Chinese organized crime organizations in the world engaged in drug trafficking, illegal gambling, racketeering, human trafficking and other crimes including bribery, corruption and graft. The Treasury also alleges the Hongmen Association co-opted elite figures in Malaysia and Cambodia and built a business network in cryptocurrency development and launching, real estate and a security company. (p. 57)[^original]

## Attractiveness of online gambling as a money laundering and underground banking method

- **descriptive / data or feature**: Figure 12 shows the traditional pyramid of illegal betting: organizer, super master agent, chief/master agent, agent, brokers and clients, with credit granted down the chain, customers graded, customers settling credit, and commission taken at each level. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Figure 12� Traditional pyramid nature of illegal betting"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Anonymous payment methods and authorities' very limited view inside online gambling accounts make it difficult to verify whether an account is used for real gambling or for laundering and underground banking; the sector is also non-face-to-face, has minimal compliance staff and huge complex, often international transaction flows, and limited harmonization of legislation across jurisdictions complicates investigations. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "authorities have a very limited view of what goes on in an online gambling account"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: As DNFBPs, online casino operators' compliance regimes lag considerably behind those of banks, making online casinos attractive targets for criminals and enabling a 'heads-down' approach by operators. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the sophistication of the compliance regimes instituted by online casino operators lags considerably behind those at banks"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Based on cases in multiple jurisdictions, the report lists the most significant AML vulnerabilities in the online casino sector in East and Southeast Asia and the Pacific, 'not limited to' those named. First, non-face-to-face transactions (anonymity): risks around identifying and verifying the customer registering the account; stolen identities are frequently used, and profiles may be set up with stolen IDs or handed to a third party to transact for high-risk clients including criminals, PEPs or sanctioned persons without appropriate enhanced CDD. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "non-face-to-face business relationships can present unique AML risks around identification and verification"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Figure 13 depicts the pyramid of online betting markets in East and Southeast Asia: major Asian bookmakers and other Asian bookmakers hedge with international sportsbooks; below them national master agents (betting syndicates, which may act as a sportsbook), regional super agents (which may sometimes act as a sportsbook), and district agents serving clients, with trading flows and hedging between levels. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Figure 13� Pyramid nature of online betting markets in East and Southeast Asia"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Second, third party transactions: among the biggest vulnerabilities, linked to the non-face-to-face nature of online casinos, where money deposited by one player can be withdrawn and paid into another party's account; for example Player A deposits via an e-wallet then withdraws to a crypto address owned by another party, which could be payment for drugs. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "third party transactions are among the biggest vulnerabilities where money deposited by one player could be withdrawn"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Third, miscoding transactions: relatively commonplace in online gambling in East and Southeast Asia to avoid government scrutiny; for example a card charge may appear as 'flower shop' rather than 'online casino'. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "miscoding transactions have been relatively commonplace in the online gambling industry"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Fourth, gambling in exchange for luxury goods using in-game online gambling points: a common e-junket service in which in-game credit is redeemed online and exchanged for high-value items such as luxury watches, handbags, vehicles and vacations. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "in-game credit can be redeemed online and exchanged for high-value items"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Fifth, gambling in exchange for illicit goods using an online gambling account: a scheme advertised by several land-based and online casino operators in high-risk Southeast Asian jurisdictions in which a buyer and seller of illegal goods join the same game, the buyer transfers funds to the seller through a game or bet, and the seller collects the funds from their payment account in exchange for the goods; the gambling account acts like a bank account and the money cannot easily be traced to illicit activity. It has been attributed to major organized crime groups in the region using junkets to disburse large payments. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a buyer and a seller of illegal goods participate in the same game"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Sixth, safe-keeping transactions using an online gambling account: an account with an illegal, unregulated or underregulated provider can be used purely to store and hide money from authorities, with money concealed and retrieved via the same payment method or underground channels. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "can be used exclusively for storing money and hiding it from the authorities"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Seventh, widespread unregulated live-dealer proxy betting: authorities report it within junket VIP rooms and online casino operations; it poses high money laundering risk because of customer identification verification challenges and appears most common in Cambodia, Lao PDR, Myanmar and the Philippines, with many large operators linked to known criminal networks. Combined with offsetting through e-junkets and loose regulation, it has let unidentified individuals move and launder vast sums between jurisdictions with a click or phone call. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "widespread unregulated and unauthorized live-dealer proxy betting taking place within junket VIP rooms"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 126: proxy betting is seen as a 'competitive edge' for the Southeast Asian casino industry; one person outside a casino gives betting instructions to an accomplice (the proxy) inside, who buys chips, places bets and cashes in winnings; in more sophisticated operations casinos provide a live video feed so gamblers watch from another jurisdiction and instruct the proxy by phone. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "involves one person somewhere outside a casino giving betting instructions to an accomplice (the proxy), inside"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Eighth, challenges of funds verification and commingling of proceeds of crime: difficulty verifying source of funds and misuse of platforms to commingle proceeds with recreational gambling flows, largely due to varied payment providers (including unregulated methods and intermediaries not subject to adequate AML controls), proliferation of underground online platforms and general lack of CDD by underregulated operators. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "difficulties in source of funds verification and misuse of online gambling platforms for commingling proceeds of crime"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Authorities also report larger organized-crime-affiliated operators using smaller online casino platforms to further obfuscate the source of funds by funnelling gross gaming revenues through the smaller platforms at a fee of 1 to 2 per cent to add layers of commingling. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "use of smaller online casino platforms by larger operators affiliated with organized crime groups"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Tenth, inadequate AML/CDD policy implementation: authorities in Southeast Asia voiced clear and growing concern about online casino operators' implementation of AML due diligence policies and procedures; lack of understanding and lack of prioritization of risk management, internal controls, reporting and AML measures has been flagged to UNODC several times. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "clear and growing concerns around the implementation of AML due diligence policies and procedures"</sub>
- **expected / typology or red flag**: Beneficial ownership is listed as an online casino vulnerability: the risk of criminals owning or infiltrating an online casino is demonstrated by numerous cases and deemed high by many authorities consulted, and numerous payment providers including VASPs have also been infiltrated by organized crime, raising the money laundering risk further. The report says the risks of criminal elements owning, controlling or infiltrating the casino, and owning or controlling the payment provider, should be actively managed and monitored. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should be actively managed and monitored"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Eleventh, considerable gaps in awareness, regulatory enforcement and investigative capacity: authorities in Southeast Asia, particularly the Mekong, indicate severely low levels of understanding and both investigative and regulatory enforcement capacity, and low AML awareness about online casino risks and vulnerabilities. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "indicate severely low levels of understanding and both investigative and regulatory enforcement capacity"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Twelfth, difficulties prosecuting predicate offences: rapid online casino growth has made casino laundering more widespread and made tracing proceeds and prosecuting offenders significantly harder; authorities say their inability to trace laundered funds has increased use of a legal technicality, namely being unable to prove beyond reasonable doubt that gambled funds correspond to the predicate offence. Some players divide funds across several betting accounts to look less suspicious and further complicate prosecution. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "unable to prove beyond a reasonable doubt in court that the gambled funds correspond to the predicate offence"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Thirteenth, broadening infiltration of organized crime: advances in information and gambling technologies have significantly lowered barriers to entry into Southeast Asia's online casino industry; with the 'white-label' model this has attracted many new actors and effectively decentralized regional money laundering, creating huge challenges for law enforcement and financial investigators. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "significantly lowered the barriers to entry for organized criminal actors"</sub>

## White-labelling

- **descriptive / definition**: White-label gambling websites are like franchises: operators can outsource every business component including complex betting software, website design, customer data and relationship management, technical support, branding and marketing, and often operating licences from third-party providers; this lets an operator set up a gambling website within weeks with virtually no bookmaking or technical expertise. This is described as a major challenge across both regulated and underregulated Southeast Asian jurisdictions. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "set up a gambling website in a matter of weeks"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 130: betting software is highly complex (odds-making and risk management, live streaming and sports data feeds, CRM and technical support, payment processing including crypto-integrated solutions) and is commonly licensed for a flat monthly fee, a percentage of bettors' losing bets, or both; in a complete white-label solution a large proportion of turnover is funnelled upstream from operators to the supplier or franchiser. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Software is commonly licensed by operators for a flat monthly fee"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: White-label providers are commonly based in Europe (for advertising and offshore licensing) and in less regulated jurisdictions including the British Virgin Islands, Isle of Man, Costa Rica, Cyprus, Curaçao, Malta, Samoa and Vanuatu, and have been identified in Cambodia, Myanmar and the Philippines for physical operations such as large-scale live-dealer streaming. Some of the largest Southeast Asian online casino operators have moved into white-labelling, giving organized crime groups very high access to the regional industry for laundering and underground banking. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "White-label providers are commonly based in Europe for advertising and offshore licensing purposes"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Unlicensed and unregulated online casino operators are understood to rely almost exclusively on white-labelling, and those in or targeting jurisdictions where gambling is prohibited, particularly in Asia, often use white-labels to gain legitimacy through sub-licensing; this lets them appear and advertise as licensed, regulated entities despite not being subject to their white-label providers' oversight, and market as official betting partners of major European sports teams and leagues (e.g. English Premier League, International Basketball Federation). (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "unlicensed and unregulated online casino operators rely almost exclusively on whitelabelling for their operations"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: There have been several documented incidents of Southeast Asia-based white-label providers implicated in criminal cases relating to human trafficking, money laundering and cyberfraud involving triad networks and other organized crime groups in East and Southeast Asia and other jurisdictions. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "white-label service providers based in Southeast Asia being implicated in criminal cases relating to human trafficking"</sub>

## Rise of ‘points running’ syndicates, ‘motorcades’, and cryptocurrency

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Online casino operators constantly evolve ways to hide illicit fund flows; enhanced capital control enforcement in some East and Southeast Asian countries, plus early-COVID restrictions on cross-border movement of goods and people that limited smuggling of illicit cash into casinos, forced organized crime to innovate in how funds are transferred. (p. 62)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "ever-evolving efforts of operators to hide flows of illicit funds from authorities"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Beyond junket misuse by high-rollers, other common modalities for moving illicit funds through illegal online gambling platforms include underground ('white-glove') laundering and banking networks such as 'points running'/'score running' syndicates or 'water houses', money mule 'motorcade' teams, and third- and fourth-party payment providers (sometimes called running points platforms). (p. 62)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "so-called ‘points running’ or ‘score running’"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Footnote 140: fourth-party payments, an evolution of third-party payments that exploited transaction reporting gaps in apps such as WeChat Pay and AliPay, are among the most common modalities; after both platforms enhanced reporting of suspected gambling transactions at Chinese authorities' request, fourth-party providers typically insert an extra intermediary between bookmaker, bettor and the third-party payment app to circumvent the measures and obscure the transactions. (p. 62)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "fourth-party payment providers now typically install an additional intermediary between bookmaker, bettor"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: As of 2020 the Government of China estimated at least 5 million participants in this underground industry, totalling an estimated US $157 billion in capital outflows from China, and attributed it to the rise in illegal online gambling and telecommunications fraud. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "estimated at least 5 million participants in this underground industry"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Running points syndicates, sometimes called 'moving ants', transfer stolen money between multiple bank or crypto exchange accounts and online casinos to obscure its source and destination; the informal, often cross-border modality can involve hundreds or thousands of people, and is popular among underemployed youth who provide their bank accounts for account pass-through activity (collecting and transferring funds of unknown origin) for a commission. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to transfer stolen money between multiple bank or cryptocurrency exchange accounts as well as online casinos"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Running points systems are frequently used to collect money for crimes such as drug trafficking, cyberfraud and illegal gambling, and are largely used to facilitate illegal online gambling as another laundering layer: funds are routed through online gambling platforms and 'whitewashed' by cashing in and out to justify the source as casino winnings, commonly mixed with transaction miscoding and reliant on third- and fourth-party payment providers. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "subsequently ‘whitewashed’ by cashing in and cashing out through the platform"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The excerpt ends mid-sentence, noting that third- and fourth-party payments have become better understood and more widely reported following 'Operation Chain Break'. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "have become better understood by authorities and more widely reported following"</sub>

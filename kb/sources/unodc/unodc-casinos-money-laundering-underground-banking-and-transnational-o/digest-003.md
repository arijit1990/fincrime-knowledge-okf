## Report Development and Analysis

- **descriptive / context**: In 2019 UNODC published the threat assessment 'Transnational Organized Crime in Southeast Asia: Evolution, Growth, and Impact', describing the characteristics and evolution of organized crime and mechanics of different forms of trafficking over five years, and highlighting related crimes and vulnerabilities including drug and other trafficking and related money laundering in border areas that have attracted the development of casinos and SEZs. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "which described the characteristics and evolution of organized crime and the mechanics of different forms"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In November 2019 UNODC met ministers and senior officials from six Mekong countries (Cambodia, China, Lao PDR, Myanmar, Thailand and Viet Nam) under the Mekong MOU on Drug Control framework. Using a spin-off policy brief on casinos and money laundering as a basis for discussion, UNODC and the six governments agreed that a deeper understanding was needed of connections between organized crime, drug trafficking, money laundering, casinos and SEZs in the region. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "under the framework of the Mekong MOU on Drug Control"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Other partner countries in the region expressed support and acknowledged that a study on the relationship between the casino industry, money laundering, drug trafficking and transnational organized crime was a necessary first step to help countries understand the threats and to facilitate multilateral cooperation and strategies. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a study exploring the relationship between the casino industry, money laundering, drug trafficking, and transnational"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report says this was shown most clearly by the surge in online gambling and e-junket platforms, which fundamentally transformed underground banking, money laundering and the wider criminal business environment in Southeast Asia. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "surge in online gambling and e-junket platforms that fundamentally revolutionized underground banking, money laundering"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: UNODC initiated an internal assessment on casinos, money laundering and transnational organized crime in Southeast Asia and a separate internal analysis of illicit financial flows, using in-house analysts and international experts, with consultation of regional public security, law enforcement and criminal and financial intelligence agencies to analyse major threats, risks and red flags associated with the proliferation of casinos and associated laundering methods. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a separate internal analysis of illicit financial flows, with a mix of in house analysts and international experts"</sub>

It also reviewed criminal indictments, case records, financial intelligence, court filings and public disclosure, including investigation, indictment and court decision documents on the convictions of Macau SAR junket bosses Alvin Chau (Suncity) and Levo Chan (Tak Chun). (p. 26)[^original]

## Regional Overview

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Transnational organized crime groups in Southeast Asia, particularly the Mekong, are remarkably open, appearing in public and presenting as legitimate businesses while investing in sectors useful for illicit business, and their leaders have been highly effective at forming alliances with influential local figures to advance criminal activities. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Transnational organized crime groups in the region are remarkably open, appearing in public and presenting themselves as legitimate business entities"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Casinos and Special Economic Zones (SEZs), which proliferated to facilitate economic development, have inadvertently enabled organized crime to traffic illicit goods, operate illegal casinos and recently cyberfraud compounds, and facilitate industrial-scale money laundering and underground banking. The risks are of particular concern in the five lower Mekong countries, which have a long documented history of organized crime and illicit economies. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "have also inadvertently enabled organized crime groups to traffic illicit goods, operate illegal casinos and recently cyberfraud compounds"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 1: according to UNCTAD, SEZs are geographically delimited areas within which governments facilitate industrial activity through fiscal and regulatory incentives and infrastructure support. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "SEZs are defined as geographically delimited areas within which governments facilitate industrial activity through fiscal and regulatory incentives"</sub>

## Growing power of organized crime and the scale of illicit economies in Southeast Asia

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Organized crime groups in Southeast Asia have become increasingly sophisticated and agile. Illicit drug supply was initially disrupted by the COVID-19 pandemic but quickly rebounded, particularly synthetic drugs, aided by porous borders and growing availability of precursors and non-controlled chemicals. In parallel, regional underground banking and money laundering architecture evolved using the casino industry and online gaming innovations. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There was an initial disruption in the supply of illicit drugs following the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: So-called high-rollers increased activity in Southeast Asia after Macau SAR tightened controls over casino and junket businesses starting in 2014, and the industry accelerated its use of technology after COVID-19. Organized crime integrated information, financial and blockchain technology, with thousands of sophisticated online casinos and 'e-junkets' operating alongside high-tech white-label and underground banking service providers. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "following the tightening of controls over casino and junket businesses in Macau SAR starting in 2014"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Growing use of cryptocurrency and largely unregulated virtual asset service providers (VASPs) in parts of the region by actors in these underground systems has compounded challenges for law enforcement; many networks are deeply connected to online cyberfraud operations and trafficking for forced criminality. Proceeds of these crimes plus the synthetic drug trade generate billions of dollars, necessitating underground banking and laundering solutions capable of moving high volumes of state-backed fiat and cryptocurrencies undetected. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "many of these networks have been found to be deeply connected to online cyberfraud operations and trafficking for forced criminality"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: UNODC estimated by the start of 2022 more than 340 licensed and non-licensed casinos in the region alongside 45 casinos in Macau SAR. After COVID-19 numbers rose in some jurisdictions and fell in others: licensed casinos in Cambodia rose from 101 in 2021 to 174 in 2023 after an all-time high of 193 in 2019, while Macau SAR fell to just 30 by end 2022. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the number of licensed casinos in Cambodia increased from 101 in 2021 to 174 in 2023"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: The online casino industry expanded rapidly in reaction to COVID-19 restrictions, shifting online to reach clients remotely and develop new revenue streams. According to industry experts in the Philippines, gross gaming revenue (GGR) from online operators for 2023 is projected to exceed US $438 million, up from US $109.2 million in 2019 before the pandemic. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is projected to reach over US $438 million, up from US $109.2 million prior to the COVID-19 pandemic in 2019"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Criminal and financial intelligence officials say the traditional hierarchical structures of Asian crime syndicates have adapted into more modern enterprises, with collaboration among a growing number of crime groups, particularly on underground banking, money laundering and online crime, becoming the norm. Cases show these groups have infiltrated licit economies and elements of the formal financial system to diversify portfolios, launder money and cover criminal activities. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "have infiltrated licit economies and, concerningly, elements of the formal financial system"</sub>

## Overview of casino industries and SEZs in Southeast Asia

- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 3: white-label gambling websites are similar to franchises; betting operators can outsource every component of the business (betting technology, offshore licensing schemes, website design, customer data and management, branding and marketing, operating license) to third-party service providers. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "White-label gambling websites are similar to franchises"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Growth in licensed and unlicensed land-based and online casinos and junket operators has been driven by policy changes in parts of East Asia causing displacement, increased controls over Macau SAR junkets, a loose or non-existent regulatory environment in parts of Southeast Asia, and new technologies enabling illegal online casinos to proliferate. The Mekong displacement is helped by autonomous Special Regions in Myanmar lacking government control and rule of law, many SEZs, remote and porous borders, rapid digitization and illicit economies of scale. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a loose or altogether non-existent regulatory environment in certain parts of Southeast Asia"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Most casinos in lower Mekong countries are in border towns neighbouring China and Thailand, where most forms of gambling are illegal and patrons can easily travel. Casinos on Cambodia's eastern and western borders and on the Sihanoukville coast were set up to attract people from Thailand, Viet Nam and tourists from mainland China (Map 1; the map's text is scrambled and shows only country names and a casino marker). (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "most casinos in lower Mekong countries are located in border towns neighbouring China and Thailand"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Map 1 note: the number of casinos for each country is an estimate from various channels, actual numbers may differ and a few may have closed due to COVID-19, and unlicensed casinos are included (source: UNODC elaboration from Country Offices and field researchers). (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The number of casinos for each country is an estimate based on information received from various channels"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A unique feature of the regional casino industry is the large number of unauthorized online operators, particularly in Cambodia, Myanmar, the Philippines and increasingly Lao PDR. Limited access to SRs, SEZs and casino compounds prevents authorities determining the extent of these operations, but cases of dismantled illegal online gambling and cyberfraud operations, rescued trafficking victims, bulk cash and virtual asset seizures and arrests of known organized crime figures show the scale is massive. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A unique feature of the casino industry in the region is the large number of unauthorized online operators"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Footnote 8: although the Philippines developed a robust regulatory framework for online casino operators under the POGO licensing scheme, authorities note severe enforcement challenges and gaps. Several cases involved licensed POGO operators acting as 'master license holders' issuing 'sub-licenses' to other operators so they can present as licensed POGOs, while these entities are commonly licensed only as 'POGO service providers' with no authority to operate online casinos; multiple such entities were involved in large-scale cyberfraud, human trafficking, illegal online gambling and money laundering. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Law enforcement authorities in the Philippines have uncovered multiple entities using this scheme"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Footnote 9: online casino compounds in Southeast Asia have diversified and regularly include cyberfraud operations; licensed land-based and online casino operators often serve as a legitimate business front for organized crime groups engaged in cyberfraud, and as high cash volume businesses casinos are also used to justify the source of stolen funds. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Licensed land-based and online casino operators in the region often serve as a legitimate business front"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The number of SEZs in the Mekong has also risen markedly, driven by governments seeking growth in border and coastal areas attractive to investors. Several SEZs are in key areas close to drug and precursor production and trafficking, and high-profile criminals and organized crime syndicates are known to be involved in SEZs as owners, developers and investors. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "several SEZs are located in key areas close to drug and precursor production and trafficking in the Mekong region"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Governments, particularly in the Mekong, increasingly support border-area development via SEZs including casinos and 'smart city' SEZs; at the March 2018 6th Greater Mekong Subregion Summit several countries emphasized smart cities to improve economic connectivity and competitiveness through cross-border SEZs. 'Smart city' is undefined, and several countries approved or tacitly accepted early projects despite growing organized crime risk and started licensing cryptocurrency mining operations, exchanges and other VASPs which, with growing unlicensed underground ones, risk becoming fronts for illegal online betting, cyberfraud, money laundering and underground banking. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "While exactly what is meant by"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Best-known infiltration of organized crime into 'smart city' SEZs is in and around Myawaddy township, Karen State, Myanmar: the Shwe Kokko (Yatai New City), Huanya International City, Saixigang Industrial Zone and KK Park cyberfraud compounds. Targeting grey and illicit businesses hedging beyond Cambodia after joint Chinese-Cambodian law enforcement operations from 2017, the companies share documented links to criminal networks in casinos, junkets, cryptocurrencies and cyberfraud, attracted gambling operations relocating from Macau SAR, the Philippines and elsewhere, and became havens for organized crime and cyberfraud. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Among the best-known examples of infiltration of organized crime into"</sub>

## Convergence of organized crime and armed groups in Myanmar

- **descriptive / case example**: Smart city projects in armed group-controlled Karen State (Shwe Kokko, Huanya International City, Saixigang Industrial Zone, KK Park) house industrial-scale cyberfraud, engage in human trafficking and have clear links to Wan Kuok Koi (Broken Tooth) and She Zhijiang. In 2018 the Myanmar Investment Commission approved a US $22.5 million cash investment for Shwe Kokko in Myawaddy Township, planning 59 villas on about 25.5 acres within three years. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "approved an investment proposal of US $22.5 million in cash for the Shwe Kokko project in Myawaddy Township"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The developer Myanmar Yatai is a joint venture between Chit Lin Myaing Company (construction and mining, owned and managed by Colonel Saw Chit Thu, sanctioned under UK Global Human Rights Sanctions in December 2023), the Karen Border Guard Force which owns 20 per cent, and Yatai International Holding Group, owned by She Zhijiang, who was also designated under the UK sanctions. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "who was sanctioned under U.K. Global Human Rights Sanctions on December 2023"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Footnote 11: She Zhijiang is a Chinese-born naturalized Cambodian citizen wanted by Chinese authorities for illegal gambling charges since 2014, arrested by Thai authorities in 2022, with a business and investment portfolio across Southeast Asia (particularly Cambodia, Myanmar, Thailand and the Philippines) spanning real estate, construction, entertainment and blockchain technology. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "She Zhijiang is a Chinese-born naturalized Cambodian citizen who had been wanted by Chinese authorities"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Wan Kuok Koi, a longtime senior 14K triad member, is a key investor in and around Karen State; through his Hong Kong SAR-registered Dongmei Group he injected millions into the local casino industry and is believed to be a key player behind the proliferation of Myawaddy cyberfraud compounds, and is understood to be involved in related projects in Cambodia and elsewhere. In December 2020 he was designated by US Treasury OFAC as a triad leader and his companies as engaging in various criminal activities. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Wan injected millions of dollars into the local casino industry"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Myawaddy has also attracted other armed groups: the United Wa State Army (UWSA) through an agricultural conglomerate said to have opened an office at Shwe Kokko, and the Kokang BGF, which has heavily invested in Karen BGF-controlled areas, including a joint business in Myawaddy launched in 2018 between the two militias' business units. This includes casino investments around Laukkaing in SR 1 and appears to include large-scale underground banking linked to cyberfraud in Myawaddy and Sihanoukville, Cambodia. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Kokang BGF has heavily invested in the Karen BGF-controlled areas"</sub>

## Displacement and evolution of cross-border gambling

- **descriptive / finding or result**: A major driver of initial displacement of cross-border gambling out of Macau SAR was stronger Chinese government emphasis on enforcing currency transfer quotas and controls. Previously individuals commonly transferred large sums by pooling quotas of relatives, friends and employees; China now prohibits anyone from transferring money on behalf of someone else. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A major driver behind the initial displacement of cross-border gambling out of Macau SAR"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Footnote 16: several Asian countries restrict the amount of fiat currency leaving their jurisdiction and enforce transfer quotas. After an anti-corruption and money laundering campaign beginning in 2014 China set a limit of approximately US $50,000 per individual per year for transfers to Hong Kong SAR, Macau SAR, Taiwan PoC or other foreign destinations; this is not new but China has placed greater emphasis on enforcement, and before 2017 pooling of quotas was not uncommon. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "China set a limit of approximately US $50,000 per individual which could be transferred out from the country"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: New currency policies slowed rapid outflows but many innovative workarounds remain, often involving Chinese companies doing business overseas. Methods to evade currency controls are many and sophisticated and can be facilitated by underground banks and exchanges, cross-border gambling and tourism groups, junket operators and increasingly online casinos and e-junket operators. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "many innovative workarounds still exist, often involving Chinese companies doing business overseas"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Government of China has formally expressed the view that cross-border gambling groups have fundamental characteristics of organized crime, with highly organized structures and open crime systems whose main purpose is procuring economic interests. Chinese public security officials note rising use of cryptocurrencies, the dark web and other technologies by organized crime, and concern over rampant cross-border and online gambling in Southeast Asia linked to drug trafficking, money laundering, kidnapping and unlawful detention. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "cross-border gambling groups have fundamental characteristics of organized crime, featuring highly organized structures"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Underground banking has become more widespread and sophisticated, in part due to regulations and controls meant to reduce cross-border gambling. According to industry estimates, cross-border illegal gambling led to at least US $145 billion of illegal capital outflows from China alone through offshore gambling websites and casinos in 2020, much understood to be facilitated by casino junkets and sophisticated offsetting arrangements, and offshore online casinos using points running syndicates, money mule motorcades and third- and fourth-party payment providers. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "at least US $145 billion of illegal capital outflows from China alone"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: As of 2020 the Government of China estimated at least 5 million participants in these underground industries. In response China initiated 'Operation Chain Break' in 2019 and intensified action against online casinos; its efforts to disrupt capital outflows from mainland China and Macau SAR to Southeast Asia included an August 2020 'blacklist' of overseas casino destinations that also applies to junket operators, private jet travel operators and water charters and is understood to include travel to Southeast Asian gambling hubs. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The list also applies to junket operators, private jet travel operators, and water charters"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 20 explains that junket-based offsetting arrangements, also called mirror transactions, are a means of junket financing: the gambler puts money into a junket account, or stakes local assets, in one jurisdiction and may then draw on this credit, less a fee, in another. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "junket-based offsetting arrangements, also referred to as mirror transactions, are ultimately a means of junket financing"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Footnote 20 adds that offsetting transfers value between jurisdictions through a junket-gambler credit and debit relationship between entities in different countries; the organization facilitating it arranges for money debited from an entity in one jurisdiction to be credited to an entity (sometimes the same one) in a second, so the facilitator needs fund access in both. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Organizations facilitating offsetting arrange for money debited from an entity in one jurisdiction to be credited to"</sub>

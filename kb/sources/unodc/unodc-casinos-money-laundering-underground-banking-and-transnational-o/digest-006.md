## Junket-based money laundering and underground banking methods

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Offsetting arrangements, first developed for cross-border gambling in Macau SAR, have evolved into an underground banking and laundering solution used by organized crime; these transactions occur outside the formal banking sector and are high risk for casino junkets because they circumvent international funds transfer reporting requirements and facilitate laundering of domestically generated proceeds of crime. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they circumvent international funds transfer reporting requirements and facilitate the laundering of domestically generated proceeds of crime"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: In junket offsetting, a high-roller deposits money into a junket account, or stakes using junket-issued credit or other assets, in one country and is issued a corresponding amount of value in another; though funds are theoretically moved for gambling, misuse lets organized crime move vast amounts of cash and increasingly cryptocurrency while bypassing the formal financial system. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a gambler (high-roller) deposits money into a junket account or stakes using credit issued by the junket"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Figure 7 (source: AMLC of the Philippines, 2023) charts the value of junket-related STRs in PHP millions by year of submission, 2016-2023, on an axis up to 12,000; no per-year values are legible. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Figure 7� Value of STRs related to junkets in the Philippines, 2016-2023"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: In regional cases, funds in junket-representative-controlled casino accounts are commonly offset against money in overseas accounts partly derived from onshore 'cash collectors' who move money from domestic organized crime groups to junket operator casino accounts; proceeds can include drug trafficking, cybercrime, fraud, scams and human trafficking. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "individuals known as ‘cash collectors’ who move money from domestic organized crime groups to junket operator casino accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Authorities have reported a more complex and less prevalent offsetting method in which funds for a player's junket activity are raised by directing the player to purchase shares in a foreign company rather than depositing money into an offshore bank account. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "directing the player to purchase shares in a foreign company"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Several industry-wide deficiencies in casino and junket management and supervision uncovered in the region have exacerbated the situation and the level of organized crime infiltration; the report lists them in the following points. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "several industry-wide deficiencies in casino and junket management and supervision"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Deficiency 1, multi-party transactions and cash deposits: loosely regulated junket accounts can receive large cash deposits from tens to hundreds of individuals in a reporting period, with only some depositors registered with the junket (people who are neither operator nor agent), violating most casinos' practice of letting only junket operators or agents transact on junket accounts. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "loosely regulated junket accounts can receive large cash deposits from a multitude (tens to hundreds) of individuals"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Allowing too many agents to transact on a junket's accounts may weaken control over transactors, leaving authorities with a limited view of gambling account behaviour and banks unable to isolate junket-related transactions from other casino transactions, which creates transaction monitoring and analysis challenges. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "too many agents allowed to transact on a junket’s accounts may diminish effective control over transactors"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Deficiency 2, misuse of VIP cash accounts: casino operators across the region often open bank accounts under their own business for junket partners, which have been observed to help transnational organized crime store and move significant sums domestically and internationally; the junket sector is also exposed to high-value cash risks per transaction and per customer, and the destination of large cash withdrawals from junket accounts is a key intelligence gap across East and Southeast Asia and the Pacific. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "casino operators across the region often open bank accounts under their business for their junket partners"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Footnote 92 refers readers to the case studies on Alvin Chau of Suncity Junket and Levo Chan of Tak Chun Junket for misuse of VIP cash accounts. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "See below case studies on Alvin Chau of Suncity Junket and Levo Chan of Tak Chun Junket"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Deficiency 3, treating junket operators as individuals rather than corporate entities: casino operators often treat junkets as individuals although some operate within large international businesses; this grey area leads to uneven due diligence and greater vulnerability in understanding beneficial ownership and control, and diversification of junket operations into other business lines raises commingling opportunities and makes it harder for banks to understand the origin of funds. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "However, it is clear that some junkets operate as part of large international businesses"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Deficiency 4, multiplier betting: a form of 'under-the-table' gambling in which a bet denominated at the casino tables is only a fraction of the total private bet between gamblers and junket operators, to avoid gaming revenue levies; it lets clients pre-negotiate payment method, betting currency and cash-out method, raises junket promoter commissions, and can conceal the total money an individual bettor moves through the casino or junket and obfuscate source and destination of funds. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "only represents a fraction of the total amount of a private bet made between gamblers and junket operators"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Figure 8 of the UNODC report is titled as a simplified informal money transfer model via a casino junket offsetting arrangement. Its labels (garbled in the extracted text, so the assignment of jurisdictions to steps cannot be reliably established) refer to transaction value collected from a junket client by a junket operator, agent or debt collector into a junket operator account, credit issued and funds disbursed to a 'VIP' gambler, transaction value debited (offset) from a client, and circumvention of the formal banking/financial system. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Simplified informal money transfer model via casino junket offsetting arrangement"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Multiplier betting is understood to have grown in popularity because most junket customers in Macau SAR come from mainland China, where strict capital controls and a nationwide gambling ban mean they do not and cannot bring money to play and rely on credit issued by junket agents; for example, for a HK $1 million credit request the agent can ask the casino for HK $100,000 of chips, with a ten times multiplier understood between agent and customer. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the junket agent can request the casino to provide HK $100,000 worth of chips"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Deficiency 5, unlicenced money service businesses (MSBs): in several markets, unlicenced money transfer businesses lending gambling patrons (often from East and Southeast Asia) funds to gamble play a key facilitation role in criminal cases; the funds' origin is often unknown and potentially criminal, the gambler repays within another jurisdiction in a domestic transaction, which circumvents strict currency controls, gives access to capital to gamble in other countries and lets the operator convert cash in one jurisdiction into a bank deposit in another. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "use of unlicenced money transfer businesses lending gambling patrons"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Deficiency 6, growing cryptocurrency use by major online casino and junket operators: no casino regulator in Southeast Asia authorizes cryptocurrency use by online casino and junket operators, yet UNODC has observed widespread advertising of cryptocurrency exchange and payment services by licensed and unlicensed operators across the region, including a Philippines licensed junket operator advertising Tether in a daily exchange rate table in August 2023. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is not authorized by any casino regulators in Southeast Asia"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report concludes these conditions have produced a complex, vulnerable casino junket lifecycle easily exploited by transnational organized crime and by junkets facilitating offsetting arrangements (Figure 9). (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a complex and vulnerable casino junket lifecycle easily exploited by transnational organized crime"</sub>

## The ‘Vancouver Model’

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The 'Vancouver model' for money laundering and underground banking is among the best-documented misuses of casinos and junkets; it was uncovered through Canada's Cullen Commission inquiry, and similar methods have been identified in Australia, Macau SAR, Cambodia, Lao PDR, Myanmar and the Philippines. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "what has been coined by law enforcement and industry experts as the ‘Vancouver model’"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: According to the inquiry the model was devised by Asian transnational organized crime groups even though junkets were not formally permitted in Canada, and laundered millions of dollars through British Columbia casinos between 2008 and 2018, including proceeds of drug cartels based in Mexico and South America; surveillance footage showed bags of cash brought in, converted to chips, played briefly and cashed out as clean money (sometimes called short play). (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "successfully laundered millions of dollars through casinos in British Columbia between 2008 and 2018"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The Vancouver model combines elements of traditional hawala laundering with cash-in, cash-out strategies. Steps 1-3: a person in Jurisdiction X, who may or may not be involved in crime, tries to move substantial funds out and may circumvent cash export control laws; the person transfers funds to a criminal group in X and travels to Jurisdiction Y; the gang in X has associates in Y give an equivalent amount, mostly criminally derived cash needing laundering, to the person now in Y. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Vancouver model combines elements of traditional hawala money laundering methods and mixes them with cash-in, cash-out strategies"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Steps 4-5 of the Vancouver model: the person enters the casino, converts the money into chips, makes several low value bets and cashes out, trading chips for clean cash or cash checks; this launders funds for the criminal group through the original transfer and also bypasses the cash exit control laws in Jurisdiction X for the person's own benefit. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The person enters the casino, converts the money into chips, then makes several low value bets and cashes out"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Junket representatives (agents) work for junkets for commissions; some open accounts with junket operators and store large cash amounts that can be advanced to gamblers. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "who work on behalf of junkets in return for commissions"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In December 2020 the People's Procuratorate of Jiangsu Province, China, indicted five people for running casino rooms in China while acting as representatives for major junkets; they opened accounts with Suncity, Tak Chun and others from which credits were advanced to gamblers, and ran casino rooms with live-streamed screens of Tak Chun's Manila VIP room gambling tables; at least RMB 170 million (about US $27 million) had been transacted through them since 2016. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "indicted five people for running casino rooms in China while acting as representatives for major junkets"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Law enforcement reports extensive misuse of the Suncity VIP room network and multiple land-based casinos, including Golden Triangle casinos, for laundering and underground banking by organized crime; financial intelligence analysis confirms their use to pay drug traffickers and arrange production of synthetic and other drugs in and around the Golden Triangle. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "extensive misuse of the Suncity VIP room network together with multiple land-based casinos"</sub>

## Proliferation of online casinos and their misuse for money laundering and underground banking

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The rapid growth of the 'offshore' online casino industry (including sports betting) in high-risk Southeast Asian jurisdictions, particularly the Mekong, is a growing concern; Macau SAR junket operators and criminal associates drove it, accelerated by enhanced enforcement and regulatory pressure in mainland China and COVID-19 mobility restrictions that curbed VIP gambler travel. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Macau SAR junket operators and their close criminal associates have been key drivers behind this trend"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Unregulated and under-regulated online casinos run by junket operators (e-junkets) were highly profitable and also a channel for credit settlement between junkets and clients, able to disguise organized crime proceeds as legitimate online betting profits; most if not all of the largest junket operators had such operations, with smaller junkets acting as customer referral agents. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "online casinos run by junket operators (sometimes referred to as e-junkets), while themselves highly profitable"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Mirror websites, cryptocurrency and third-party betting software or 'white-label' providers have made it easier than ever to set up an online casino with limited technical expertise and capital, irrespective of local gambling laws; growth in third-party payment providers, e-wallets and other digital payment solutions has also supported the sector. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "never been easier to set up an online casino operation with limited technical expertise and overhead capital"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The industry's scale has drawn an unprecedented number of young employees into the sector, creating opportunities for some and risks for others given the surge in related recruitment fraud and human trafficking. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "posing opportunities for some and risks for others given the surge in related recruitment fraud and human trafficking"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 99: most online gambling platforms in Southeast Asia use 'mirror websites', exact replicas of primary betting sites under different, often algorithmically generated URLs, used to evade regulators; hundreds can run on one server so service continues if a URL is shut down. Footnote 100: white-label gambling websites work like franchises, letting operators outsource betting technology, offshore licensing schemes, website design, customer data and management, branding and marketing, and operating licence to third-party providers. Footnote 101: 'social gaming' means online video games accessed mainly through social media platforms, where users share the experience with their network. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "exact replicas of primary betting websites housed under different URLs"</sub>

## The ‘Vancouver Model’

- **expected / finding or result**: The phenomenon has occurred in countries with robust regulatory capacity such as Australia and Canada, while many of the world's largest junket operators remain active in casinos in higher-risk Southeast Asian jurisdictions. Given the relatively minimal regulation and enforcement against money laundering in the region, the report says the availability of junket operators should be seen as a serious money laundering risk likely to be accompanied by other criminal activity. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the availability of junket operators should be seen as a serious money laundering risk"</sub>

## Proliferation of online casinos and their misuse for money laundering and underground banking

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Faster internet and technology have enabled more sophisticated online games including live-dealer casino streaming that replicates the land-based experience; mobile apps have made online betting and casinos available to mass markets, and social gaming, 'crypto casinos' and NFT play-to-earn games are changing the industry and its money laundering risks. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "live-dealer casino streaming which can essentially replicate the same experience for a gambler as land-based casinos"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The formal online gambling market is projected to exceed US $205 billion by 2030 (Polaris), with Asia Pacific projected to account for 37 per cent of market growth between 2022 and 2026 (Technavio). (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "more than US $205 billion by 2030"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Philippines has 34 licensed offshore operators (POGOs), each allowed to run up to 10 online gaming platforms and sub-license 10 service providers; industry experts project 2023 gross gaming revenue above US $438 million, up from US $109.2 million in 2019 before COVID-19 and more than double 2022. Projections exclude illegal unlicensed operators, and offshore online casinos are also prevalent in Cambodia, Lao PDR and Myanmar where little data exists because there are no formal, functioning, enforced regulatory frameworks. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there are currently 34 licensed offshore operators (POGOs)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Online gambling growth in Southeast Asia is part of a changing casino and junket model that has pivoted from luxury gambling tourism for high-rollers toward the premium mass market online; law enforcement action shows online casinos, like land-based ones, can be integral to regional underground banking and laundering infrastructure and provide more channels to commingle recreational gambling flows with proceeds of crime. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "steadily pivoted away from identifying high-rollers and facilitating their luxury gambling tourism"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In 2022, after a surge in murder, kidnapping, money laundering and organized crime influence, Philippine authorities ordered 175 offshore gambling firms shut and about 40,000 foreign workers deported. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "ordered the shutdown of 175 offshore gambling firms and deportation of approximately 40,000 foreign workers"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In 2022 Thailand arrested 461 people for illegal online gambling websites and blocked more than 6,000 URLs; Thai authorities noted illegal gambling flows totalled billions of dollars in the first half of 2023, and one of the largest networks under investigation generated illegal revenues above US $500 million per month. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a total of 461 individuals were arrested in Thailand for involvement in illegal online gambling websites"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Chinese authorities reported more than 37,000 cross-border and online gambling cases and a nationwide crackdown on 2,600 online gambling platforms, more than 1,100 casinos, over 2,500 illegal payment platforms and underground banks, 1,200 technical support teams and 1,600 platforms promoting illegal gambling. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "more than 37,000 cases involving cross-border and online gambling"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Figure 11 (source: Polaris Market Research Analysis, 2023) shows online gambling market size by region 2018-2030 in US $ billion, with growth contributed by APAC of 37 per cent and growth for 2022 of 11.82 per cent; other values are not legible. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Figure 11� Online gambling market size by region 2018-2030 (US $ billion)"</sub>

## Associated challenges and risks

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Millions of new online betting customers emerged after COVID-19 despite stronger enforcement, widening the customer base for licensed and unlicensed operators in Cambodia, Lao PDR, Myanmar, the Philippines and Viet Nam, boosting illegal betting revenues and creating new outlets for laundering and underground banking. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "millions of new online betting customers have emerged following the COVID-19 pandemic"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Because the Asia-facing junket industry has longstanding links to Macau SAR-based triad groups, continued geographic diversification by junkets, the e-junket model and dispersion of online gambling across the Mekong risk giving organized crime and partners huge new revenue streams and fuelling consolidation and expansion of syndicates already in the region. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "risks providing huge new revenue streams for organized crime and their partners"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Illegal online betting operations function like organized crime syndicates and share the structure of traditional illegal street bookmakers; operators in East and Southeast Asia have emulated it, with a greater volume of smaller regional online operators acting as mid-level agents for the biggest grey market Asian bookmakers. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "illegal online betting operations function similarly to most organized crime syndicates"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: In the traditional pyramid, syndicate organizers issue credit down a chain of ranked agents and middlemen to brokers at the bottom, who acquire customers by assessing creditworthiness and taking bets; bets are passed upward to hedge liability and aggregate risk, each level taking a commission toward the organizer. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "illegal betting syndicate organizers issue credit down the chain through a roster of ranked agents and middlemen"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: In online markets customers increasingly bet directly on illegal websites; super and master-agents often run their own online sites, buying syndicate 'materials' with premade web design, content and a registered domain, and the network of regional and national sites hedges liability between themselves and with larger sites above. The biggest grey-market sites accept billions of dollars in bets annually, with liquidity so high that major global sports betting sites hedge with them. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "customers can increasingly place bets directly on the illegal websites by themselves"</sub>

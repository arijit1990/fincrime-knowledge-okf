## Proliferation of USDT-based money laundering networks

- **descriptive / context**: USDT (Tether) is a stablecoin pegged to and backed by fiat currencies like the US dollar, reported to have the most liquid markets with about US $20 billion in estimated daily trading volume at the time of writing; USDT on the TRON blockchain is preferred by regional cyberfraud operations and launderers for its stability, ease, anonymity and low fees. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "reported as having the most liquid markets with an estimated US $20 billion daily trading volume"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A Bitrace fund audit of USDT-based transactions between September 2022 and September 2023 found transactions totalling 17.07 billion USDT connected to underground currency exchanges, illegal commodity trades, unlawful collection and payment processes and various criminal activities. East and Southeast Asian authorities also report USDT among the cryptocurrencies most used by organized crime, shown by surging cyberfraud, laundering and underground banking cases. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "transactions totaling 17.07 billion USDT connected to underground currency exchanges"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnotes 154-155: as of June 2023 TRON had over 165.5 million user accounts, over 5.81 billion transactions and over US $11.79 billion total value locked, hosting the largest circulating supply of USDT globally since April 2021; Tether issues tokens on Bitcoin (Omni and Liquid), Ethereum, EOS, Tron, Algorand, SLP and OMG Network, making conversion easy. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "TRON had over 165.5 million total user accounts, more than 5.81 billion total transactions"</sub>

## Operation Chain Break

- **descriptive / case example**: To combat estimated annual criminal proceeds outflows of RMB 1 trillion (US $157 billion) from China tied to illegal cross-border gambling and related transfer schemes involving an estimated five to six million participants, China launched a three-year campaign, 'Operation Chain Break', in 2019; it was broader than any earlier anti-illegal betting crackdown and involved agencies including the PBOC, Central Cyberspace Office and the Ministries of Public Security, Tourism and Culture, and Finance, with local authorities in countries including Cambodia and the Philippines and Macau SAR, intensifying pressure on offshore casinos and junkets. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "launched a three-year campaign, codenamed ‘Operation Chain Break’"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In 2019 Chinese authorities set up a 'blacklist' system to identify, designate and investigate high-risk cross-border gambling tourist destinations including specific casinos, junkets, underground banks and especially online betting websites. The PBOC cited methods used by organized crime for illegal cross-border gambling: running points syndicates, misuse of third and fourth-party payment platforms, licensed and unlicensed remittance providers, and cryptocurrency. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "established a so-called ‘blacklist’ system to identify, designate, and investigate high-risk cross-border gambling tourist destinations"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In October 2020 the PBOC's Huizhou City branch dismantled a USDT-based cross-border online gambling operation, arresting 77 suspects, shutting down three online gambling platforms and recovering RMB 120 million (US $18.8 million). (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "arresting 77 suspects, shutting down three online gambling platforms, and recovering RMB 120 million"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In April 2020 Zhuhai Public Security Bureau (Guangdong) uncovered an illegal online gambling business run by a technology company posing as a software developer; it was a white-label provider giving technical support and maintenance to at least 45 overseas Southeast Asian operators, including in Thailand and Cambodia, and collected over RMB 45 million (US $7 million) in service fees. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "offering technical support and maintenance to at least 45 overseas operators in Southeast Asia"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: That case was one of several in a broader investigation in which four suspected organizers were arrested in Macau SAR in December 2020; they had tried to cover up a sophisticated online casino network, including sites impersonating licensed Macau gaming operators, by masquerading as several technology and advertising companies in Macau SAR and Zhuhai. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "authorities ultimately arrested four suspected organizers in Macau SAR in December 2020"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In 2022 Chinese police announced dismantling of a US $1.7 billion USDT-based laundering network coordinated by the Horqin branch of Tongliao Public Security Bureau: over 200 officers across 17 provinces and municipalities arrested 63 suspects and seized US $18 million in cash over a three-month operation; the syndicate used USDT to launder proceeds of illegal gambling and other crimes for domestic and foreign groups, had a clear division of labor, and recruited individuals to register accounts with crypto exchanges through Telegram for pass-through activities; two ringleaders fled to Bangkok but returned to China. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "dismantlement of a US $1.7 billion USDT-based money laundering network"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Between December 2021 and July 2022 Chinese authorities dismantled a separate cryptocurrency-based 'points running' network that laundered a total of US $5.6 billion for groups involved in telecommunication scams and illegal gambling; police arrested 93 suspects across Hainan, Guangdong, Fujian and Jiangxi (printed 'Gianxi'). (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "laundered a total of US $5.6 billion for criminal groups involved in telecommunication scams and illegal gambling"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In November 2023 a US Department of Justice investigation with exchange OKX and Tether led to voluntary freezing of US $225 million in USDT tied to a Southeast Asia-based human trafficking and pig butchering cyberfraud ring; DOJ also seized nearly US $9 million in USDT, tracing victim deposits laundered through chain-hopping across several cryptocurrencies. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "voluntary freezing of US $225 million in USDT connected to a Southeast Asia-based human trafficking"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnotes 169 and 171: the pig butchering scam lures victims into digital relationships to build trust before persuading them to invest in cryptocurrency on fraudulent platforms; chain-hopping, one of the fastest-growing laundering typologies, moves virtual assets from one blockchain to another, often rapidly, to impede tracing and make detection by exchanges harder. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "involving the transfer of virtual assets from one blockchain to another, often in rapid succession"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In August 2023 Singapore announced its largest ever money laundering investigation, arresting 10 foreign nationals suspected of laundering proceeds of overseas organized crime including online gambling and telecommunication scams; more than 400 officers conducted raids after analysing suspicious transaction reports and seized US $737 million in cash, USDT, real estate, luxury cars and other assets including over 250 luxury handbags, jewelry, watches, electronic devices and virtual asset seed phrases. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "culminating in the arrest of 10 foreign nationals suspected of laundering the proceeds of overseas organized crime activities"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 174: a seed phrase is a series of 12 or 24 random words giving the data needed to recover a lost or broken crypto wallet, also called a mnemonic phrase, a security measure for self-custodied digital assets. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A seed phrase is a series of 12 or 24 random words"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In the Singapore case the suspects held passports from countries including China, Turkey, Cyprus, Cambodia and Vanuatu and had forged documents to substantiate the source of funds in Singapore bank accounts; nine of the ten appear to have been naturalized Cambodian citizens per Royal Decree records, several previously investigated in China for related criminal activities. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "had forged documents in order to substantiate the source of funds in Singapore bank accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In the week after the Singapore operation, authorities brought additional charges against three of the ten suspects relating to possession of criminal benefits from an unlicensed moneylending business in China and illegal online gambling operations in the Philippines, while at least one suspect is alleged to have operated illegal gambling websites in Myanmar using USDT. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "possession of criminal benefits from an unlicensed moneylending business in China"</sub>

## Convergence between online gambling, cyberfraud, and human trafficking

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Organized crime groups have built extensive cyberfraud operations in Southeast Asia, with an estimated more than 220,000 people held in forced cyberfraud and scam work in Cambodia and Myanmar alone. Together with enhanced pressure on the online casino industry, post-COVID mobility restrictions forced criminal groups, having invested hundreds of billions of dollars in the region's casino industry, to innovate, digitize and diversify. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an estimated more than 220,000 people being held in situations where they are forced to carry out various cyberfraud"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Land-based and online operators moved bases into loosely regulated jurisdictions including Cambodia, Lao PDR, the Philippines and armed-group-controlled Myanmar border areas, expanding into cyberfraud; others moved outside Asia to Dubai, Africa, Eastern Europe and the Pacific and tried to legitimize operations by investing in offshore licensed but underregulated gambling companies. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "have moved their bases of operation into loosely regulated and highly vulnerable jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: These groups use the casino industry, SEZ infrastructure, IT and web design advances and rising youth unemployment as a legitimizing cover; they lure labourers into trafficking with promises of lucrative jobs and professionalized recruitment, often via Telegram, WeChat, TikTok and Facebook, while targeting cyberfraud victims with data bought and sold on dark and clear web platforms and luring them into professionally designed fraudulent investment platforms and increasingly fraudulent international banking applications. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "They lure labourers into trafficking using promise of lucrative employment and increasingly professionalized recruitment schemes"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: There are growing signs of influence over local officials and infiltration of key formal-economy sectors including money and virtual asset service providers, property developers, dealers in precious metals and stones, legal professionals and accountants, and trust and company service providers. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "growing signs of influence over local officials, as well as infiltration of key areas of the formal economy"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Cyberfraud's growth has required parallel development of underground banking and laundering capabilities, with mounting evidence pointing to casinos; the relationship between these illicit industries and others such as the synthetic drug trade is described as symbiotic. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "have required parallel developments in underground banking and money laundering capabilities"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In one Mekong country, estimates suggest cyberfraud yields about US $300 to $400 per day per worker across roughly 500,000 workers, though other sources estimate 80,000 to 100,000; even the lower estimates imply US $7.5 to $12.5 billion in raw earnings (assuming a 6-day week), against the country's GDP of just under US $27 billion in 2021. (p. 69)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "approximately US $300 to $400 per day per worker, through approximately 500,000 workers"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Tens of thousands of cyberfraud labourers have been identified or rescued in Lao PDR, Myanmar and the Philippines after government appeals and crackdowns; Indonesia reported repatriating over 1,100 nationals trafficked into cyberfraud companies in Cambodia in 2023, though data for broader regional estimates remains limited. (p. 69)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "tens of thousands of cyberfraud labourers have also been identified and/or rescued"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In 2022 the FBI announced reported victim losses of US $3.3 billion from pig butchering, more than double 2021, with most unsealed and geographically attributed cases linking back to Mekong-based cyberfraud operations. (p. 69)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "victims had reported totaling US $3.3 billion from pig butchering"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Philippine authorities raided cyberfraud operations embedded in licensed offshore gambling companies; victims in three large, highly securitized compounds were largely lured into the country illegally and forced to work 16 to 18 hours per day, often without food and with pay withheld for missing targets, doing online game manipulation, investment scams and fraud, and romantic relationships with targets in Canada, the United States, Europe and Asia. (p. 69)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "forced to work between 16 to 18 hours per day, often lacking food, and with pay withheld"</sub>

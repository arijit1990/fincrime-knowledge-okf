- **descriptive / context**: The October 2020 Conceptual Framework for the Statistical Measurement of Illicit Financial Flows was produced by UNCTAD (its Development Statistics and Information Branch, in consultation with the Debt and Development Finance Branch) and UNODC (its Research and Trends Analysis Branch). The UNCTAD-UNODC Task Force on statistical measurement of illicit financial flows endorsed it as a foundation for further work. (p. 1)[^original]

## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

- **descriptive / context**: The 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development identifies reducing illicit financial flows (IFFs) as a priority for building peaceful societies; SDG target 16.4 reads: by 2030, significantly reduce illicit financial and arms flows, strengthen recovery and return of stolen assets and combat all forms of organized crime. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "By 2030, significantly reduce illicit financial and arms flows, strengthen the recovery and return of stolen assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In July 2017 the UN General Assembly adopted the SDG indicator framework, with indicator 16.4.1, 'Total value of inward and outward illicit financial flows', chosen to measure progress on target 16.4; at the time there was no universal agreement on the scope of IFFs or how components could be measured. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there was no universal agreement on what should be included within the scope of IFFs"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Because IFFs are understood differently and many are deliberately hidden, measurement is extremely difficult; the indicator has two custodians, UNODC (crime-related IFFs) and UNCTAD (methods for IFFs related to taxes and trade). (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "UNODC, leading the work on crime-related IFFs, and UNCTAD, leading the development of methods to measure IFFs"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: From 2017 to 2018 UNODC and UNCTAD held expert consultations and, on their recommendation, set up a statistical Task Force to develop methodologies for measuring IFFs, producing a conceptual framework, statistical definitions, typology and methodologies. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "UNODC and UNCTAD held expert consultations and, on their recommendation, established a statistical Task Force"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The approach identifies main IFF types to measure and lays out a framework based on existing statistical definitions, classifications and methods in line with the System of National Accounts (SNA) and the balance of payments, proposing a disaggregated, bottom-up measurement approach following international efforts to measure non-observed or illegal economic activities. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A disaggregated and bottom-up measurement approach is proposed in line with these frameworks"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: IFFs cover activities considered criminal offences and also some behaviours related to tax and commercial practices; the International Classification of Crime for Statistical Purposes (ICCS) provides definitions of the illegal activities generating IFFs. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Illicit financial flows refer to activities considered as criminal offences, but also some behaviours related to tax and commercial practices"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: For each type, IFFs can generally emerge at two stages. Illicit income generation covers cross-border transactions performed in the context of producing illicit goods and services or generating illicit income for an actor during a non-productive illicit activity. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Illicit income generation, which includes the set of cross-border transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The proposed framework groups the activities that can generate IFFs into four main types: tax and commercial activities; illegal markets; corruption; and exploitation-type activities together with the financing of crime and terrorism. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identifies four main types of activities that can generate IFFs"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Illicit income management refers to cross-border transactions that use illicit income to invest in financial and non-financial assets or to consume goods and services. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Illicit income management, which refers to cross-border transactions that use illicit income to invest"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: IFFs are multi-dimensional: flows originating from illicit activities, illicit transactions transferring funds of licit origin, and flows from licit activity used illicitly. Not all IFFs stem from illegal activity; for example the indicator includes aggressive tax avoidance as an IFF because it can harm sustainable development in many countries even though generally not illegal. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Not all IFFs stem from illegal activity"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In October 2019 the 10th session of the IAEG-SDGs in Addis Ababa reviewed the methodological proposal and reclassified indicator 16.4.1 from Tier III to Tier II, meaning it is conceptually clear with internationally established standards though countries do not yet regularly produce data. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "reclassified the indicator from Tier III to Tier II"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The framework and measurement methods are being tested in ongoing and new pilots; methods for selected illegal markets were tested in Latin America and at subregional level for selected activities (wildlife goods and drug trafficking), and preliminary findings show feasibility of the developed approaches. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Preliminary findings show the feasibility of the developed measurement approaches"</sub>

## INTRODUCTION TO ILLICIT FINANCIAL FLOWS

- **descriptive / context**: Organized crime, trade in illegal goods (such as drug and firearms trafficking) and illegal or illicit tax and commercial practices generate billions of dollars in illicit flows each year; proceeds may be channelled abroad, often to safe havens, or laundered and reinvested in legal markets. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Proceeds of crime may be channelled abroad, often to safe havens"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Large-scale organized crime weakens state institutions by fuelling corruption and violence, undermines the rule of law, discourages public and private investment and deprives the licit economy of resources for sustainable development; IFFs from crime and corruption erode criminal justice systems, reduce state revenues, erode the tax base and weaken institutions. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "weakens state institutions by fuelling corruption and violence, and undermines the rule of law"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: IFFs originating in the legal economy may also divert development resources, erode the tax base and hamper structural transformation and sustainable growth; the 2030 Agenda stresses mobilizing more financial resources, which IFFs undermine, and the Addis Ababa Action Agenda calls for redoubled efforts to substantially reduce IFFs with a view to eventually eliminating them. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "calls for a redoubling of efforts to substantially reduce IFFs, with a view to eventually eliminating them"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Progress on target 16.4 is measured by SDG indicator 16.4.1, 'Total value of inward and outward illicit financial flows (in current United States dollars)', which focuses on cross-country flows to assess the size of IFFs entering or leaving a country. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This indicator is focused on crosscountry flows to assess the size of illicit financial flows entering or leaving a country"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In 2017, when the SDG indicator framework was adopted, UNCTAD and UNODC, co-custodians of indicator 16.4.1, began methodological work and broad consultations on statistical definitions and methods to measure IFFs. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "started methodological work and broad consultations on the development of statistical definitions and methods to measure IFFs"</sub>

## STATISTICAL CHALLENGES

- **descriptive / limitation**: Lack of consistent IFF statistics causes uncertainty about size and trends, origins and development impact; IFFs differ markedly across countries and regions, and the lack of a common framework makes their measurement impossible. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the lack of a common framework makes their measurement impossible"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The literature shows differing understandings of what IFFs constitute and how to measure them, from estimates of laundering through the banking system to macroeconomic mirror trade statistics comparisons; the absence of a common understanding hampers national policy action and agreeing common international policy goals. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The absence of a common understanding hampers policy action to combat IFFs"</sub>

## Conceptual challenges

- **descriptive / limitation**: Some quantitative studies equate IFFs with trade misinvoicing only, although trade-based money laundering is not the only method of moving money illicitly, and the proposed measures suffer from simplifying assumptions about data sources and methods. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "some quantitative studies equate IFFs with trade misinvoicing only"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: By definition IFFs only include illicit flows, but studies show it is empirically hard to separate practices such as evasion (illegal) from borderline practices like aggressive tax avoidance (generally legal but often considered illicit academically); it is not always clear when lawful tax planning becomes aggressive, and it may only be possible to measure a range because the border may be methodology specific. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is empirically challenging to separate some practices, such as evasion (illegal) and borderline practices"</sub>

## Ensuring validity and soundness of estimation methods

- **expected / limitation**: Because IFFs are deliberately hidden, take many forms and use varying channels, measuring them is hard both conceptually and in practice, and the challenges differ across countries, as do practices for measuring the illegal, non-observed or informal activity that may generate IFFs. The report therefore says that room for country-specific solutions and flexible application of methods within a common framework is necessary. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "space for countryspecific solutions and the flexible application of methods in line with a common framework is necessary"</sub>

## Availability and limitations of data

- **descriptive / data or feature**: National statistical systems already hold some needed data, but scattered across authorities and statistical domains and often lacking a unifying framework; existing national accounts and balance of payments statistics, including estimates of illegal economic activities and the non-observed economy, are a good starting point for measuring IFFs. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "provide a good starting point for the measurement of IFFs"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Other relevant data may be held by police and justice ministries, financial intelligence units and other agencies collecting seizure and criminal offence information; tax authorities typically collect data for assessing tax gaps and exchange country-by-country reporting data on multinational groups, though often for other purposes (e.g. risk assessment). (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "exchange country-by-country reporting data on multinational enterprise groups"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 10 quotes the OECD handbook: the activity groups most likely to be non-observed are underground, illegal, informal sector or household own-final-use activities, and activities may also be missed because of deficiencies in the basic statistical data collection programme. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The groups of activities most likely to be nonobserved are those that are underground, illegal, informal sector"</sub>

## Ensuring validity and soundness of estimation methods

- **expected / method**: The report says statistics need to rest on definitions that can be applied across countries and over time, so that they are comparable. It cites the ICCS as saying crime statistics should rest on a globally agreed classification that defines crimes by actions and behaviours, whatever national legislation says. It adds that statistics cannot be built directly and solely on legal considerations, because legal frameworks differ across jurisdictions, can be very complex to interpret and are often reactive (for example, long delays before new activity types are declared illegal), which leads to unequal treatment across countries and over time. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "statistics cannot be based directly and exclusively on legal considerations since legal frameworks are not consistent across jurisdictions"</sub>
- **expected / method**: Illicit transactions can take several guises and arise at various stages of economic activity, for example funds used for cross-border imports of illicit goods that are then sold on to another country. The report says the activities that generate IFFs need to be analysed carefully within a framework that identifies the components giving rise to illicit activity, and that IFFs need to be classified with a discrete, exhaustive and mutually exclusive statistical classification aligned with existing statistical frameworks and principles. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "discrete, exhaustive and mutually exclusive statistical classification aligned with existing statistical frameworks"</sub>
- **expected / method**: Many illicit activities are intertwined, for example bribery linked to drug trafficking, or trade mispricing. The report says that to avoid double counting, income generation and income management need to be accounted for separately, consistently with the SNA and other statistical frameworks, and that measurement should also use an exclusive statistical classification that separates corruption from other illicit activities as far as possible. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "To avoid duplication, separate accounting for income generation and income management is needed"</sub>

## Availability and limitations of data

- **expected / data or feature**: Trade statistics can inform on commercial IFFs. Customs data on merchandise trade cover all commodities with only small gaps for goods that bypass customs, but countries often lack a single or harmonized data source for trade in services. Services trade is very challenging to measure and seems potentially a large carrier of IFFs, so the report says special efforts will be required to measure and analyse it. It also says due attention is needed when interpreting trade statistics, especially mirror statistics, because several components explain discrepancies between origin- and destination-country data and the existence of IFFs is only one of them. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "often countries do not have a single or harmonized data source to capture trade in services"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 9, citing the 2008 SNA, says there may be no clear borderline between the non-observed economy and illegal production. It lists illegal production as: (1) production of goods or services whose sale, distribution or possession is forbidden by law; (2) usually legal production activities that become illegal when carried out by unauthorized producers, e.g. unlicensed medical practitioners (SNA 6.30); (3) production not complying with certain safety, health or other standards, which could be defined as illegal (SNA 6.35); and (4) a scope that depends on the laws in place in each country, e.g. prostitution. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there may be no clear borderline between the non-observed economy and illegal production"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Footnote 9 also states that transactions on unofficial markets must be included in the accounts, whether those markets are actually legal or illegal. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Transactions on unofficial markets must also be included in the accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 11: some countries have advanced services-trade reporting; since October 2012 Brazil has required legal entities and individuals to report all services taken or rendered abroad as imports or exports of services to the Integrated System of Foreign Service Trade (SISCOSERV), which includes services, intangibles and other transactions that may change net worth in Brazil. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "since October 2012 Brazil requires Brazilian legal entities and individuals to report on all services taken or rendered abroad"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Systematic data collection on crime-related IFFs exists: UNODC compiles drug statistics reported by countries, including demand, supply, prices, drug characteristics and seizures; some countries will likely need additional information sources to measure IFFs consistently. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "UNODC compiles statistics on drugs as reported directly by countries"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Compiling IFF statistics requires data from many authorities; central banks, tax authorities and national statistical offices typically have a strong mandate to collect and access such data, and national statistical offices, as coordinators of the national statistical system and focal points for SDG indicators, should be in charge of bringing the necessary stakeholders together to measure IFFs. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should be in charge of bringing the necessary stakeholders together to measure IFFs"</sub>

## MAIN STEPS TAKEN TO DEVELOP THE MEASUREMENT OF ILLICIT FINANCIAL FLOWS

- **descriptive / context**: UNCTAD and UNODC began with analytical studies and a review of measurement methods used by researchers and organizations; from 2017 to 2018 they held expert consultations (national statistical offices, financial intelligence units, tax authorities, academia, NGOs, international organizations), which highlighted the need to agree statistical concepts and definitions and recommended further engagement with national statistical authorities. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "highlighted the need to agree on statistical concepts and definitions and recommended further engagement with national statistical authorities"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Consultations included the UNODC expert consultation in Vienna (12-14 December 2017), the UNCTAD expert meeting in Geneva (20-22 June 2018), and other fora including the World Statistics Congress session in Kuala Lumpur (August 2019), a UNODC side event in Vienna (April and May 2019), a UNECA meeting in Addis Ababa (April 2019), an ESCWA conference in Beirut (November 2018), a UNODC technical meeting in Mexico City (November 2018) and an FTC/ICRICT forum in Nairobi (November 2018). (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Expert consultation on the SDG indicator on illicit financial flows (IFF), Vienna, 12–14 December 2017"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: UNCTAD and UNODC established the statistical Task Force in January 2019 to define statistical concepts, assess data availability, develop statistical methods and review country-level activities; it started with a review of previous research and expert consultation outcomes. Its members are statistical experts from Brazil, Finland, Ireland, Italy, Peru, South Africa and the United Kingdom (national statistical offices, central banks, customs or tax authorities) plus Eurostat, IMF, OECD, UNECA, UNSD, UNCTAD and UNODC. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "established a statistical Task Force17 on the measurement of IFFs in January 2019"</sub>

## Availability and limitations of data

- **descriptive / finding or result**: A 2018 IMF survey on measuring the informal economy found that more than 60 per cent of national statistical offices gather relevant data on illegal, underground and informal activity, drawing on surveys, administrative sources, mirror statistics, international studies and expert assessment. Informal activities are largely domestic, but many of them can produce cross-border flows. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "over 60 per cent of national statistical offices collect relevant data on illegal, underground and informal activities"</sub>

## MAIN STEPS TAKEN TO DEVELOP THE MEASUREMENT OF ILLICIT FINANCIAL FLOWS

- **descriptive / context**: After several conference calls and one face-to-face meeting in Geneva on 16-17 July 2019, the Task Force reached a common understanding on statistical scope and definitions; in October 2019 the custodians presented a reclassification request to the 10th IAEG-SDGs session, which endorsed the direction and moved indicator 16.4.1 from Tier III to Tier II. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "one faceto-face meeting in Geneva on 16–17 July 2019"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Methodologies are being pilot tested in UN Development Account (UNDA) projects with UNECA and ECLAC, with a regional focus on Africa and Latin America and the Caribbean, building country capacity to compile and disseminate IFF statistics and giving insights into feasibility and capacity gaps; another UNDA project is to launch in Asia and the Pacific in 2020 with ESCAP. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Methodologies are currently being pilot tested as part of United Nations Development Account (UNDA) projects"</sub>

## STATISTICAL DEFINITION OF ILLICIT FINANCIAL FLOWS

- **descriptive / definition**: The statistical definition of IFFs is 'Financial flows that are illicit in origin, transfer or use, that reflect an exchange of value and that cross country borders.' (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "illicit in origin, transfer or use, that reflect an exchange of value and that cross country borders"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Feature 1, illicit in origin, transfer or use: a flow is illicit if illicitly generated (e.g. from criminal activities or tax evasion), illicitly transferred (e.g. violating currency controls) or illicitly used (e.g. financing terrorism); it can be legally generated, transferred or used but must be illicit in at least one aspect. Some flows not strictly illegal, such as cross-border tax avoidance that erodes the tax base of the income's source country, fall within the definition. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A flow of value is considered illicit if it is illicitly generated"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Feature 2, exchange of value: more than purely financial transfers, including exchange of goods and services and financial and non-financial assets; for instance illicit cross-border bartering (illicit exchange of goods and services for other goods and services), common in illegal markets, is an IFF. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "illicit cross-border bartering, meaning the illicit exchange of goods and services for other goods and services"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Feature 3: IFFs measure a flow of value over a given time, as opposed to a stock measure, which would be the accumulation of value. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "IFFs measure a flow of value over a given time"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Feature 4, flows that cross a border: includes assets that cross borders and assets whose ownership changes from a resident to a non-resident even if the assets remain in the same jurisdiction. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "assets where the ownership changes from a resident of a country to a non-resident"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: SDG indicator 16.4.1 calls for the 'total value' of IFFs, which is useful for the overall size of the problem but could limit policy usefulness; a more granular measurement and finer typology would help separate the main sources and channels, guide national and international interventions, and enable disaggregating IFFs that are currently legal from those that are not. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A more granular measurement and a finer typology would help to identify and separate"</sub>

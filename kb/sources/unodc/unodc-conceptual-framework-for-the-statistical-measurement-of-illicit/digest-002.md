## STATISTICAL DEFINITION OF ILLICIT FINANCIAL FLOWS

- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 20: the proposed bottom-up approach also treats domestic illicit financial flows as part of the illegal economy; such flows fall outside the IFF definition for SDG 16.4.1 but matter for understanding organized cross-border illicit flows. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These flows would not fall under the definition of IFFs for SDG 16.4.1"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Category 1, illicit tax and commercial IFFs: illicit practices by legal entities, arrangements and individuals aimed at concealing revenues and reducing tax burden through evading controls and regulations; divided into illegal tax and commercial IFFs and IFFs from aggressive tax avoidance. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Illicit tax and commercial IFFs . These include illicit practices by legal entities"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Illegal tax and commercial IFFs include illegal practices such as tariff, duty and revenue offences, tax evasion, competition offences and market manipulation (as in the ICCS); most are non-observed, hidden or part of the shadow, underground or informal economy. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "tariff, duty and revenue offences, tax evasion, competition offences and market manipulation amongst others"</sub>
- **expected / context**: IFFs from illegal economic activities can be laundered so that later transactions look legal. The report says flows of capital of illegal origin should be considered IFFs when they cross a border, because their origin lies in illicit activity, but notes that determining the illicit origin of some flows becomes challenging as distance from that origin increases. IFFs can also start in legal economic activity and become illicit when the flows are managed or transferred illicitly, for instance to evade taxes or to finance illegal activities. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is challenging to determine the illicit origin of certain financial flows as the distance from the illicit origin increases"</sub>
- **expected / method**: IFFs can be classified by sources, channels, impacts, actors involved and motives. The report says a useful typology should give priority to a perspective that enables effective policy action, and therefore a link to the activities from which IFFs arise; for that reason Figure 1 looks at the underlying activities that may generate IFFs rather than at IFFs themselves. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A useful typology should prioritize a perspective that enables effective policy action"</sub>
- **expected / definition**: IFFs from aggressive tax avoidance are illicit flows generated from legal economic activities, through manipulation of transfer pricing, strategic location of debt and intellectual property, tax treaty shopping and the use of hybrid instruments and entities. The report says these flows need to be carefully considered, because they generally arise from legal business transactions and only the illicit part of the cross-border flows falls within the scope of IFFs. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "only the illicit part of the cross-border flows belongs within the scope of IFFs"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Category 2, IFFs from illegal markets: trade in illicit goods and services when the financial flows cross borders, focusing on criminal activities where income comes from exchange (trade) of illegal goods or services, often with criminal organization aimed at profit; includes trafficking in goods such as drugs and firearms and services such as smuggling of migrants, with IFFs from transnational trade and from cross-border flows managing the resulting income. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These include trade in illicit goods and services when the corresponding financial flows cross borders"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Category 3, IFFs from corruption: the ICCS, based on the UN Convention against Corruption, identifies bribery, embezzlement, abuse of functions, trading in influence, illicit enrichment and other acts; corruption IFFs occur when the economic returns directly or indirectly generate cross-border flows and when financial assets are transferred across borders to commit these crimes. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identifies a broad range of criminal acts to be considered as corruption"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnotes 21-23 give definitions: the IMF says the shadow economy includes all economic activities hidden from official authorities for monetary, regulatory and institutional reasons; the SNA says underground production comprises productive, quite legal activities deliberately concealed to avoid income, value added or other taxes, social security contributions, legal standards (minimum wages, maximum hours, safety or health) or administrative procedures; the IMF Task Force working definition of the informal economy comprises household production of goods and market services and activities of corporations (illegal; underground) possibly not covered in regular macroeconomic data collection, including cross-border transactions of resident units. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The shadow economy includes all economic activities which are hidden from official authorities for monetary, regulatory, and institutional reasons"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Category 4, IFFs from exploitation-type activities and financing of crime and terrorism: exploitation-type activities are illegal activities involving a forced and/or involuntary transfer of economic resources between two actors (e.g. slavery and exploitation, extortion, trafficking in persons, kidnapping), where the focus is the financial benefit gained by exploiting or depriving someone of assets; terrorism financing and financing of crime are illicit, voluntary transfers of funds between two actors to fund criminal or terrorist actions; when flows cross borders they constitute IFFs. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "terrorism financing and financing of crime are illicit, voluntary transfers of funds between two actors"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A specific conceptual challenge is deciding which activities are illicit or licit, particularly for tax avoidance; SDG target 16.4 refers to 'illicit' rather than 'illegal' flows, and tax avoidance, including by multinational groups, although usually legal, can drain resources and be considered illicit. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "target 16.4 of the Sustainable Development Goals refers to “illicit” rather than “illegal” financial flows"</sub>

## TAX AVOIDANCE AND ILLICIT FINANCIAL FLOWS

- **descriptive / context**: Including tax avoidance in the IFF definition blurs the line between legal and illegal activity; for SDG indicator 16.4.1 aggressive tax avoidance is included as an IFF while noting such activities are generally legal. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "aggressive tax avoidance is included as an illicit financial flow, while noting that such activities are generally legal"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The Institute for Advanced Studies describes a continuum from legal tax planning to illegal tax evasion; aggressive tax planning is described as taking advantage of the technicalities of a tax system or of mismatches between tax systems for the purpose of reducing tax liability. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "taking advantage of the technicalities of a tax system or of mismatches between two or more tax systems"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Activities treated as aggressive tax avoidance in the OECD Action Plan on Base Erosion and Profit Shifting can include BEPS through interest payments, strategic location of intangible assets, abuse of tax treaties, artificial avoidance of permanent establishment and transfer pricing manipulation. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "strategic location of intangible assets, abuse of tax treaties, artificial avoidance of permanent establishment"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Inclusive Framework on BEPS brings together over 135 jurisdictions; the BEPS package released in October 2015 by OECD and G20 countries gives governments solutions to close rule gaps that let profits be artificially shifted to low or no tax jurisdictions where companies have little or no economic activity, and work on outstanding issues continues. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "brings together over 135 jurisdictions to work on an equal footing to tackle tax avoidance"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Inclusive Framework on BEPS has improved data availability for measuring multinational tax avoidance, including a forthcoming public release of aggregated, anonymized country-by-country report statistics that can be used for indicator 16.4.1. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "forthcoming public release of aggregated and anonymized country-by-country report statistics"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: In the SNA, each transaction satisfying the 'mutual agreement' principle between parties is within the production boundary as productive activity whether or not legal; only productive activities generate value added, but non-productive activities can also transfer value. Illegal markets are productive, exploitation-type activities non-productive, and corruption mixed: bribery or trading in influence involve mutual agreement and are productive, while embezzlement, misappropriation or other diversion of resources are non-productive. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "each transaction that satisfies the principle of “mutual agreement” between parties is to be included within the production boundary"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 26: many illegal actions, such as theft, are crimes against persons or property that cannot be transactions under mutual agreement and are not recorded in the SNA unless thefts or violence (including war) involve significant redistribution or destruction of assets, in which case they are other flows or transfers (changes in asset and liability values), not transactions. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Many illegal actions are crimes against persons or property that cannot be considered as transactions under"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Given differing national laws and practices, and because IFF measurement is a statistical exercise rather than an audit or judicial one, the scope of activities cannot be defined directly by legality; the indicator is built on a typology of behaviours, events and activities generating IFFs, in line with the ICCS and crime statistics generally. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is not possible to define the scope of activities for measurement directly in terms of their legality"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Footnote 27: not all IFFs would map onto the ICCS-based framework, since IFFs related to tax avoidance may not be covered in the ICCS. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "not all IFFs would map onto this framework"</sub>

## STATISTICAL CONFORMITY AND POLICY RELEVANCE: THE PECULIAR CASE OF TRAFFICKING IN PERSONS

- **descriptive / context**: Trafficking in persons is classed as an exploitation-type activity but analytically and in policy is often treated as part of illegal markets because it is perpetrated for profit and analysed with other organized crime markets such as drug and firearms trafficking; IFFs from it can still be analysed under the most appropriate policy and analytical framework given specific or national circumstances. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "from an analytical and policy point of view, it is often considered as part of illegal markets"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The statistical distinction between illegal markets and exploitation-type activities rests on mutual agreement: in illegal markets income comes from exchange of goods or services by mutual agreement (e.g. drugs or firearms trafficking), while in exploitation-type activities (trafficking in persons, forced labour, slavery) perpetrators profit from exploiting others without mutual agreement. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The statistical distinction between illegal markets and exploitation-type activities is based on mutual agreement"</sub>
- **expected / context**: Trafficking in persons may involve no economic transaction between trafficker and victim, because any statistically defined economic transaction must rest on mutual agreement. Other transactions may occur, for example at recruitment or between traffickers, but exploitation of victims appears to be the core activity from which traffickers derive their main illegal proceeds, as is also true of sexual exploitation and forced labour. The report says the various IFFs generated in trafficking in persons should in principle be categorized as exploitation-type activities, although they can also be assessed from different analytical perspectives. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should in principle be categorized as “exploitation-type activities”"</sub>

## STATISTICAL APPROACH FOR MEASURING ILLICIT FINANCIAL FLOWS

- **descriptive / method**: Existing IFF measurement proposals fall into two approaches. Top-down methods interpret or model inconsistencies in aggregated data such as currency demand, international trade and the balance of payments capital account; mirror statistics can inform on some tax and trade IFFs generated in licit activity but transferred illicitly. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Top-down methods attempt to measure IFFs by interpreting or modelling inconsistencies in different types of aggregated data"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Bottom-up approaches analyse how relevant illicit activities function, identify the flows that are IFFs and produce estimates for each, with overall estimates aggregated from lower to higher level (e.g. by IFF type or source). Consistent with defining IFF types by their generating activity, a bottom-up and direct measurement approach is proposed. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Bottom-up approaches attempt to measure IFFs by analysing the functioning of relevant illicit activities"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Footnote 28 groups top-down approaches by the data set examined: the Currency Demand Approach compares actual currency demand with the amount that should be demanded given observable economic indicators; the Hot Money Method and Residual Approach use discrepancies in financial accounts to estimate flows tied to the non-observed economy; discrepancies in mirror trade statistics may measure trade-related illicit flows; gravity models use attractiveness to judge how far flows between countries may be illicit. Footnote 29 says the bottom-up approach is consistent with Eurostat (2018). (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Currency Demand Approach (Schneider, F., 2011; Schneider, F. and Williams, C., 2013; Ardizzi et al., 2016)"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Two stages at which IFFs arise: IFFs linked to income generation are cross-border transactions performed in producing illicit goods and services, or cross-border operations that directly generate illicit income for an actor in a non-productive illicit activity; IFFs linked to income management are cross-border transactions that use illicit income for investment in (legal or illicit) financial and non-financial assets or consumption of (legal or illegal) goods and services. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "IFFs linked to income management , as the set of cross-border transactions finalized to use the (illicit) income"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: IFFs arise when criminal acts generate illicit income: sale of illicit goods and services sends value from buyer to seller and extortion generates income for the extorter; inward or outward IFFs occur at income generation when the operation crosses a border (e.g. drugs sold by a resident of one country to a resident of another). (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Inward or outward IFFs occur during income generation when the operation in question is performed across a border"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnotes 30-31: this typology is coherent with the SNA, where income generation relates to the production account and the generation and distribution of income accounts and income management to the capital and use of income accounts; an 'operation' is an exchange between parties independent of any agreement among them. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An operation is defined as an exchange between parties, independent of a possible agreement among them"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: IFFs also arise from illicit income management (use of illicit income for investment or consumption, or illicit management of income from legal activity, e.g. to reduce the tax base): spent abroad it is an outward IFF; income from illicit activity outside a jurisdiction but spent or invested domestically is an inward IFF. In sum IFFs comprise four flow types: inflows and outflows from illicit income generation and inflows and outflows from illicit income management. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "inflows and outflows from illicit income generation and inflows and outflows from illicit income management"</sub>

## Illicit financial flows related to income generation

- **descriptive / method**: IFFs from income generation mainly relate to goods and services in illegal markets as transactions aimed at unlawful productive processes for profit; like any productive process, income generation is represented by gross output, intermediate expenditure (intermediate costs) and value added (net income for the actors). (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "can be represented by three main aggregates: gross output, intermediate expenditure"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: (Illicit) gross income/gross output is the value of illicit goods or services produced in a period, determined as quantity times price (for example the domestic retail price, or the export price if exported). (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "refers to the value of the illicit goods or services produced in a given period"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: (Illicit) intermediate expenditure is the value of licit and illicit inputs acquired to produce illicit goods and services in a period, determined as quantity per price (for example the domestic price if bought from a resident, or the import price if bought from a non-resident). (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "refers to the value of (licit and illicit) inputs acquired to produce illicit goods and services over a given period"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: (Illicit) value added/net income is the economic result of the productive process, gross output minus intermediate expenditure, representing net income earned by all actors carrying out the illicit activity. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is determined as gross output-minus-intermediate expenditure"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Inward IFFs occur when illicit goods and services are exported abroad, either for final consumption (e.g. an online dealer sells synthetic drugs to a consumer abroad) or as licit or illicit intermediate inputs to illicit production abroad (e.g. morphine exported to make heroin, or transport services bought by a non-resident trafficker to ship cocaine). Outward IFFs occur when intermediate inputs are imported against payments abroad (e.g. drugs imported for domestic resale) or final illicit goods and services are imported. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Inward IFFs occur when illicit goods and services are exported abroad"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Corruption can generate IFFs similarly: when a foreign business entity bribes a public official to obtain a procurement contract or natural resource extraction permission, the unlawful service of the official is a productive activity (mutual agreement) that generates an IFF from the business's country to the official's country. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "when a foreign business entity bribes a public official to facilitate the granting of a procurement contract"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: The figure on income generation in illegal markets (example: drug trafficking; source UNODC) notes: outflows (a) are imports of illicit goods and services used to generate illicit final products; inflows (b) are exports of final goods and services; outflows (c) are imports of illicit final goods and services produced in another country (e.g. self-imported ivory or illicit drugs ordered online). (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Outflows (a) correspond to the import of illicit goods and services that are used for generating illicit final products"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Direction depends on residence: if the sender (e.g. a victim) is resident and the recipient (e.g. extorter) is non-resident, non-productive activities generate outward IFFs; if the sender is non-resident and the recipient resident, inward IFFs emerge. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "if the sender (e.g. victim of an exploitation-type activity) is resident and the recipient"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: IFFs from income generation by non-productive activities, such as exploitation-type activities, equal the aggregated value of all funds and goods that move between resident and non-resident entities (for example when cross-border extortion is committed). Intermediate costs of non-productive activities are treated as 'operational costs', which the SNA implicitly accounts for as final consumption. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they equal the aggregated value of all funds and goods that move between resident and non-resident entities"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Footnote 32 states that when illicit income from 'domestic' exploitation-type activities (for example extortion or kidnapping between resident entities) is 'transferred above' (wording as in the extracted text), it refers to the income management phase. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "transferred above, it refers to the income management phase"</sub>

## Illicit financial flows from income management

- **descriptive / method**: IFFs can result from using (managing) illicit income from any of the four activity types in Table 1 when transferred abroad for investment or consumption. Outward IFFs emerge when resident illicit funds buy goods and services abroad (other than those counted as intermediate costs) or acquire foreign financial or non-financial assets; inward IFFs arise when non-resident illicit funds (e.g. from drug trafficking elsewhere) are used in the country. These flows are difficult to measure and not easy to link to a specific illicit activity. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These flows are difficult to measure and are not easy to link to a specific illicit activity"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The size of outward IFFs from income management relates to domestically generated (licit or illicit) net income; inward IFFs depend on other factors such as a country's attractiveness for money laundering, inter-country differences in tax rates, rules and regulations, the likelihood of nationals abroad sending home illicitly generated income, and the internal structure of multinationals and other globally present firms. IFFs from income management can also cover income from licit or illicit activities illicitly transferred abroad. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the attractiveness of the country for money laundering, the inter-country difference in tax rates and rules and regulations"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: BEPS activities can relate to tax and trade IFFs in income management and take forms including strategic location of debt and intangible assets, transfer pricing manipulation and artificial avoidance of permanent establishment; BEPS estimates range between US$100 billion and over US$600 billion. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Estimates of BEPS range between US$100 billion and over US$600 billion"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: IFFs can also occur when funds are shifted out of a jurisdiction to evade (not avoid) tax, including illegally concealing income or assets from tax authorities; corporate structures with no real economic activity can be used for tax evasion strategies, concealing corrupt funds of public officials and other crimes such as money laundering, for example to transfer and conceal trade profits in lower-tax jurisdictions or to conceal the beneficial owner or ultimate investor to avoid tax on investment income. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Corporate structures with no real economic activity can be used as part of tax-evasion strategies"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Chains of corporate structures with misreporting or non-reporting of beneficial owners, called 'anonymous ownership vehicles', aim to benefit from routes with tax advantages and so can potentially generate IFFs. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "also called “anonymous ownership vehicles”, aim to benefit from routes with tax advantages"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The document has set out the statistical definition and scope of IFFs for measuring SDG indicator 16.4.1, endorsed by the IAEG-SDGs; next steps are to develop and test statistical methodologies consistent with the definitions, the SNA and balance of payments statistics, and countries already collect data on illegal activities and other statistics that can contribute. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The next steps will be to develop and test a range of statistical methodologies consistent with the definitions above"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Some IFF aspects are more readily measurable, for instance IFFs from trade in illegal goods and services (illicit drug markets, smuggling of migrants, prohibited wildlife products), where national and international data on supply, demand, trade flows and prices are collected systematically. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "IFFs originating in trade of illegal goods and services"</sub>

## Deepfakes

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Recent research found at least 493 child sextortion reports submitted to the U.S. National Center for Missing and Exploited Children (NCMEC) between 2022 and 2024 traceable to around 40 scam centres across Cambodia, Myanmar and Laos, plus an additional 18,000 child exploitation reports linked via IP addresses to these locations, suggesting possible scam centre involvement in online child sexual exploitation and abuse (OCSEA). (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "at least 493 reports of child sextortion, submitted to the U.S. National Center for Missing and Exploited Children (NCMEC)"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In Myanmar, testimonies indicate trafficked persons held in scam centres are forced into sexually explicit online conversations with international targets. Regional law enforcement noted scam networks that, when unsuccessful securing investments through fraudulent trading platforms, shifted to sextortion by coercing victims into explicit video interactions and then extorting them for payment. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "shifted to sextortion by coercing victims into explicit video interactions and subsequently extorting them for payment"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Underground marketplaces advertise deepfake-as-a-service, letting buyers request tailored video or voice impersonations of specific individuals for a fee. Real-time voice changers are increasingly accessible and let scammers alter their voice on live calls to impersonate people of different ages, genders or backgrounds; online child predators have used them to pose as minors in video chats, facilitating grooming and exploitation. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Underground marketplaces are now advertising deepfake-as-a-service offerings"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Concerns are being raised about generative AI being weaponized in OSCEA (as written) contexts, warning that voice and video manipulation could be used to entrap children in live interactions. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "voice and video manipulation could be used to entrap children in live interactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Scam centres in Southeast Asia show indications of using AI for sextortion. Operations increasingly rely on AI-generated images, deepfakes and synthetic profiles to lure and entrap victims, and in Cambodia trafficked people have been forced into video sex rooms where AI overlays simulate nudity during video calls so that compromising material can later be used for blackmail. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There are indications of scam centres in Southeast Asia exploiting AI to conduct sextortion"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Southeast Asia accounts for approximately 10.2 per cent of global social media users, with the Philippines, Thailand and Indonesia among the highest globally for time spent online; the platforms have been used to disseminate AI-generated disinformation and promotional scams. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "approximately 10.2 per cent of global social media users"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In December 2023, deepfake videos of then Singaporean Prime Minister Lee Hsien Loong and Deputy Prime Minister Lawrence Wong falsely promoting investment platforms and immigration services circulated widely, prompting an official response from the Prime Minister's Office. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "falsely promoting investment platforms and immigration services were widely circulated"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Deepfakes have been used in broader disinformation campaigns: during elections, AI-generated videos falsely depicted candidates and their families engaging in illegal activities to manipulate public perception and influence elections, as seen in recent cases affecting France, Indonesia, the Philippines and the Republic of Korea. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI-generated videos falsely depicted candidates and their family engaging in illegal activities"</sub>

## Exploitation of AI for malware development and evasion

- **descriptive / finding or result**: AI is increasingly exploited to help develop more advanced, stealthy and adaptive malware able to circumvent traditional cybersecurity defences, significantly lowering the technical barrier for threat actors while raising the complexity of threats for defenders. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "significantly lowered the technical barrier to entry for threat actors"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Generative AI and large language models (LLMs) let criminals accelerate cyber-enabled fraud through automation, translation, grammar improvement and reconnaissance. To get output against provider safety guardrails, the user must 'jailbreak' the LLM; criminals use generative AI to automate malicious code creation, allowing relatively unskilled hackers to produce malware previously possible only for skilled hand-coders, and discuss using AI chatbots to generate or refine malware in underground forums. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the user must “jailbreak” the LLM to get the model into a state in which it can generate malware"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In 2024 Japanese authorities arrested four teenagers for generating malicious scripts with an LLM to bypass an online contract system of a mobile service provider, and a 25-year-old man for creating ransomware with a generative AI tool, the first known case in Japan involving malware made with a generative AI tool. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Japanese authorities arrested four teenagers for generating malicious scripts with a large language model"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Several methods exist to overcome LLM safety guardrails: a request for malware would normally be rejected as violating ethical guidelines, but prompt manipulation can overcome this; online forums discuss jailbreaking techniques for popular platforms; and open-source models can be jailbroken to run on local devices, producing content that generative AI service providers cannot examine. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "opensource models can be jailbroken to run on local devices"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The brief says the effectiveness of jailbroken models is uncertain at the time of writing, though their potential to evolve into more sophisticated threats is significant. Some are jailbroken open-source systems; criminal groups have also developed and trained models such as WormGPT to operate without guardrails and generate illicit content such as malware and phishing emails. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "While the effectiveness of jailbroken models remains uncertain at the time of writing"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: AI is used to help malware stay undetected: modern variants incorporate machine learning that lets malware adapt to its environment and avoid activation when observed. IBM's DeepLocker proof-of-concept embedded a payload in a benign application and triggered execution only when specific biometric, geographic or behavioural traits of the intended victim were detected; the approach has informed real-world tactics where malware lies dormant across infected machines and activates only under precise conditions, bypassing static detection. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "allow the malware to adapt to its environment and avoid activation when under observation"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Some malware strains use AI-enabled polymorphism to evolve their code autonomously and avoid signature-based antivirus, supported by reinforcement learning frameworks that let malware learn from defensive responses and modify tactics. AI also generates phishing lures that evade linguistic pattern filters; spam filters and web security tools trained on known malicious signatures struggle with dynamically generated content, which is often more contextually convincing, localized and linguistically sophisticated than manual variants. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "polymorphic capabilities, enabled by AI, to evolve their code autonomously and avoid signature-based antivirus systems"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote definitions: a prompt is a directive or input string given to an AI model that defines context and parameters for a specific output; WormGPT is an uncensored AI tool using GPT-J, optimized to help cybercriminals, released around mid-2023 and popular on dark web forums sharing tips on phishing emails, malware and business email compromise language. A further footnote notes that jailbreaking LLMs is not strictly for illicit purposes: researchers and security practitioners use jailbroken models to understand capabilities, identify jailbroken output and test for zero-day vulnerabilities. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "WormGPT is an uncensored AI tool that uses GPT-J and is optimized to help cybercriminals"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Researchers at University College Dublin developed a jailbreaking technique with a generative AI tool. The jailbroken LLM produced highly malicious content, including a sophisticated credential-dumping tool, and code comments showed the tool was aware it was developing ransomware yet did not stop generating. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Recently, researchers from University College Dublin developed a jailbreaking technique using a generative AI tool"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 43 defines credential dumping as ransomware attackers extracting authentication credentials from the operating system into locations of their own choosing, usually plaintext files. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Credential Dumping is when ransomware attackers extract (i.e. ‘dump’) authentication credentials from the operating system"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: At network level, attackers use adversarial AI to subtly alter traffic flows and deceive machine-learning-based intrusion detection systems, and use vision-based AI to solve CAPTCHA tests. AI-generated biometric forgeries, such as deepfake ID photos and video feeds, are used to defeat face verification during digital identity checks. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "leveraging adversarial AI to subtly alter traffic flows and deceive intrusion detection systems"</sub>

## AI-enhanced ransomware

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Ransomware (malicious software that encrypts victims' data for extortion) remains a severe threat in Southeast Asia, with attacks in Asia rising 85 per cent in 2023. In 2024, the 'Brain Cipher' group's campaign disrupted over 160 Indonesian government agencies and an attack on a major Vietnamese brokerage halted trading for over a week. Rapid digitization with limited comprehensive security has made the region attractive; syndicates use AI and machine learning to enhance malware, automate target selection and optimize ransom negotiation. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "disrupted over 160 Indonesian government agencies"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Unlike traditional ransomware with static code and pre-configured routines, AI-enabled variants show adaptive behaviour across their lifecycle (reconnaissance, evasion, encryption, extortion). Embedded machine learning can autonomously analyze target environments, detect critical assets and identify security protections so attackers operate without direct human control; malware may rewrite its code or alter its payload in response to defences, enabling polymorphism. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "autonomously analyze target environments, detect critical assets, and identify security protections"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Criminal collectives such as FunkSec have reportedly begun to use AI-assisted development to streamline production of advanced ransomware, even offering RaaS platforms with automated payload generation and target selection features. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Criminal collectives such as FunkSec have reportedly begun to incorporate similar AIassisted development processes"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: AI is used in the post-encryption phase: threat actors assess victims' financial capacity from company records, public filings or stolen internal data to generate personalized ransom pricing ('smart extortion', mirroring dynamic pricing). Emerging AI-powered negotiation bots can interact with multiple victims in diverse languages at once on portals, simulate human negotiators and adjust pressure tactics in real time, shifting ransomware toward a psychologically manipulative, data-driven enterprise. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "threat actors use AI to assess victims’ financial capacity"</sub>

## Exploitation of AI for malware development and evasion

- **descriptive / finding or result**: In 2023, over two million identity fraud attempts were studied, and AI-powered fraud including deepfake IDs emerged as the top threat. The brief reports a global tenfold increase in deepfakes used to bypass KYC (know your customer) verifications, and says criminals are using AI to counter AI by generating fake faces to fool face-recognition identity checks. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "with a global 10 times increase in deepfakes used to bypass KYC (know your customer) verifications"</sub>

## AI-enhanced ransomware

- **descriptive / finding or result**: In Southeast Asia, where healthcare providers, educational institutions and private enterprises are recurrent targets, cybersecurity firms have observed malware strains with AI-enabled reconnaissance and evasion features, indicating the region may be an early testing ground for these ransomware techniques. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Southeast Asia may be an early testing ground for these evolving ransomware techniques"</sub>

## AI-driven social engineering and scams

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Generative AI is significantly amplifying the scale, convincibility and reach of social engineering. Phishing-as-a-service kits increasingly integrate AI modules that generate tailored phishing content: criminals prompt LLMs to produce professional messages replicating banks, government agencies or commercial platforms, specifying tone, subject and formatting and producing multiple variants that reduce detection by pattern-based filters. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "phishing-asa-service kits are increasingly integrating AI modules to automatically generate tailored phishing content"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: AI-enabled translation lets scammers run campaigns across language barriers, for example English-speaking operators convincingly phishing victims in Thai, Vietnamese or Japanese. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "permitting English-speaking operators to convincingly phish victims in Thai, Vietnamese, or Japanese"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Reliance on AI does not eliminate the role of persons trafficked for forced criminality: scam centres still recruit or coerce people with specific language skills, reflecting LLM limits in cultural nuance and a preference for human interaction where persuasion is critical. Trafficking or recruiting a native speaker (for example Japanese or Korean) is often easier and more effective than relying on automated tools, helping explain the growing presence of victims from outside Southeast Asia in scam centres. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "reflecting both the limitations of LLMS in maintaining cultural nuance"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Evidence indicates scam centres in Southeast Asia use LLMs for multilingual conversations with victims, with AI-driven scripts able to hold simultaneous conversations in different languages, work that once needed a trained multilingual team. Some tools automatically detect and suppress unintended language output (for instance keeping Chinese text out of scams aimed at non-Chinese speakers) to conceal operational origins, letting one scammer engage many more victims. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "automatically detect and suppress unintended language outputs"</sub>

## Box story 2. Major call-centre scam crackdown in southern Thailand

- **descriptive / case example**: In March 2024 Thailand's Department of Special Investigation (DSI), supported by cyber police, immigration and communications regulators, dismantled a large scam operation in Chawang District, Nakhon Si Thammarat, raiding a hotel, luxury residence and commercial buildings repurposed as call-centre hubs run by Chinese nationals. Authorities arrested 71 suspects (52 Chinese nationals and 19 Thais) and seized 223 computers, 1,001 mobile phones, 14 tablets, 298 SIM cards and 86 bank passbooks. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Authorities arrested 71 suspects, including 52 Chinese nationals and 19 Thais"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The syndicate targeted victims in Thailand, China, Russia and Japan with scripted conversations for investment scams, romance fraud and fake e-commerce schemes. Forensics revealed multilanguage scam templates, encrypted communication platforms and extensive mule banking infrastructure; rental agreements linked one scam property to a local deputy mayor, and the suspected ringleader was apprehended. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "multilanguage scam templates, encrypted communication platforms, and extensive mule banking infrastructure"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Investigators noted partial automation in managing victim outreach (scripted dialogues, SIM box coordination, centralized dashboards for multiple communications). No advanced AI tools were confirmed in this case, but the structure suggests potential for AI-enhanced scalability; generative AI could automate real-time scam conversations, adapt emotional tone, impersonate voices or public figures with deepfakes and harvest personal data for tailored targeting, dramatically increasing reach, speed and impact if unchecked. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "While no advanced AI tools were confirmed in this case"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: AI is used to create false digital identities for long-term deception: romance scams and investment frauds increasingly use AI-generated profile images of non-existent people that evade reverse image detection, supported by deepfake 'proof of life' videos. Tools can generate entire digital identities (profile photos, social media content, fake identification documents and coherent online footprints) to gain victims' trust over time, and some scammers have used chatbot personas with AI-generated voices and scripted seduction dialogues to lure victims into fraudulent investment schemes. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "profile images, which depict non-existent individuals and evade reverse image detection tools"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Beyond traditional BEC, threat actors have begun using AI-generated voice clones to impersonate corporate executives on phone calls; criminals in the Hong Kong, China case used AI-generated video and voice to impersonate senior executives during a virtual meeting. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "using AI-generated voice clones to impersonate corporate executives during phone calls"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Per a security group analysis, 1.8 billion emails were processed globally and 208 million were malicious; BEC accounted for 58 per cent of phishing attempts, and 89 per cent of these involved impersonations of authority figures such as CEOs and IT staff. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "1.8 billion emails were processed globally, revealing that 208 million were malicious"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Approximately 40 per cent of BEC attempts globally involved AI-generated emails, and a 1,760 per cent year-over-year increase in AI-assisted BEC activity was reported. Cybercriminal groups also routinely use deepfake voice generation and natural language processing tools to simulate authentic communication, making fraudulent messages harder to identify. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Approximately 40 per cent of BEC attempts globally involved AI-generated emails"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Figure 3 (lifecycle of an AI-enhanced phishing attack; text scrambled) shows stages: reconnaissance and targeting, preparation, attack email delivery, exploitation and credential harvesting, payload execution and monetization, and impact with cleanup and covering tracks; it also shows the attacker manually designing prompts because of LLM content-filtering warnings, prompts instructing the model to produce seemingly benign functional objects containing source code, and the LLM fine-tuning and generating prompt sets. (p. 15)[^original]

## AI-enabled surveillance and evasion in criminal networks

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Criminal networks may integrate AI to enhance operational security, monitor law enforcement and disguise digital and physical footprints. In territories under de facto control of organized groups it is conceivable that commercial facial recognition software could be integrated into local CCTV networks; there are indications such systems are used to automatically identify and monitor law enforcement personnel or rival actors, and drug trafficking networks have used them to detect undercover officers or unmarked police vehicles entering key operational zones. (p. 15)[^original]

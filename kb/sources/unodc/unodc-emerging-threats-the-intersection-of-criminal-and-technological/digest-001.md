## Introduction

- **descriptive / context**: Scam compounds and their online fraud networks operate within a wider ecosystem of transnational organized crime and draw on shared enablers such as underground banking, cryptocurrency-based laundering, and exploitation of jurisdictional and regulatory gaps; the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) is further transforming this environment. (p. 2)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "underground banking, cryptocurrency-based laundering, and the exploitation of jurisdictional and regulatory gaps"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Non-AI automation (bots, scripts and malware kits) remains widely deployed, while AI integration is reshaping the threat landscape with unprecedented speed and complexity; criminal actors adopt AI to enhance the scale, efficiency and adaptability of operations. (p. 2)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "While non-artificial intelligence automation continues to be widely deployed in the form of bots, scripts, and malware kits"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Malicious AI uses named include autonomous cyberattacks, deepfake-enabled impersonation, and AI-optimized malware and phishing campaigns, which challenge conventional crime prevention and investigative frameworks. (p. 2)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "From autonomous cyberattacks and deepfakeenabled impersonation to AI-optimized malware and phishing campaigns"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: AI-generated code can be embedded into malware that dynamically adapts to its environment, significantly reducing the chance of detection. IBM's DeepLocker prototype showed AI concealing malicious payloads in a legitimate application and releasing them only when the intended victim was identified through facial recognition or geolocation; such stealthy targeted behaviour, once the domain of advanced persistent threats, is becoming more accessible through commodified AI tools. (p. 2)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "IBM’s DeepLocker prototype, which demonstrated how AI could conceal malicious payloads within a legitimate application"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: AI-enabled botnets can scan for system vulnerabilities, bypass verification mechanisms such as CAPTCHA tests, and sustain high-volume spam or denial-of-service campaigns with limited oversight. In a recent case the AkiraBot spam network used AI to bypass human verification and post automated content across tens of thousands of websites (the footnote title cites 80,000 sites). (p. 2)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AkiraBot spam network, which used AI to bypass human verification protocols"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: AI has enhanced credential stuffing and password cracking by enabling models trained on leaked datasets to generate plausible password combinations, significantly increasing success rates. (p. 2)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "models trained on leaked datasets to generate plausible password combinations"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The brief introduces the concept of crime automation, focusing on integration of AI into cybercrime methods, and examines how traditional and AI-powered automation is exploited to conduct, scale and conceal criminal activity, especially in Southeast Asia, to support law enforcement, policymakers and practitioners. (p. 2)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "how automation, both traditional and AI-powered, is being exploited to conduct, scale, and conceal criminal activity"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The brief links these developments to the growth of transnational scam operations that combine cyber-enabled fraud with other grave offences, such as human trafficking for forced criminality and large-scale illicit financial flows. (p. 2)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "proliferation of transnational scam operations that merge cyber-enabled fraud with other serious crimes"</sub>

## Automation of crime

- **descriptive / context**: Southeast Asian cybercriminals use scripts and automated software to greatly expand the scale and speed of operations, such as sending thousands of phishing messages, breaching online accounts or distributing malware globally. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "have become adept at using scripts and automated software to commit crimes more efficiently"</sub>

## Automated phishing attacks

- **descriptive / definition**: The brief defines phishing as tricking victims into revealing passwords or financial information via fake communications, and says it remains the most common cybercrime, made more dangerous by automation. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the tricking of victims into revealing passwords or financial information via fake communications"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Criminals use phishing kits and phishing-as-a-service (PhaaS) platforms, i.e. pre-packaged malicious websites and email templates deployable with minimal effort. The '16shop' kit (operated out of Indonesia) was sold on the dark web and used by over 70,000 people in 43 countries; it let even low-skill attackers instantly generate realistic clone sites of brands such as Apple, Amazon or PayPal. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "used by over 70,000 people in 43 countries"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Over 150,000 phishing domains were created using 16shop kits targeting users worldwide. The kits cost around US $60-150 each and automated much of the work: the buyer specified a target brand and the service generated a ready-to-use phishing site. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "over 150,000 phishing domains were created using 16shop kits targeting users worldwide"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Automation lets criminals send thousands or millions of phishing emails and SMSs using spam-sending bots and mass-mailing software. Cybersecurity studies estimate nearly 1.2 per cent of all emails sent globally are malicious, about 3.4 billion phishing emails every day; the scale indicates coordinated, automation-driven operations. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "nearly 1.2 per cent of all emails sent globally are malicious, translating to about 3.4 billion phishing emails every day"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Offenders buy bulk email lists from underground forums and use automated mailing tools to send deceptive messages such as fake security alerts. The emails often carry malicious attachments, such as Microsoft Office files with macro scripts; when opened with macros enabled, the script installs malware, typically a 'dropper' that downloads further payloads. Automation lets one actor target thousands of victims at once, and even with a low success rate the volume keeps phishing profitable. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Microsoft Office files embedded with macro scripts"</sub>

## Botnet operations and mass attacks

- **descriptive / definition**: A botnet is a network of malware-infected computers or Internet-of-Things (IoT) devices under an attacker's control; botnets act as force multipliers, letting a criminal remotely command thousands of machines to flood a website with traffic, send spam or spread malware. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a network of malware-infected computers or Internet-of-Things (IoT) devices under an attacker’s control"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Southeast Asia has many vulnerable devices conscripted into global botnets. Analyses found many Mirai botnets were in Southeast Asia and South America because Mirai targeted insecure IoT devices often shipped with default passwords, making areas with many non-authentic gadgets easy targets. Mirai enabled DDoS attacks exceeding one terabit per second, with several of the largest recorded incidents involving traffic from Southeast Asia-based botnets. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Mirai targeted insecure IoT devices, often delivered with default passwords"</sub>

## Box story 1. Stingray devices linked to regional scam network found in Thailand

- **descriptive / case example**: In April 2024 Thailand's Cyber Crime Investigation Bureau (CCIB) arrested six suspects in Bangkok, including two individuals from Hong Kong, China, believed involved in a transnational scam network using telecommunications interception devices. The CCIB also seized three modified vehicles equipped with IMSI-catchers (Stingray devices), used to impersonate mobile phone towers and send scam messages to phones in the vicinity. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "arrested six suspects in Bangkok, including two individuals from Hong Kong, China"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The seized IMSI-catchers appeared manufactured in another country in the region and were marketed via Telegram channels. They could intercept real-time mobile phone metadata; scammers used them to target specific groups with SMS phishing messages fraudulently mimicking banks or official institutions, and authorities confirmed they were used to acquire subscriber data and general fraudulent prompts to compromise victims' accounts. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "marketed for sale via Telegram channels potentially accessible to criminal organizations throughout the region"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: One victim was confirmed; forensic financial tracing showed funds taken from the victim were used for purchases at a hotel in another country in the region, showing transnational capacity. Thai police intelligence shows active linkages to scam syndicates in the Philippines, where part of the network's support operations appear to be based. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "forensic financial tracing showed that funds taken from the victim were subsequently used to make purchases at a hotel"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In August 2025 a 35-year-old South Korean was apprehended driving a rental car fitted with a false base station (FBS) able to transmit bulk spoofed messages while circulating through densely populated Bangkok districts. In both cases the suspects were low-level operators for Chinese syndicates required to give regular updates via Telegram; the South Korean was told to report his location every 30 minutes. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a 35-yearold South Korean was apprehended driving a rental car fitted with a false base station (FBS)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: UNODC concludes that organized crime groups repurpose surveillance-grade telecommunications tools for mobile smishing, combine them with encrypted messaging platforms for remote command-and-control, and exploit urban density and foreign couriers to maximize reach, which complicates attribution, strains legal frameworks and challenges law enforcement detection and response. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "organized crime groups repurpose surveillance-grade telecommunications tools for mobile smishing campaigns"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The cases represent an increasing trend of IMSI-catcher exploitation by criminals as part of online scams in Thailand, with indications of similar activity in other countries such as the Philippines. Thai authorities, with multi-national partners, are analyzing seized material, mapping regional supply chains and examining transnational financial flows. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "increasing trend of IMSI-catcher exploitation by criminal actors as part of online scams in Thailand"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Botnets enable automation of spam distribution, malware delivery and click fraud. A single infected device may send only a few hundred emails, but a botnet of one million can distribute hundreds of millions daily under one operator; the Srizbi botnet reportedly sent up to 60 billion spam emails per day at its peak. More recent botnets still deliver malicious spam, often as a vector for ransomware or banking trojans, and are frequently leased as infrastructure-for-hire. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Srizbi botnet reportedly sent up to 60 billion spam emails per day at its peak"</sub>

## Credential stuffing and automated account cracking

- **descriptive / definition**: Credential stuffing is an automated technique where stolen credentials, exposed in breaches and available on the dark web, are rapidly tested across multiple websites, targeting users who reuse passwords. Attackers use bots and specialized scripts for mass logins, often routing through botnets or proxy servers to evade detection and bypass IP restrictions. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an automated technique where stolen credentials are rapidly tested across multiple websites"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: An identity and access management firm reported over 10 billion credential stuffing attempts in the first quarter of 2022 alone, 34 per cent of all login activity on its platform. The threat was particularly pronounced in Southeast Asia and the United States, where automated login attempts consistently surpassed legitimate user traffic. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "over 10 billion credential stuffing attempts, accounting for 34 per cent of all login activity"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Indicators and consequences of credential stuffing: bots testing thousands of credentials per minute, causing sudden spikes in login requests and triggering mass account lockouts; where credentials are reused, compromise can lead to widespread account hijacking, including access to financial platforms. Tools are widely circulated on criminal forums; in response many service providers monitor for anomalous login activity and promote multi-factor authentication as a key mitigation measure. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "causing sudden spikes in login requests and triggering mass account lockouts"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Figure 1 (simplified credential stuffing attack flow) has five steps: stolen credentials obtained from previous breaches or dark web marketplaces; automated attack initiated using bots or credential-stuffing tools across multiple platforms; login requests sent to target sites; successful logins where users reused credentials; account takeover exploited for financial gain, data theft or further criminal activity. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Stolen Credentials ObtainedAttacker acquires usernames andpasswords from previous data breachesor dark web marketplaces."</sub>

## Malware distribution at scale

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Malware distribution is a core component of cybercrime, with automation central to large-scale infections. Criminals use malware droppers, exploit kits and self-propagating worms to deliver payloads with minimal manual effort; phishing emails with macro-enabled attachments commonly serve as the initial infection vector. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "malware droppers, exploit kits, and self-propagating worms to deliver malicious payloads"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Exploit kits, often embedded in compromised or malicious websites, scan a visitor's browser for known vulnerabilities and execute 'drive-by downloads' without user interaction. Kits such as Magnitude and RIG have discreetly delivered ransomware and banking trojans; use has declined with improved browser security but they still pose risks, particularly on poorly maintained or compromised servers. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "scanning a visitor’s browser for known vulnerabilities and executing “drive-by downloads” without requiring user interaction"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The GoldDigger remote access trojan, developed by the GoldFactory group identified in 2023, targeted users including in Southeast Asia by disguising itself as legitimate iOS and Android apps and using accessibility features and overlay attacks to steal banking credentials and intercept one-time passwords. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The GoldDigger remote access trojan, developed by the GoldFactory group identified in 2023"</sub>

## Automated money laundering

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Bots now help open mule accounts using stolen or fabricated identities, automating form-filling, document uploads and solving CAPTCHAs; these hybrid bots cut account-creation time and let one operator control multiple accounts simultaneously. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Bots now assist in opening mule accounts using stolen or fabricated identities"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Once operational, mule accounts are scripted to move funds through automated smurfing (footnote: breaking transfers into smaller amounts) to convert criminal proceeds to cryptocurrency or send assets through mixers and tumblers that obfuscate transaction trails. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "mule accounts are scripted to move funds through automated smurfing19"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Europol estimates that 90 per cent of mule activity is linked to cybercrime. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Europol estimates that 90 per cent of mule activity is linked to cybercrime"</sub>

## Automation in online scams

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Southeast Asia is a hotspot for large-scale online scams including investment fraud, romance scams and call centre-based extortion. Though they often involve human interaction, their infrastructure is increasingly automated: syndicates run scam centres combining bulk messaging systems, auto-dialers and VoIP software; scam call centres use auto-dialing to deliver thousands of automated calls daily, with victims hearing pre-recorded messages or being routed to live scammers. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "scam centres that combine bulk messaging systems, auto-dialers, and VoIP software to target victims at scale"</sub>

## Malware distribution at scale

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Banking trojans such as Zeus and TrickBot use web injects to change what victims see on online banking pages, so that victims disclose one-time passwords or PINs, which attackers then use to make fraudulent transactions from the victim's device. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Banking trojans like Zeus and TrickBot use these scripts to alter what users see on online banking sites"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The brief describes a wider shift toward service-based cybercrime, including Malware-as-a-Service and Ransomware-as-a-Service, in which automated components are developed and sold through underground markets. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "service-based cybercrime models, such as Malware-as-a-Service (MaaS) and Ransomware-as-a-service (RaaS)"</sub>

## Automated money laundering

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Money mules are a key enabler of cyber-enabled laundering: they are individuals whose bank accounts are used to move and disguise the source of illegal funds. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "individuals whose bank accounts are used to transfer and obscure the origin of illegal funds"</sub>

## Automation in online scams

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: In countries such as Singapore, Malaysia and Thailand, automated calls impersonating police or immigration authorities are commonly used to initiate scams; a human operator is involved only when a target engages, letting small teams reach vast numbers of people. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "automated calls impersonating police or immigration authorities are commonly used to initiate scams"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: On social media, bots distribute links to fraudulent investment platforms, and romance scams often begin with automated outreach before human scammers take over. Inside scam centres, trafficked workers use scripted templates and CRM-like dashboards to manage multiple conversations, with automated tools tracking victim profiles and sending preset messages; scam websites are preprogrammed to display fake account balances or stall withdrawals. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "trafficked workers use scripted templates and customer relationship management (CRM)-like dashboards"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Figure 2 (smishing using a SIM box) shows a flow from SIM box farm through an online gateway to scam messages (for example a bank alert prompting a link click), with links also placed on social media leading to a fake platform and fake website tied to the scam centre. A map figure shows known or reported cyberfraud compounds in Myanmar (including Shwe Kokko and Myawaddy) and nighttime illumination change around Myawaddy between 2021 and 2024, based on ASPI visualization (navy blue = increase, red = decrease). (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The change in nighttime illumination around Myawaddy between 2021 and 2024, with specific scam centre locations annotated"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Smishing operations use SIM box farms and online gateways to send thousands of scam text messages each minute. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "SIM box farms and online gateways are used to dispatch thousands of scam messages per minute"</sub>

## Malicious use of AI powering illicit activities

- **descriptive / context**: In Southeast Asia, described as a testing ground for emerging criminal methodologies, AI tools are accelerating speed, sophistication and reach of cyber-enabled crime; criminals embed AI to generate deepfakes, fabricate synthetic identities, create adaptive malware and circumvent security mechanisms. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "generate deepfakes, fabricate synthetic identities, create adaptive malware, and circumvent security mechanisms"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Deepfakes are AI-generated images, videos or audio produced through advanced machine learning that create highly convincing but fabricated portrayals of individuals; they require a large number of photos or recordings of a person to model. Though typically aimed at celebrities, improved technology and social media content have increased targeting of everyday people. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Deepfakes require a large number of photos or recordings of a person to model and create content"</sub>

## Deepfakes

- **descriptive / case example**: Early 2024 incident: a finance worker in Hong Kong, China was deceived into transferring US $25 million (HK $200 million) in a deepfake case. The employee of a British multinational design and engineering company received a phishing email apparently from the CFO requesting a secret transaction; though suspicious, the worker was convinced after a video call with apparent CFO and colleagues, and the company later confirmed fake voices and images were used. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a finance worker based in Hong Kong, China was deceived into transferring US $25 million"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In March 2025 a Singapore-based finance director was contacted over WhatsApp by scammers impersonating the CFO, asked to join a Zoom meeting where deepfake technology impersonated the CFO, and deceived into transferring US $494,000 to a Hong Kong, China bank account controlled by the scammers. The scammers also moved the meeting time forward and forced the victim to sign a non-disclosure agreement to stop them talking to other employees. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "deceived into transferring US $494,000 into a bank account registered in Hong Kong, China controlled by the scammers"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Voice cloning enables emotionally manipulative schemes such as 'fake kidnapping' scams in which synthetic voices impersonate family members in distress; in one case perpetrators cloned a teenage girl's voice from public videos to extort ransom from her mother. A cyber security company survey found one in four participants had experienced an AI voice cloning scam or knew someone who had, and in January 2025 the then Thai Prime Minister warned of an attempted voice cloning scam using her likeness. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "one in four participants had experienced an AI voice cloning scam or knew someone who had"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The brief reports a rise in criminal exploitation of deepfake technology in Southeast Asia, particularly in business email compromise and impersonation scams. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Southeast Asia has witnessed a rise in criminal exploitation of deepfake technologies"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Since the early 2024 Hong Kong case, other similar deepfake scams have been reported throughout Southeast Asia. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Since the reported case in early 2024 other similar scams have been reported throughout Southeast Asia"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: AI tools have lowered barriers to such techniques: most voice cloning platforms were free or low-cost with minimal verification; in many cases only 20 seconds of audio sufficed for a convincing clone, and most services required no consent statement. Social media provides a vast pool of audio and visual data that can be scraped to train generative models. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "only 20 seconds of audio was sufficient to generate a convincing voice clone"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Recent studies found around 98 per cent of all deepfake content online was sexually explicit and non-consensual, with 99 per cent of such content targeting women. Criminals produce fake sexual imagery from public photographs and threaten distribution unless a ransom is paid (AI-driven sextortion); law enforcement including the FBI note a marked increase in such reports, victims often unaware until they receive threats or demands. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "around 98 per cent of all deepfake content online was sexually explicit and non-consensual"</sub>

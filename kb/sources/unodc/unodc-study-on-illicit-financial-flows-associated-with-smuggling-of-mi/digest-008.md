## 3.5. IFFs associated with Income from Exploitation

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Higher profits tend to be made in the exploitation phase because revenue flows continuously rather than as a one-off, and payments from customers to exploiters and profits passed to other network members may constitute IFFs. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Higher profits tend to be made in the exploitation phase, as the flow of revenue is continuous rather than one-off."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Customer payments to an exploiter may also be IFFs where, for example, in sexual exploitation in tourist regions the exploiter is a resident and the customer a non-resident. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the exploiter is a resident and the customer is a non-resident."</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Combining the estimated number of victims with the prices at which victims and their services are sold gives a general understanding of traffickers' income, and cross-border income indicates the potential size of IFFs. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "When used in combination with the estimated number of victims, the prices at which a victim and their services"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Prices obtained for exploited services and products depend largely on the form of exploitation and the market in which transactions take place. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "depends largely on the form of exploitation and the market in which the transactions take place."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The most profitable exploitation is that which lets traffickers sell services repeatedly; trafficking for forced labour and sexual exploitation are the most common forms and yield repeated profits. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Te most profitable forms of exploitation are those that allow traffickers to sell such services repeatedly."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: An Iraqi law enforcement official described a case in which two women were sexually exploited over time, generating $200 per day for their exploiter. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "two women were sexually exploited over a period of time, generating $200 per day for their exploiter."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In trafficking for organ removal, crime proceeds can be very high although each organ may be sold only once; in one case a kidney was sold for $6,300. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a kidney was sold for $6,300."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Studies show sexual exploitation in prostitution can yield a return on investment from 100 per cent to 1,000 per cent; even in less profitable agricultural labour in India an exploited labourer can produce over 50 per cent profit. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Studies show that sexual exploitation in prostitution can result in a return on investment ranging from 100 per cent to 1,000 per cent."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Cross-border trafficking and cross-border sale of exploited products and services are more lucrative than domestic ones for both sexual and labour exploitation, so the IFFs generated are significant. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "cross-border trafficking and cross-border sale of products and services provided through exploitative labour are more lucrative than domestic trafficking and domestic sales."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In Pakistan brick kilns in 2020, a labour exploitation victim earned on average about PKR 960 (about US$6.28) for producing 1,000 bricks, and roughly half was retained by the exploiter under a debt bondage scheme. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the average salary of a labour exploitation victim was around PKR 960 (about US$6.28) for producing 1,000 bricks."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In the same year local news reported kilns selling 1,000 bricks for RS 14,000 (about US$186) or higher, with likely much higher mark-ups after export, generating substantial IFFs even after operating costs. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "kil-ns were selling 1,000 bricks for RS 14,000 (about US$186) or higher"</sub>

## CHAPTER 4 Value Transfer Systems related to Smuggling and Trafficking IFFs

- **descriptive / definition**: Hawala is an informal money exchange system in which money moves without any actual movement of cash, operating outside traditional banking as an alternative remittance channel. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "in which money is transferred without any actual movement of cash. It is an alternative remittance channel that exists outside of traditional banking systems."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Hawala transactions between brokers rely heavily on trust, often leave no paper trail and are therefore very difficult to monitor. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Transactions between hawala brokers are heavily based on trust and often do not have a paper trail, thus making it very difficult to monitor."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Value transfer methods for IFFs in smuggling or trafficking from GLO.ACT partner countries include cash, hawala and, to a lesser extent, money transfer service providers. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "from GLO.ACT partner countries include cash, hawala, and, to a lesser extent, money transfer service providers."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Afghans being smuggled often pay an upfront deposit to a third-party guarantor who pays the smuggling fee in full or in instalments on reaching milestones; the hawala system is used often by Afghans. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Before departure, Afghans who are being smuggled leave an upfront deposit with a third-party guarantor"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: In unsuccessful smuggling, part of the fee is refunded to the client's family via the hawaladar; on success the client confirms arrival and instructs release of money to the smuggler. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "If the smuggling is unsuccessful, part of the fee is refunded to the smuggling client’s family via the hawaladar."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Hawala is commonly used for financial transactions within, to and from Afghanistan and neighbouring and other South, Central and West Asian and African countries, and provides access to financial services for some who could not otherwise access them. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is commonly used for a range of financial transactions within, to, and from Afghanistan"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: In 2017 only an estimated 15 per cent of Afghanistan's population (seven per cent of females and 23 per cent of males) had access to a bank account or mobile-money-service provider. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "only an estimated 15 per cent of the population (seven per cent of females and 23 per cent of males) have access to a bank account"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The hawala system relies on trust and reputation to avoid the risk of defaulting on payment requests, and hawaladars may act as trustees or guarantors on licit and illicit transactions and offer credit. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Hawaladars (brokers) may act as trustees or guarantors on licit and illicit transactions, and may offer credit."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: FinTRACA reported improved registration of hawaladars and increasing notifications of suspected money laundering cases. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "reported an improvement in registration of hawaladars and increasing notifications of suspected cases of money laundering."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report's footnote states that the observation on Afghan hawala licensing and AML/CFT rules predates the Taliban takeover of August 2021. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This observation predates the Taliban takeover in August 2021."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Hawala has historically not been regulated, but there has been a push for regulation in recent years. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "While the system historically has not been regulated, there has been a push for regulation in recent years."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Formal money transfer services are less commonly used, but higher-level smuggling organisers, who may be based in third countries, are more likely to use them. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Formal money transfer services are less commonly used. Higher-level smuggling organizers, who may be based in third countries, are more likely to use such services."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In a 2012 French case, a smuggling network running routes from I.R. Iran to northern Europe used specialised money transfer providers or cash couriers for client payments and inter-smuggler transfers. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Payments made by clients to smugglers and value transfers between smugglers were made by specialized money transfer service providers, or via cash couriers."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Another predominant payment method for smuggling is direct cash payment. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Another predominant method of payment for smu-ggling is a direct cash payment."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A young Afghan man interviewed by UNODC in Bosnia and Herzegovina said smugglers accept only cash. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A young Afghan man interviewed by UNODC in Bosnia and Herze-govina indicated that smugglers only accept cash."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Payments through formal money transfer services are sometimes made in small amounts to avoid detection. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Payments using formal money transfer services are sometimes made in small amounts to avoid detection."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In a French case, an Afghan smuggler received payments from clients in Afghanistan and Pakistan ranging from €100 to €300 each. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "small amounts ranging from €100 to €300."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The French case payments predate the entry into force of Pakistan's AML/CFT regime, which the excerpt links to completing action plans and removal from the FATF grey list. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "with the completion of its action plans and Pakistan’s removal from the Financial Action Taskforce (FATF) grey list"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Bosnian prosecutors described family payments routed through hawala or money transfer providers under pre-agreed stage-by-stage schemes, with foreign organisers recruiting local transporters and coordinating meetings and amounts by mobile phone. (p. 62)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The payment system by family members usually goes through the hawala system"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Key Finding 15: high-level organisers of smuggling and trafficking may be based in origin, transit, destination or other countries and may manage illicit income remotely via wire or bank transfers, informal systems or cash couriers. (p. 62)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "They may manage the illicit income, costs, and proceeds remotely, through wire or banking institution transfers"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Key Finding 16: for GLO.ACT partner country smuggling and trafficking, IFFs consist of cash, hawala and, to a lesser extent, money transfer providers; most cases use methods that are not easily traceable, posing challenges for the response. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the majority of cases involve payment and transfer methods that are not so easily traceable."</sub>

## CHAPTER 5 IFFs associated with Proceeds of TIP and SOM

- **descriptive / limitation**: Information on what traffickers and smugglers do with profits is rather limited; little evidence shows traffickers operating in GLO.ACT partner countries investing in assets, possibly due to methodological limitations. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Little of the evidence gathered and analysed suggests that traffickers and smugglers operating physically in GLO.ACT partner countries participate in any asset investment"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Criminal organisations may use cash to buy appreciating assets such as real estate and luxury goods, invest in the financial sector or fund other illegal activities; asset investment is generally used by high-level perpetrators operating from third countries. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Nevertheless, asset invest-ment is generally employed by high-level perpetrators organizing operations from a third country."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Perpetrators in Europe tend to launder proceeds through cash-intensive businesses such as money service businesses, real estate and high-value goods, according to key informant interviews. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "perpetrators in Europe tend to use cash-intensive businesses,"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Smugglers and traffickers from GLO.ACT partner countries operating in Europe use illicit income in three ways: sending money back to origin countries for investment in legal businesses or real estate; funding a lavish lifestyle; or reinvesting in other criminal or legitimate activities in the destination country. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Smugglers and traffickers from GLO.ACT partner countries operating in Europe use illicit income in three main ways"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Reports indicate cash from trafficking in and from Afghanistan is deposited in and laundered through poorly regulated financial institutions worldwide, notably in Western European and Middle Eastern banking systems; some IFFs flow inward to Afghanistan, which is particularly difficult to track. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "deposited in and laundered through poorly regulated financial institutions throughout the world"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Perpetrators may deposit money in banks using a common mobile phone number, address, and accounts in different names. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "perpetrators may deposit money in banks using a common mobile phone number, address, and accounts in different names."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Traffickers and smugglers frequently transfer money to 'risk' countries. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "frequently transfer money to “risk” countries"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Other indicators include unusual withdrawals, deposits or wire activity inconsistent with normal business practices. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "have unusual withdrawals, deposits, or wire activity inconsistent with normal business practices"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Another indicator is dramatic and unexplained changes in account activity. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "dramatic and unexplained changes in account activity"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: An Iraqi law enforcement officer reported that traffickers often invest exploitation profits in cash purchases of cafés, roulette and gambling rooms, spas and nightclubs, as depositing in banks is far less common in Iraq due to poor banking systems. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it was not uncommon for traffickers to invest profits from exploiting victims in cash purchases of cafés"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Key Finding 17: smugglers and traffickers use criminal proceeds in three ways, and each of these ways may generate IFFs and often involve some form of money laundering. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Smugglers and traffickers use criminal proceeds in three ways"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Each of the three ways of using proceeds may generate IFFs and often involves some form of money laundering. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Each of these ways may generate IFFs and often involve some form of money laundering."</sub>

## 6.1. Illicit Financial Flows associated with Smuggling of Migrants and Trafficking in Persons

- **expected / recommendation**: International cooperation must include partner, transit, destination and relevant third countries; IFF investigations into and out of all these countries need to be undertaken proactively, including through joint or parallel financial investigations, and be multi-sectoral with private sector involvement. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "investigations of IFFs flowing into and out of origin, transit, destination and third countries need to be undertaken proactively."</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: The legislative framework must be strengthened: national law should define and criminalise participation in an organised criminal group with tougher penalties for organised crime involvement in trafficking and smuggling. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "National legislation should specifically define and criminalize participation in an organized criminal group."</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Where smuggling of migrants is not specifically criminalised, it is essential to criminalise it by ratifying and domestically implementing the Protocol against the Smuggling of Migrants by Land, Sea and Air. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is essential to do so, by ratifying and domestically implementing the Protocol against the Smuggling of Migrants by Land, Sea and Air"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Criminal justice actors need better understanding of how digital tools, social media, encryption, cryptocurrency and the darkweb are used for trafficking and smuggling; OSINT and specialised training on detecting online crime and collecting electronic evidence are essential. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Vigilant intelligence gathering on the developments and trends of such technology in IFFs, including open-source intelligence (OSINT), is an essential measure"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: National responses to illicit financial flows from trafficking and smuggling must adopt a human rights-based and gender-sensitive approach. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "must adopt a human rights-based and gender-sensitive approach"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Trafficking in persons and migrant smuggling should be included as predicate offences under money laundering criminal provisions. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Trafficking in persons and migrant smuggling should be included as predicate offences under money laundering criminal provisions"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Such provisions must enable seizure of assets used to commit these offences and confiscation of proceeds of the crime. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "such provisions must enable both seizure of assets used to commit these offences and confiscation of proceeds of the crime."</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Web- and mobile-based tools used by criminals include digital applications for navigation, encryption, and locking or erasing device contents. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "digital applications for navigation, encryption, locking and erasing contents on a device"</sub>

## 6.2. Smuggling of Migrants

- **mandatory / requirement**: Under Article 10 of UNTOC, liability of legal persons must be established by law, without prejudice to the criminal liability of the natural persons who committed the offences; legal persons must not shield natural persons from liability. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "liability of legal persons must be established by law, without prejudice to the criminal liability of the natural persons"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Preventive measures such as expanding income generation opportunities must be scaled up in communities near migrant smuggling hotspots in origin and transit countries, as lack of alternative livelihoods helps explain opportunistic smugglers. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Preventive measures (e.g., expanding income generation opportunities) must be scaled up in communities"</sub>

## 6.3. Trafficking in Persons

- **expected / recommendation**: National authorities investigating trafficking and smuggling should be trained to identify indicators of scenarios, modalities and actor types that generate higher illicit income, to better detect corresponding IFFs. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "national authorities should be trained in identifying the indicators in the scenarios, modalities and types of actors"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The South Asia subregion convicts fewer trafficking perpetrators relative to its population than most regions, so investigation and prosecution of internal trafficking should increase; internal labour trafficking for internationally sold goods may still generate significant IFFs. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Te South Asia subregion convicts fewer perpetrators of trafficking in persons in proportion to its overall population"</sub>

## 6.2. Smuggling of Migrants

- **expected / recommendation**: Cooperation should include proactive, intelligence-driven investigations of loosely connected and less hierarchical providers based in known hubs. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Cooperation should include proactive, intelligence-driven investigations targeting loosely connected and less hierarchical providers"</sub>

## 6.3. Trafficking in Persons

- **expected / requirement**: There is a need to strengthen law enforcement capacity to identify, investigate and prosecute illicit financial flows linked to trafficking and smuggling. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is a need to strengthen law enforcement capacity to identify, investigate and prosecute illicit financial flows"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: According to available statistics, seizures and confiscation of assets are low. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "According to available statistics, seizures and confiscation of assets are low"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The UNTOC provisions stipulate that proceeds of crime or property must be confiscated and can be used to compensate victims. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "despite the UNTOC provisions stipulating that proceeds of crime or property must be confiscated"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Financial investigations provide evidence of the offence, deprive the trafficker or smuggler of financial motivation, and allow compensation for victims. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "deprive the trafficker or smuggler of his/her financial motivation, and allow direct and indirect compensation for victims"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Lack of robust national responses to internal trafficking can lead to unchecked proliferation of such crimes and fuel organized criminal groups. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Lack of robust national responses to internal trafficking can lead to unchecked proliferation of such crimes"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: UNODC Global Reports on Trafficking in Persons have found women and girls make up the majority of detected victims; female victims face physical or extreme violence at three times the rate of males in court cases. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "female victims are subjected to physical or extreme violence at the hands of their traffickers at a rate three times higher than males."</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Special gender-focused attention is needed on IFFs from trafficking for sexual and labour exploitation, and more research is needed to quantify income from labour exploitation, which is often shrouded in legitimacy. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Special attention, especially from a gender perspective, must be given to strengthening the understanding"</sub>

## 6.4. Value Transfer Systems and Illicit Income

- **descriptive / finding or result**: During data collection, investigations of trafficking cases generally did not include financial investigations in practice, although such evidence would improve investigations and reach organisers who profit most. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "investigations of trafficking cases generally do not include financial investigations in practice."</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Countries should provide specialised training on IFFs for police, investigators, prosecutors and judges, adapt legislation to strengthen asset management and recovery agencies, compensation funds and databases, improve international cooperation through parallel or joint investigations, and strengthen cooperation with the private sector. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Provide specialized training for relevant actors (e.g., police, investigators, prosecutors, judges) on illicit financial flows;"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Effective monitoring and investigative mechanisms must be put in place to identify and act against criminal abuse of hawala and other cash-based value transfer systems, without penalising their legitimate use. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Effective monitoring and investigative mechanisms must be put in place to identify, monitor, and take action"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Criminal justice institutions need to strengthen their understanding of how value transfer systems move illicit gains, but measures should not penalise the legitimate use of hawala, an established way of doing business in many partner countries. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "any measures taken should not penalize the legitimate use of these systems"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: There is a need for closer government and private sector cooperation, especially with value transfer providers, possibly via an enabling legal provision, a framework or designated focal points, with MSBs and social media platforms having an important role. (p. 69)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Tis may entail enactment of an enabling legal provision or establishment of a framework for such cooperation"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Awareness-raising and training for private and public stakeholders should cover financial disruption, transnational cash-courier networks, OSINT and red flag indicators of trafficking and smuggling. (p. 69)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Awareness raising initiatives and training for the private and public sector stakeholders should also take place"</sub>

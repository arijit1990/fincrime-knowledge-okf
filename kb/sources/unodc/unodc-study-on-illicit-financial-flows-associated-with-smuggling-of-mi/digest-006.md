## 2.4. IFFs associated with Smuggling Income

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Europol and Interpol state that more than 90 per cent of irregular entries into the EU are facilitated by smugglers, so the study estimates nearly 166,000 of the detected attempts were smuggling incidents. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "more than 90 per cent of irregular entries into the EU are facilitated by smugglers"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Bangladeshi migrants were quoted EUR 2,000 for the trip from Iran to Greece, but on arrival in Istanbul, Türkiye, the smuggler demanded another EUR 4,000 for onward travel to Greece. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the cost of the trip from I.R. of Iran to Greece would be €2,000"</sub>

  <sub>anchor: "This compares to 26,628 refusals of entry at air borders during 2020, and 62,859 in 2019"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: From 2018 to 2021 the Eastern Mediterranean Sea route was the route most commonly used by people from GLO.ACT partner countries to enter the EU irregularly. (p. 42)[^original]

## COVID-19 AND IFFS ASSOCIATED WITH SMUGGLING OF MIGRANTS

- **descriptive / finding or result**: COVID-19 border closures, visa restrictions and immigration controls shifted smuggling demand and smugglers' modus operandi, often increasing demand due to fewer economic opportunities at origin and tighter entry and visa rules at destination. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Closed borders, visa restrictions and increased immigration controls all caused shifts in both smuggling demand"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In field research in 2020 covering 1,419 people using smugglers in four regions, half of respondents said smugglers' fees had increased since the pandemic began. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "covering 1,419 people using smugglers in four regions, half of all respondents noted that smugglers"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Also prior to the pandemic, more dangerous routes resulted in smugglers charging higher fees. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Also prior to the pandemic, more dangerous routes resulted in smugglers charging higher fees."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Smugglers were often obliged to pay fees to criminal groups and law enforcement to move people through territories they had not used for transit before the pandemic. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Smugglers were often obliged to pay fees to criminal groups and law enforcement in order to smuggle people"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Over 60 per cent of respondents in the same study said smugglers had started using more dangerous routes since the pandemic began; border closures and surveillance often led to riskier routes. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "over 60 per cent of respondents stated that since the beginning of the pandemic, smugglers have started using more dangerous routes"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: People fleeing conflict and persecution cannot postpone or cancel migration as others may, and desperation can lead to abuse, exploitation, trafficking and use of costlier, riskier smuggling. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "people fleeing conflict and persecution do not have that option"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Higher smuggler prices are likely to increase 'pay as you go' or 'travel now pay later' payment structures, under which clients may be forced to work en route or at destination to repay debts, increasing their vulnerability to exploitation. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Higher prices are likely to engender an increase in “pay as you go” or “travel now pay later” payment structures."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Higher fees would increase IFFs, whereas reduced smuggler availability and migration controls should have the opposite effect; however, stricter border controls could make clients attempt crossings far more often, raising smuggler revenue. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "stricter border controls could lead smuggling clients to make far more attempts than previously"</sub>

## 2.5. IFFs associated with Smuggling Costs

- **descriptive / context**: Smuggling costs include transportation (drivers, fuel, vehicle purchase, rental and maintenance), accommodation, payments to guides and associates, equipment for forging documents, and bribes, and vary by route, origin, destination, services and transport mode. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Costs incurred in committing smuggling offences include, among others, transportation"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Because many smuggling costs and associates' 'salaries' involve cross-border transactions, paying these costs generates significant IFFs. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the payment of costs by smugglers also generates significant IFFs."</sub>

## Case 8: SMUGGLING COSTS

- **descriptive / case example**: In a land smuggling case from Türkiye to Sofia, Bulgaria through Greece, the organizer of the group made between €10,000 and €13,000 per trip from four to five clients, and per trip spent €1,000 on a vehicle, €500 on a guide and €200 on the person who bought the vehicle. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the organizer of the smuggling group made between €10,000 and €13,000 per trip from a group of four to five clients"</sub>

## 2.5. IFFs associated with Smuggling Costs

- **descriptive / context**: Bosnia and Herzegovina prosecutors said smugglers mostly spend earnings on accommodation or food for other migrants and share earnings with helpers, with no information yet on purchases of real estate or cars. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "So far, we have not had information that they are buying real estate"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Smugglers lower costs and maximise profits by overcrowding boats and using old, cheap, poorly maintained vessels for transport from Türkiye to the EU. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Approaches such as overcrowding of boats used to smuggle people from Türkiye to the EU"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In a land smuggling case from Türkiye to Sofia, Bulgaria through Greece, the organizer of the group made EUR 10,000 to 13,000 per trip from four to five clients. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "made between €10,000 and €13,000 per trip from a group of four to five clients"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: A Bosnia and Herzegovina prosecutor reported that smugglers avoid losing their own vehicles to confiscation by renting cars from agencies or third parties, and some use cars without legal plates fitted with legal plates. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "If it was their own vehicle, that vehicle would surely be confiscated."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Europol revealed that documents stolen by pickpocketing groups in European tourist areas were modified and sold online by smugglers in Greece and Türkiye and posted to clients in various countries; this constitutes four distinct IFFs (inward and outward shipments and payments). (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "documents stolen by organized criminal pickpocketing groups in certain European tourist areas were modified and sold online"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A significant proportion of smuggling and trafficking cases remain undetected, making it difficult to estimate total costs, proceeds and cross-border IFF shares. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a significant proportion of cases remain undetected."</sub>

## KEY FINDINGS RELATING TO ILLICIT FINANCIAL FLOWS ASSOCIATED WITH SMUGGLING OF MIGRANTS

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Key Finding 5: IFFs associated with smuggling arise from cross-border transfers of migrant payments, smuggler cost expenses, 'salaries' between group members, and crime proceeds (for example invested or laundered). (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Crime proceeds issuing from smuggling of migrants, such as through investing or money laundering."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Key Finding 6 also notes that IFFs relating to smuggling costs vary greatly by route, origin, destination, services and transport mode. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "IFFs relating to the costs of committing the smuggling offences also vary greatly"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Key Finding 7: smugglers may operate seemingly legitimate travel and labour recruitment agencies, so smuggling proceeds can appear as legal business income, making IFFs harder to detect. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Smugglers may operate seemingly legitimate travel and labour recruitment agencies."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Key Finding 8: IFFs from comprehensive smuggling packages to distant destinations involve larger sums due to higher prices, and high-quality fraudulent document dealers can charge significant markups; these packages are less common because many clients cannot afford them. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "as such packages are inaccessible for many potential clients due to the high cost"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In the marketplace form of smuggling, competing smuggling services generate smaller-sum IFFs between origin countries, transit smuggling hubs and smugglers' centres of economic interest. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "IFFs involving smaller sums move between refugees’ and migrants’ countries of origin"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Key Finding 6: the volume and directions of IFFs associated with smuggling often depend on the scale at which smugglers operate. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The volume and directions of IFFs associated with smuggling often depend on the scale at which the smugglers operate."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Smuggling is committed by perpetrators ranging from local, independent and opportunistic smugglers to large, structured transnational crime organizations, and high-level organizers may be based in origin, transit or destination countries or elsewhere. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The high-level organizers of migrant smuggling may be based anywhere"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Small cash payments by clients to opportunistic actors in transit countries, where clients usually have their economic centre in origin countries, constitute IFFs on a very small scale. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these payments constitute IFFs albeit on a very small scale"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Key Finding 9: about 90 per cent of the 184,323 irregular entry attempts from the four partner countries (2018-2022) are estimated to be smuggling-facilitated, giving nearly 166,000 smuggling incidents; the Western Balkans and Central Mediterranean routes were most frequently identified in recent years. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The routes on which people from GLO.ACT partner countries have most frequently been identified in recent years"</sub>

## 3.1. Trafficking in persons context in GLO.ACT partner countries

- **descriptive / limitation**: Information on victim profiles and forms of exploitation is limited for the countries considered in the study. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Information on victim profiles and forms of exploitation is limited for the countries considered in this Study."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In South Asia, women (43%) and boys (24%) are the majority of detected victims, followed by men (20%) and girls (13%). (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "women (43%) and boys (24%) make up the majority of victims detected by the authorities"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Ninety-nine per cent of victims detected in South Asia and reported to UNODC were trafficked within their own country of citizenship, so the data suggests limited inward IFFs from TIP. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Ninety-nine per cent of all victims of trafficking detected in South Asia that were reported to UNODC were trafficked internally"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Indications of outward IFFs exist because the number of South Asian victims detected abroad (East Asia and the Pacific, Western and Southern Europe, North America) increased between 2017 and 2020. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "as an increasing number of victims from South Asian countries were detected in East Asia and the Pacific"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In the Middle East subregion including Iraq, women make up 87% of detected victims, men 12% and child victims 1%; forced labour and sexual exploitation are found in roughly equal proportions. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "women make up the vast majority of detected victims (87%)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Sixty-three per cent of foreign victims identified in the Middle East subregion were from other subregional countries, and 14 per cent were from East Asia. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Sixty-three per cent of foreign victims identified in the subregion were from other subregional countries"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The different trafficking actors, forms of exploitation and victim profiles crucially influence the size, modalities and directions of IFFs associated with trafficking in persons. (p. 49)[^original]

  <sub>anchor: "detected victims only represents a part of the picture since undetected victims may be trafficked to other destinations"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Internal trafficking can involve IFFs where payments and profits from trafficking are moved into and out of the country. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "where payments and profits related to committing trafficking offences are transferred into and out of the country"</sub>

  <sub>anchor: "families may use daughters as a tool to resolve conflict by giving a daughter to another family"</sub>

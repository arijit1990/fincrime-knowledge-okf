## 2.3. Payment Modalities

- **descriptive / context**: Payment for smuggling of migrants from GLO.ACT partner countries generally takes one of two forms: monetary payments from clients to smugglers, or labour connected to a debt from the smuggling fee, which may or may not be exploitative. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "monetary payments from clients to smugglers or labour connected to a debt from the smuggling fee"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Some smugglers offer a single payment fee that guarantees multiple attempts if the first attempt is unsuccessful. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Some smugglers offer one payment fee, under which they guarantee multiple attempts if the"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Although all smuggling of migrants involves a financial or other material benefit transferred between client and smuggler, the form of payment varies, which influences the amount, frequency and direction of associated illicit financial flows. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the form of this payment varies. Tis influences the amount, frequency, and direction of associated illicit financial flows."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The comprehensive package model, covering the whole journey from origin to destination for one payment including transport and border crossings, is the most common model for reaching more distant destinations quickly. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the _comprehensive package_ model is the most common.77 It covers the entirety of the journey"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Comprehensive packages require a high level of organisation and efficient networks, so they are generally provided by organised criminal groups at a very high price; upfront or destination-only payment may be requested. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is generally provided by organized criminal groups, for a very high price."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: High costs of certain full-package routes leave victims with large debts, making them susceptible to forced labour or forced prostitution. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the high cost of certain full package smuggling routes leads to victims incurring large debts"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Only sophisticated, well-organised transnational organised criminal groups are generally in a position to offer such comprehensive services. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In general, only sophisticated, well-organized transnational organized criminal groups are in a position to offer such services."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: IFFs associated with comprehensive packages involve large sums, especially payments by clients to the smuggling organiser, but such packages are inaccessible to many clients so this type of IFF is less common. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this type of smuggling-related IFF is less common."</sub>

## Case 4

- **descriptive / case example**: In a 2012 UK Court of Appeal case, an Iranian national was smuggled from Türkiye to the UK by several smugglers using air, train, accommodation and fraudulent documents, for an up-front fee of €12,000 paid to one arranger in Türkiye. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "arranged the entire journey for an up-front fee of €12,000"</sub>

## 2.3. Payment Modalities

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The marketplace smuggling model involves competing smugglers offering services at hubs such as Istanbul and Izmir in Türkiye, actively seeking clients, and hubs also disseminate information on routes and prices. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "At key smuggling hubs, such as Istanbul and Izmir in Türkiye, services are offered by competing smugglers"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In marketplace smuggling, IFFs involving smaller sums move between clients' countries of origin, transit hubs, and centres of economic interest of smuggling actors. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In these cases, IFFs involving smaller sums move between refugees’ and migrants’ countries of origin"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: An Iraqi woman interviewed identified cafés in a district of Istanbul as a key hub for smuggling activity to the EU, and an Iraqi man described a similar café arrangement in Izmir offering papers and boats. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identified cafés in a certain district in the city of Istanbul, Türkiye, as a key hub for smuggling activity to the EU"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: In many cases clients make small cash payments for individual services to opportunistic actors who supplement other income with smuggling or engage in it ad hoc; this is common in transit countries, and because clients' economic centre is in origin countries these payments are small-scale IFFs. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these payments constitute IFFs, but on a very small scale."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The most expensive comprehensive package from GLO.ACT partner countries combines air and land transport. Beyond transportation, comprehensive packages also include accommodation at transit points and potentially fraudulent travel or residence documents. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In addition to various forms of transportation, comprehensive packages also include accommodation at the various transit points"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Comprehensive packages for air travel are most commonly bought by Iranians and to a lesser extent by other groups. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Comprehensive packages for air travel are most commonly purchased by Iranians"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The services smugglers offer often depend on the perceived wealth of potential clients, and in some cases smugglers act as job brokers or offer accommodation. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Te services offered by smugglers often depend on the perceived wealth of the potential clients."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Investigating and prosecuting smuggling cases is complex because some individuals unknowingly aid smuggling while others are aware but turn a blind eye, so proving criminal intent is a persistent challenge. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Proving the criminal intent of an actor is thus a persistent challenge in criminal investigations and prosecution."</sub>

## Case 5

## 2.4. IFFs associated with Smuggling Income

- **descriptive / finding or result**: An Iraqi woman said her family paid €9,000 for one family member to be smuggled from Türkiye to Denmark by sea and land, while humanitarian aid workers in Iraq put the price per person from Türkiye to Europe at around US$8,000. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "paid a total of €9,000 for one family member to be smuggled from Türkiye to Denmark"</sub>

## Case 6

- **descriptive / case example**: In an older Austrian case reported in 2006, Bangladeshi clients paid $8,500 to $11,000 each to be smuggled by air into the EU via the UAE, Russia and Ukraine; the group smuggled about 900 people over three years and made an estimated $7.65 to $9.9 million in the early 2000s. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The organized criminal group smuggled approximately 900 people into the EU over a three-year period."</sub>

## 2.4. IFFs associated with Smuggling Income

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Information on air smuggling fees is limited, but air appears to be the most expensive mode from GLO.ACT partner countries to Europe, generally costing between US$12,000 and $18,000 per person. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "generally costing between US$12,000 and $18,000 per person."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Smuggling by sea or land tends to be cheaper than by air; in a 2014 Italian case, Afghans, Iraqis and Pakistanis paid US$6,000 to US$6,500 for boat passage from Türkiye to Italy. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "paid between US$6,000 and US$6,500 to be smuggled by boat from Türkiye to Italy."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Comprehensive land and sea packages from Pakistan to Greece are estimated at around US$4,000, and an Iraqi man paid €2,000 for a trip in a plastic boat from Izmir to Greece. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An Iraqi man interviewed for this Study indicated that the trip from Izmir, Türkiye, to Greece in a plastic boat cost him €2,000."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Organised criminal groups charge higher fees for more sophisticated services and can generate far larger IFFs than individual actors. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Organized criminal groups typically charge higher fees, as their smuggling services are more sophisticated."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In 2019, French and Dutch authorities dismantled a network that smuggled about 10,000 Afghans, Iranians, Iraqis and Syrians from Le Mans and Poitiers to the UK by lorry and ferry, usually in groups of 20; clients paid up to €7,000 and the organisation earned around €70 million. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "clients paid up to €7,000, and the organization earned around €70 million."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Children are charged higher fees because smuggling them is more difficult; in 2010 smugglers reportedly charged US$7,000 to US$20,000 to smuggle a child from Afghanistan to Europe, although the information is dated. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "in 2010, smugglers charged between US$7,000 and $20,000 to smuggle a child from Afghanistan to Europe."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Children are far less commonly detected irregularly entering the EU, so IFFs associated with smuggling children are less frequent, though they may involve larger amounts. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the IFFs associated with smuggling children are less frequent, though they may involve larger amounts."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Country-specific factors such as conflict also affect smuggling fees; an Iraqi humanitarian aid worker said prices are typically doubled or raised during a crisis, rising from about $100 to $150 or $200 per person. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "then the prices get automatically doubled or get higher."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Smugglers dealing in high-quality fraudulent documents can charge a significant mark-up; in 2019 Greek authorities detected a group providing fraudulent travel documents earning up to €13,000 per client. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "earning up to €13,000 per client."</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Figure 8 gives 2021 fraudulent document prices: for Iraqi clients a visa presumably to enter Türkiye at $1,130 and a foreign passport at approximately $1,000; for Afghan clients a Turkish visa at $2,800 per adult and $1,400 per child; for Pakistani clients a fraudulent passport plus airline ticket bought in Greece at $4,000. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Fraudulent passport plus airline ticket (purchased in Greece,nationality not specified)|$4,000|"</sub>

## 2.5. Gross and net income of smuggling

- **descriptive / method**: Gross income from smuggling is calculated as the average fee paid multiplied by the number of smuggling incidents in a period such as a year; counts refer to incidents, not people, since one person may be smuggled several times. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Gross income = average fee for being smuggled x number of smuggling incidents."</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Net income is gross income minus intermediate expenditure, which is calculated as average costs borne by smugglers per client multiplied by the number of smuggling incidents. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Net income = Gross income – (average expenditure x number of smuggling incidents)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Global evidence indicates that in 2016 at least 2.5 million smuggling incidents occurred, generating a profit of US$5.5 to $7 billion. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "at a minimum, 2.5 million smuggling incidents took place, generating a profit of US$5.5-$7 billion in 2016."</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Estimating IFFs from GLO.ACT partner countries to the EU requires estimating gross income, costs, salaries transferred and crime proceeds processed, the cross-border proportion of transactions and the direction of flows. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the gross income for smuggling, costs covered, “salaries” transferred, and crime proceeds processed must be estimated."</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The number of smuggling incidents is estimated as a proportion of detections of people from GLO.ACT partner countries attempting irregular entry into the EU, with most assumed to have used smugglers. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Te number of smuggling incidents is a proportion of the number of times people from GLO.ACT partner countries were detected attempting to irregularly enter the EU"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Figure 9 estimates gross income for January 2015 to May 2021: the single passage Türkiye to Greece by land and sea was $918 million total ($153 million annual average); the complete passage Türkiye to the Western Balkans by land and sea was $438 million total ($73 million annual average). (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Single passage from Türkiye to Greece(land and sea)|$918 million|$153 million|"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Routes were matched to those used by Frontex for Afghan, Iraqi and Pakistani detections in January 2015 to May 2021, assuming all detected people used and paid for smuggling, although only a high proportion actually did. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "For these calculations, it is assumed that all those detected attempting irregular entry to the EU used and paid for smuggling services"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The Türkiye to Greece estimate uses 487,906 detections and an average fee of $1,881 from 22 interviews and cases, giving total gross income of $917,751,186. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "When 487,906 is multiplied by 1,881, the total gross income for all smuggling along the route is $917,751,186."</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The Western Balkans estimate uses 122,885 land detections on Türkiye to Serbia, Bosnia and Herzegovina and Bulgaria, with an average fee of $3,564 from seven interviews and cases, giving $437,962,140. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "When 122,885 is multiplied by 3,564, the total gross income for all smuggling along the route accounts for $437,962,140."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The calculations are rough estimates not to be considered precise or absolute, because information on route, mode, client and smuggler profiles is largely unavailable, so the figures are considered conservative. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These calculations are rough estimates and should not be considered as precise and absolute"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Limitations include the difficulty of determining what share of people from the three countries attempting entry were smuggled, undetected cases, and the fact that figures cover only land and sea entries, not air. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Firstly, it is difficult to determine what proportion of people from the three countries attempting irregular entry into the EU were smuggled."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Smuggling fees fluctuate with services, demand, logistical challenges and surveillance, and smugglers may add charges along the route or on arrival at the destination. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "smugglers may add additional charges along the route or once the destination is reached."</sub>

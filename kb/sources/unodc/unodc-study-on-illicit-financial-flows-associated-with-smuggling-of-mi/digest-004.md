## 2.2. Smuggling Actors

- **descriptive / definition**: The coordinator or organiser has overall responsibility for the smuggling operation, may direct, employ or subcontract others, assigns smugglers, selects route and transport, and arranges accommodation. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Te coordinator or organizer is the person who has the overall responsibility for the smuggling operation"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Recruiters advertise smuggling services and connect smugglers with prospective clients, often luring people by misinforming them about migration and destination conditions, and may collect initial transport fees. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Recruiters often lure such persons into illegal migration by deliberately misinforming them"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Spotters give information about police, border guard or army checks, travelling ahead and warning by mobile phone; enforcers safeguard the operation, possibly using threats or violence against migrants. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Spotters often travel ahead of the vehicle carrying the smuggled migrants"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Ad hoc service providers and suppliers are linked to smugglers and paid a share of proceeds, often working with several networks; boat owners or builders may be complicit. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Te ad hoc service providers and suppliers often have an established link with the smugglers"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Transporters or guides carry out the practical part of smuggling by accompanying migrants across one or more borders; they are often people from border regions with local knowledge, are the actors most likely to be intercepted, and play a crucial role in whether migrants cross successfully. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In many cases, transporters/guides are people from border regions who have good local knowledge."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: When intercepted while smuggling a group, transporters or guides often attempt to pass themselves off as one of the migrants, and because they are usually easy to recruit their loss does not necessarily affect the operation seriously. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they often attempt to pass themselves off as one of the migrants"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Collaborators include corrupt officials who may be bribed, forgers of documents, hotel owners, train conductors, taxi drivers, travel agents or airline staff; some facilitate unknowingly, others turn a blind eye. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Other individuals may include: forgers of passports, visas and other travel documents"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Low-level opportunistic smugglers generally work across one border or leg of the journey, while larger networks have a clear division of work across a broader area. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Low-level, opportunistic smugglers generally work across only one border or one leg of the journey"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In Afghanistan, initial payments to local smugglers referred by friends or relatives may not constitute IFFs, as they operate in the same locality as clients. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Accordingly, initial payments for services may not constitute IFFs."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Smugglers may operate seemingly legitimate travel or overseas labour recruitment agencies; resulting flows can be registered as legal income, making IFFs harder to detect, and a legitimate business front may record flows with legal funds. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial flows resulting from the smuggling activity can be registered as legal income"</sub>

## Case 2

- **descriptive / case example**: Case 2 (2006, UK): two men smuggled Bangladeshis into the UK under the guise of a 'law association', forging documents for visa applications at the British High Commission in Bangladesh or the UK Home Office. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Under the guise of being a “law association”, the two smugglers forged documents"</sub>

## 2.2. Smuggling Actors

- **descriptive / context**: Smuggling networks operating in the region are usually loosely connected, informal and not strictly hierarchical, and members of flexible networks can be easily replaced with little or no disruption. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Smuggling networks operating in the region are usually loosely connected, informal and not strictly hierarchical."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Less is known about smugglers in Iraq; based on cases analysed, both individual smugglers and organised criminal groups are involved, with many being local civilians transporting people to borders. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Less is known about smugglers in Iraq, but based on cases analysed for this Study"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: High-level organisers may be based in origin, transit or destination countries or elsewhere, managing income remotely via bank transfers, informal money transfer systems or cash couriers and paying salaries to lower-level actors. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Tey may manage income, costs and proceeds remotely, either through wire or banking institution transfers"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: If smuggling is transnational, IFFs may affect countries beyond origin, transit and destination, including where higher-level smugglers have their centre of economic interest; air smuggling is more often run by organised criminal groups. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "More organized criminal groups are involved in smuggling by air, due to the challenges involved."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In transit countries, origin-country smugglers often cooperate in loose, changing networks with transit-country nationals who know the routes. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "smugglers from origin countries often cooperate in loose networks with nationals of the transit countries"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Unaffiliated ad hoc smugglers receive relatively small payments, but these still generally constitute IFFs as they move between people in origin and transit countries such as West Asia or the Western Balkans. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Tese small sums, however, still generally constitute IFFs"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A Bosnian prosecutor described how smuggling groups may start small and grow more organised, with larger IFFs as demand increases, with participants often younger people drawn by quick money. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the group starts with a couple of people, and as the illegal activities grow, so does the number of people involved"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In Pakistan, reports describe smugglers operating behind the legal front of travel agencies and visa consultants, offering fraudulent documents, flights and accommodation; local smugglers connect clients to transit-country smugglers, so related payments are likely to generate IFFs. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Local smugglers then connect clients to other smugglers based in transit countries"</sub>

## Case 3

- **descriptive / case example**: Case 3 (Austria, 2014): eight Afghan, Indian and Pakistani men were accused of smuggling 278 Pakistanis into Austria over five months; clients paid €6,500 to €10,000 each, generating €3 million for the group, while the eight accused received only a small portion of profits. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a group of eight Afghan, Indian and Pakistani men were accused of smuggling 278 Pakistani people into Austria"</sub>

## 2.2. Smuggling Actors

- **descriptive / context**: Highly structured crime organisations in transit countries earn larger income because they include multiple actors across countries, and proceeds move between origin, transit, destination and other countries according to the smugglers' centres of economic interest. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Highly structured crime organizations also operate in transit countries along the route to Europe."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Family members of potential clients in origin countries may pay the organiser on behalf of the traveller, creating an outward IFF in their country of residence; a Bosnian prosecutor described organisers contacted by families in Afghanistan who negotiate price and persons. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Te organizer may also be contacted by family members of potential clients"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Group members communicate with the organiser at all times and may send a photo as proof of completed work before receiving remuneration. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may send a photo to prove the work is complete before receiving their remuneration"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Transporters, guides, spotters, accommodation providers and ad hoc service providers are active mainly in transit countries and their actions may constitute smuggling offences; fraudulent document providers and money laundering service providers have less direct client contact. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Other actors who have less direct interaction with smuggling clients include fraudulent document providers"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Document prices vary with quality and desirability of documents; providers either work with specific networks or collaborate loosely with multiple smugglers. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "with the price of the service varying according to the quality and desirability of the documents provided"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Law enforcement and government officials, including border guards, military, immigration, police, embassy and consular staff and port authorities, may receive bribes to facilitate smuggling, usually a fixed amount or share of proceeds. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Te bribe is usually a fixed amount or a share of the criminal proceeds."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Anecdotal information on bribery gathered for the Study suggested a lack of adequate detection and reporting of bribery. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "anecdotal information on bribery suggested a lack of adequate detection and reporting"</sub>

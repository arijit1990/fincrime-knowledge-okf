## 1.1. Illicit Financial Flows, Smuggling of Migrants, and Trafficking in Persons

- **descriptive / definition**: Illicit financial flows are financial flows that are illicit in origin, transfer or use, reflect an exchange of value and cross country borders. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "financial flows that are illicit in origin, transfer or use, that reflect an exchange of value and that cross-country borders"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Under SDG Target 16.4 the international community agreed to significantly reduce illicit financial flows, measured by indicator 16.4.1 (total value of inward and outward IFFs in current US dollars). (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "measured by indicator 16.4.1 (total value of inward and outward IFFs in current United States dollars)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: UNODC and UNCTAD, as custodian agencies of SDG indicator 16.4.1, jointly developed a statistical measurement framework and methodology for the indicator. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "worked jointly to develop a statistical measurement framework and methodology for this indicator"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Smuggling of migrants is defined in the SOM Protocol as procuring, to obtain directly or indirectly a financial or other material benefit, the illegal entry of a person into a State Party of which the person is not a national or permanent resident. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the procurement, in order to obtain, directly or indirectly, a financial or other material benefit"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The SOM Protocol also criminalises enabling irregular stay and document fraud offences when committed to smuggle migrants and for a financial or other material benefit. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Te SOM Protocol also criminalizes enabling irregular stay and document fraud offences"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: In the Study, the specific smuggling offence means facilitating illegal entry, while enabling irregular stay and document fraud are related offences; all three fall under SOM and are addressed together. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "All three fall under the overall offence of SOM and thus this Study addresses IFFs associated with all three types of smuggling offences."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Smugglers range from sophisticated multinational crime organisations to independent, opportunistic actors, and smugglers in GLO.ACT partner countries are located along this spectrum. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries are located somewhere along this spectrum from sophisticated multinational crime organizations"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Refugees and migrants who cannot migrate safely and legally often use smugglers, whose charges vary with distance, routes and risk of detection. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Smugglers charge various amounts for their services, depending on factors such as distance, routes, and the risk of detection."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Smuggling may involve large multinational organisations using international banking systems to transfer and launder money, or independent smugglers paid in cash by refugees and migrants. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it can also involve smugglers who work independently in return for cash payments from refugees and migrants"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: IFFs associated with smuggling arise from cross-border transfers of client payments, smugglers' expenses, salaries between group members, and crime proceeds from investment or laundering. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the IFFs associated with SOM arise from the cross-border transfer of"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Fig. 1 gives examples of smuggling IFFs by value direction: client to smuggler payments (including third-party payments), smuggler to smuggler salaries, smuggler to official bribes, and transfers or laundering of smuggling proceeds. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Payment made on behalf of client by third party, resident in any country"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Trafficking in persons is defined in the TIP Protocol as recruitment, transport, transfer, harbouring or receipt of persons by coercive, deceptive or abusive means, for the purpose of exploitation. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "recruitment, transportation, transfer, harboring or receipt of persons, by means of the threat or use of force"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Under the TIP Protocol, exploitation includes at a minimum the exploitation of prostitution or other sexual exploitation, forced labour or services, slavery or similar practices, servitude, or removal of organs. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Exploitation shall include, at a minimum, the exploitation of the prostitution of others"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: For adults, trafficking comprises the trafficking act, the means of control and the purpose of exploitation; for children the means element is not necessary. The list of exploitation forms is non-exhaustive. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The element of the means is not necessary to constitute the crime if the victims are children."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Different typologies of traffickers are active, including transnational crime organisations, smaller opportunistic associations and less sophisticated individuals; the amounts crossing borders depend on form of exploitation, phase and region. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "different typologies of traffickers and trafficking groups are active"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Exploitative services or products provided by the victim are generally the largest source of profit for traffickers. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Te exploitative services or products provided by the victim are generally the largest source of profit for traffickers."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: IFFs associated with trafficking include cross-border transfers of trafficking costs and payments (recruitment, transport, accommodation), costs of means of control such as drugs, costs of exploitation premises, income from exploitative services, income from sale of a person including forced marriage, and crime proceeds. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Costs and payments of the trafficking act, such as recruitment of trafficking victims and their transportation and accommodation"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Trafficker-side IFFs include transfers to accomplices as remuneration or cost coverage, bribes to foreign officials, salaries between traffickers across borders, transfers of trafficking proceeds, and laundering of proceeds between countries. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Value transferred as bribe to facilitate trafficking by trafficker resident in one country"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In 2020, 60 per cent of detected trafficking victims were reported as trafficked domestically, and in 2019 the proportion was 55 per cent, according to UNODC's Global Report on Trafficking in Persons 2022. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In 2020, 60 per cent of detected victims were reported as trafficked domestically"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Not all smuggled migrants become trafficking victims and not all trafficking victims are smuggled; domestic trafficking may have no IFFs, while cross-border cases may involve actors outside the country. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "not all smuggled migrants and refugees become victims of TIP and not all victims of TIP are smuggled migrants and refugees"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: IFFs arise when movement of finance, commodities or services across international borders is involved in committing SOM or TIP; affected countries may be origin, transit and destination countries or additional countries, and some IFFs relate to perpetrators of both. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "IFFs arise when the movement of finance, commodities or services across international borders is involved"</sub>

## Defining Illicit Financial Flows

- **descriptive / definition**: For statistical measurement, IFFs are financial flows illicit in origin, transfer or use, that reflect an exchange of value and cross country borders. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "For the purpose of statistical measurement, IFFs are defined as"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: A flow is illicit if illicitly generated, illicitly transferred or illicitly used, such as covering smuggling or trafficking costs; it may be legally generated, transferred or used but must be illicit in at least one aspect. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The flow can be legally generated, transferred or used, but it must be illicit in at least one of these aspects."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Exchange of value includes goods, services, and financial and non-financial assets; illicit cross-border bartering is also an IFF. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Exchange of value includes exchange of goods and services, and financial and non-financial assets."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: IFFs measure a flow of value over a given time rather than a stock, which is an accumulation of value. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "IFFs measure a flow of value over a given time, as opposed to a stock measure"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Flows crossing a border include assets whose ownership passes from a resident to a non-resident even if they stay in the same jurisdiction; residency follows an economic rather than legal definition. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the residency is thus independent of nationality or residence permit"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: IFFs linked to income generation are cross-border flows in providing services that directly generate illicit income; IFFs linked to income management use illicit income for investment or consumption. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "IFFs linked to income generation are cross-border flows that are performed in the context of the provision of services"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Illicit gross income is the value of illicit services or products provided by residents in a period; illicit intermediate expenditure is the value of inputs used to facilitate the crime, such as transportation. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Illicit intermediate expenditure refers to the value of inputs that smugglers or traffickers use to facilitate the crime"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Illicit net income equals gross income minus intermediate expenditure, the income earned by smugglers or traffickers after costs. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is the income earned by all smugglers or traffickers after subtracting the costs incurred."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The market size of SOM or TIP is the overall income generated by these criminal activities within a country during a defined period, typically a year, counting income of people whose centre of economic interest is in that country. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "describes the overall income generated by these criminal activities within a country during a defined period (typically a year)"</sub>

## 1.2. Methodology

- **descriptive / method**: The Study analyses available evidence on IFFs associated with SOM and TIP originating in GLO.ACT partner countries, with European countries as destinations. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "analyses the available evidence on illicit financial flows (IFFs) associated with smuggling of migrants"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Four focus group discussions were held with government, law enforcement and humanitarian participants in Afghanistan, Iraq and Pakistan. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Four focus group discussions were held with participants from government agencies, law enforcement, and humanitarian aid organizations"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The Study analysed 33 TIP and SOM cases submitted by 11 Member States to the SHERLOC Case Law Database; in total 176 cases from 16 countries were analysed. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Tis Study is also informed by an analysis of 33 TIP and SOM cases submitted by 11 Member States"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Field research findings were triangulated with official Member State submissions to UNODC on 404 trafficking victims and complemented by secondary literature. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "triangulated with data from official submissions by Member States to UNODC in relation to 404 victims of TIP"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: A total of 23 migrants and refugees (two women and twenty-one men) were interviewed in 2020-2021 in Bosnia and Herzegovina and Denmark, and in-depth 2019 interviews from the MACRO project in Albania, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Montenegro and Serbia were also used. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A total of 23 migrants and refugees (two women and twenty-one men) from GLO.ACT partner countries"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Migrant interview information provides anecdotal experiences and is not a representative quantitative sample; field research is qualitative. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It does not provide a representative, quantitative sample of the relevant interviews."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Due to COVID-19 restrictions, transit and destination countries were selected for feasibility rather than relevance, so evidence from them is indicative rather than representative. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the countries of transit and destination were selected not on the basis of their relevance"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Interview information reflects the situation at the time of interviews (2019-2021) and should not be assumed representative of the situation since then. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the information from the interviews is relevant to the situation at the time of interview (2019-2021)"</sub>

## Key Findings

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Key Finding 1: people leaving their countries due to conflict, persecution or other circumstances include smuggling clients and trafficking victims, and both scenarios affect illicit financial flows. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Both scenarios have an impact on illicit financial flows."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Key Finding 2: SOM and TIP generate considerable IFFs affecting origin, transit and destination countries and third countries; highly organised criminal groups rather than loose networks make the most financial gains. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is the highly organized criminal groups, rather than individuals and loosely connected, flexible criminal networks"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Key Finding 3: SOM and TIP are distinct crimes but closely linked in practice, sharing routes and methods and sometimes perpetrators, so some IFFs relate to both. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they are closely linked in practice as they often occur along the same routes"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Key Finding 4: ICT is increasingly used in cross-border SOM and TIP, for example smugglers selling fraudulent documents online and traffickers recruiting via online platforms, generating or laundering IFFs. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "smugglers sell and send fraudulent travel documents to clients via the Internet"</sub>

## Part II: Best practices, tools, and mechanisms

- **descriptive / context**: Part II identifies best practices, tools and mechanisms that jurisdictions and competent authorities may adopt to achieve successful transnational enforcement results, including leveraging multilateral networks and joint and diagonal co-operation. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Part II identifies best practices, tools, and mechanisms that jurisdictions and competent authorities may adopt"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Detection phase: FIUs primarily identify suspicious transactions or activities indicating potential financial crimes, relying on STRs data, databases they can access, financial information from obliged entities, LEA feedback and IT tools, focusing on quality leads without needing certainty that an offence exists. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "without being required to identify with certainty the existence of an offence"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In the detection phase LEAs play a key role by proactively initiating parallel financial investigations alongside ongoing predicate offence investigations, and may probe ML activities using other sources such as human intelligence. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "LEAs, for their part, play a key role in the detection process by proactively initiating parallel financial investigations"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Investigation phase: LEAs obtain more concrete evidence to establish a reasonable suspicion linking suspicious activity to a potential crime, a level sufficient to justify coercive investigative actions such as applying for subpoenas to interrogate suspects or preventing dissipation of illicit assets. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This level is sufficient to justify coercive investigative actions"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Prosecution phase: the standard of proof rises to beyond a reasonable doubt, especially in criminal proceedings, and prosecutors must present compelling evidence of the charge while respecting the presumption of innocence. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the standard of proof rises significantly to beyond a reasonable doubt"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The progressive thresholds across detection, investigation and prosecution are presented as ensuring a balanced approach that protects individuals' rights while enabling effective action. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These progressive thresholds ensure a balanced approach to financial crime enforcement"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Part II first aims to identify overarching best practices applicable universally when seeking informal international co-operation; later chapters delve into tangible practices and solutions across the ML enforcement spectrum. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identify overarching best practices that can apply universally when seeking informal international co-operation"</sub>

## 5.1. Good quality of requests and exchanges

- **expected / recommendation**: Each request should generally include a clear objective, including the types of assistance being sought. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Include a clear objective of the request, including the types of assistance being sought."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Each request should generally clearly state and describe the facts of the criminal offence under investigation, with a clear description of the link to the receiving jurisdiction (criminal nexus). (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Clearly state and describe the facts of the criminal offence being investigated"</sub>
- **permissive / expectation**: The description of the criminal nexus may include detailed references to transactions in the receiving jurisdiction, the suspected predicate offence, the period under investigation and identifiers of the persons of interest. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Such description may include: detailed references to transactions conducted within the receiving jurisdiction"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Requesting authorities that do not include actionable information risk their requests being seen as 'fishing expeditions', leading to rejection, de-prioritisation or low-quality responses, which ultimately erodes mutual trust. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Requesting authorities that do not include such actionable information run the risk of their requests being perceived"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The Egmont Biennial Census is a survey conducted every two years by the Egmont Group to gather detailed information on FIUs' operational capabilities, legal frameworks and modalities, enhancing mutual understanding and supporting co-operation. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Egmont Biennial Census (EBC) is a comprehensive survey conducted every two years"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: The Egmont Biennial Census should be considered a valuable tool for FIUs to tailor requests to foreign counterparts, because the capacity for access to data and enforcement powers varies greatly between FIUs. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the EBC should be considered as a valuable tool for FIUs to tailor their requests"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Egmont Biennial Census findings are typically summarised in the Egmont Group's annual reports. The European Judicial Network is cited as a similar source of information at local EU level. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Similar source of information at the local EU level is the European Judicial Network."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Singapore implemented SOPs and guidelines on outgoing investigation and asset recovery requests to foreign counterparts, covering ML detection, investigation, prosecution and asset recovery, to guide investigation officers. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Singapore implemented standard operating procedures (SOPs) and guidelines on the management of outgoing"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Singapore's SOPs highlight setting out the purpose of the request, providing adequate information and keeping requests within reasonable parameters; standardisation makes requests quicker to read and to target, and produces more focused, higher-quality inquiries. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "They highlight the importance of setting out the purpose of the request, providing adequate information on the request"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Singapore's SOPs on outgoing requests make information easier to target and show who to contact, saving time because the requests are standardised. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is quicker to target the information to be exchanged and to know who to contact to do so"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: The GAFILAT RRAG Open Source Inventory lists publicly available information types accessible to competent authorities in RRAG member jurisdictions without requesting it through co-operation channels, including DNFBP records, beneficial ownership, NPOs, public procurement and company registries. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Open Source Inventory developed by the GAFILAT Asset Recovery Network (RRAG) lists all the types of information"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: The Matrix of Information complements the Open Source Inventory by mapping data types available through international co-operation requests to help build targeted requests; unlike the inventory it is not public and is accessible only by request through the RRAG platform. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Contrary to the Open Source Inventory, this matrix is not public"</sub>

## 5.2. Continuous communication

- **descriptive / expectation**: Continuous communication is vital during co-operation to ensure the requirements of requesting and receiving authorities are satisfied, and is key to the agility and flexibility of informal co-operation. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Continuous communication is vital during co-operation to ensure that both the requirements"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Where information may be scant, requesting authorities should indicate this upfront, with assurances to follow up with additional information when available. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "requesting authorities should be upfront in indicating so, with assurances to follow up"</sub>
- **permissive / expectation**: Receiving authorities can share in advance the information easily available as a first partial or preliminary response before a more comprehensive response. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the receiving authorities can share in advance the type of information easily and quickly available"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Authorities should aim to ensure feedback mechanisms exist to inform receiving authorities whether exchanged information was useful, which may be targeted to a single request or to request types such as typologies, building trust. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Authorities should also aim to ensure there are feedback mechanisms to inform the receiving authorities"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: A best practice for receiving FIUs or LEAs is a structured two-phase response: first supplying results from internal databases and directly accessible sources, such as transaction records, suspicious activity reports and open-source data, allowing the requester to assess and refine its request. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A best practice for the receiving FIU or LEA is to follow a structured approach"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The second phase involves more complex gathering from external sources such as LEAs, financial institutions and regulatory bodies, typically taking more time due to legal, procedural or operational constraints. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This second phase typically requires more time due to legal, procedural, or operational constraints."</sub>

## 5.3. Standardisation and templates

- **descriptive / expectation**: Standardisation of protocols and practices helps structure co-operation, makes exchange more coherent and reliable, and its efficiency may compensate for lack of human resources. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The standardisation of protocols and practices often helps making co- operation structured"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: Agreed definitions, terms and terminologies reduce ambiguities and raise request quality, especially in bilateral exchanges or joint analysis, since authorities may use different descriptors domestically. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Using agreed upon definitions, terms and terminologies helps reduce ambiguities and raise request quality"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Many countries have developed standardised templates that account for due processes to accelerate acting on requests, focusing both sides on key areas such as transaction movements or individuals and aiding data triage. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "many countries have developed standardised templates that take into consideration the relevant due processes"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Rigid templates may impede agility; formalised processes raise the risk of additional authorisations and may lead authorities to share less information due to the 'officialness' of the channel. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "as the rigidity of templates may impede the agility of international co-operation"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: FIU Argentina developed an electronic form on a secure restricted portal, completed by judges and prosecutors, covering purpose of use (intelligence or evidence), description of triggering events, identifying data for persons or property, and the alleged link with the receiving jurisdiction. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIU Argentina has developed an electronic form (accessible via a secure and restricted portal"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Argentina's form-based approach ensures requests are complete and better structured; delegations report receiving more valuable inputs and faster responses. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Filling in fields instead of drafting a request ensures not only that the request is complete"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: India's Ministry of Home Affairs, the central authority for criminal international co-operation, launched an online MLA portal and standardised guidelines issued under the Criminal Procedure Code and the Prevention of Money Laundering Act to manage incoming and outgoing MLA requests. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Ministry of Home Affairs (MHA), India's central authority for international co-operation on criminal matters"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: India's online MLA portal provides an online request template and is reported to have enhanced capacity, reducing delays and miscommunication. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "By establishing a predictable and legally compliant process, the online MLA portal has enhanced"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The UK Central Authority co-ordinates MLA requests in England, Wales and Northern Ireland for criminal matters, while HMRC co-ordinates tax and fiscal customs criminal matters. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The UK Central Authority (UKCA) co-ordinates MLA requests in England, Wales and Northern Ireland"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The UKCA Online Submission Form provides a secure means of transmitting MLA and extradition requests, offering instant transmission and prompts for necessary information, reducing delays. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Using the online service reduces the risk of delays."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The UK has produced MLA guidelines for foreign authorities making requests to the UK, intended to ensure requests contain sufficient information for quick execution and also indicating what can be obtained in the UK without a formal MLA request. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The guidelines are intended to ensure that requests for MLA received by the UK contain sufficient relevant information"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The UK uses a standard form for MLA and asset freezing and confiscation requests with EU Member States and publishes letter-of-request templates for all other states. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the UK has a standard form for all requests for MLA and asset freezing and confiscation"</sub>

## 5.4. Adherence to governance principles, rules, and confidentiality

- **descriptive / expectation**: Governance principles and rules in multilateral networks or MoUs, and data security and confidentiality, are key ingredients for successful informal co-operation, promoting trust and accountability and encouraging openness. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Governance principles and rules, e.g., in multi-lateral networks or in MoUs"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Operational independence and autonomy of an FIU are fundamental to its effectiveness against financial crime, ML and terrorist financing, safeguarding integrity and enabling impartial decisions. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The operational independence and autonomy of an FIU are fundamental to ensuring its effectiveness"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Autonomy fosters trust: reporting entities are more likely to share accurate information when an FIU operates without interference, and international counterparts are more inclined to exchange information. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "reporting entities are more likely to share accurate and timely information"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Only operationally independent and autonomous FIUs are likely to conduct suspensions of transactions based on a foreign counterpart request effectively and without data leaks that could cause funds dissipation. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is likely that only operationally independent and autonomous FIU will be able to conduct such suspension"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Competent authorities should ensure clear domestic operational rules governing the confidentiality of handling exchanged information, including seeking permission from the source authority before sharing with third parties. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "competent authorities should ensure that there are clear domestic operational rules governing the confidentiality"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Authorities should ensure confidentiality agreements are understood to avoid misuse or leaks of foreign-provided information. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Authorities should further ensure that confidentiality agreements are understood"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Prior consent for dissemination is the explicit approval required from the originating FIU before the recipient FIU may disclose shared information to a third party or use it for purposes other than those approved. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Prior consent for dissemination refers to the explicit approval required from the originating FIU"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Prior consent lets the originating FIU assess risks of the proposed use, such as impact on ongoing investigations or information security, before granting permission, and also saves time while securing exchange. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It allows the originating FIU to evaluate the risks associated with the proposed use"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Breaches of confidentiality undermine trust and endanger the whole AML/CFT international co-operation system. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Breaches of confidentiality ruin trust and endanger the whole AML/CFT international co-operation system"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Prior consent for dissemination protects the integrity of shared information, prevents unauthorised disclosures and maintains trust between FIUs. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The requirement of prior consent safeguards the integrity of the information, prevents unauthorised disclosures"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Europol receives information from Member State and third-country authorities under agreements allowing operational information exchange, covering sensitive areas such as terrorism, organised crime, cyber and financial crime; contributors keep ownership and decide use. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The contributing countries maintain the ownership of the information, and they can decide on the way"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: In Europol's SIENA, contributors apply handling codes to messages that limit use of information, indicating what the receiver can do, whether it may be used in a judicial case, preventing further dissemination or setting storage deadlines. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "With this handling code, an authority indicates what the receiver can do with the information"</sub>

## 5.5. Domestic co-ordination, capacity, and resources

- **descriptive / context**: Successful international co-operation requires domestic co-ordination, capacity and resources; authorities differ in powers and information access, e.g., customs data on trade-based ML, central bank information on unlicensed activity, tax authority beneficial ownership information. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Successful international co-operation requires good domestic co-ordination, capacity and resources."</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: Insufficient domestic access to information or lack of powers to use platforms may make it difficult to answer international requests or join analyses or investigations, hence the necessity of formalised domestic co-ordination mechanisms and of agencies understanding each other's mandates. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "If a competent authority does not have sufficient access to information at the domestic level"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Possible mechanisms for domestic co-ordination include specialised domestic units for information exchange with foreign authorities, which may be multiple (per district or key locations) or a single unit for the territory. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Specialised domestic units for information exchange with foreign competent authorities"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Possible mechanisms include information sharing systems so that all relevant authorities know of previous or ongoing analysis or investigation on the same persons or legal entities. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Information sharing systems whereby all relevant authorities would be aware of previous or on-going"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Possible mechanisms include single points of contact for international co-operation within competent authorities. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Single points of contact in charge of international co-operation within the competent authorities."</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Possible mechanisms include policies and procedures, formalised through written agreements such as MoUs, that promote information sharing between competent authorities and establish processes for resolving disputes. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Policies and procedures—formalised through written agreements such as MoUs"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: FIU Netherlands is working with law enforcement on structured labelling in LEA systems when STRs are used, giving direct feedback on relevance and usability; this informs FIU quality review, priorities and FIU-LEA risk discussions and fosters indirect diagonal co-operation. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIU Netherlands is working on a structured way of labelling in the LEA's systems when STRs are used by LEAs."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Domestic task forces bring personnel from different competent authorities sharing a common goal, combining expertise, tools and legal powers; they may be permanent or temporary, topic-specific or general, local or national, case-related or not, and are multi-agency. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The task force model incorporates personnel from different competent authorities who share a common goal"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In 2021 and 2022 FIU Italy (UIF), using operational analysis, foreign FIU exchanges and law enforcement and Tax Revenue Agency assistance, uncovered a multi-billion-euro fraud and cross-border ML scheme involving fictitious tax credits linked to COVID-19 government measures. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "uncovered a multi-billion-euro fraud and cross-border ML scheme"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In the Italian case, UIF meetings and workshops with the Tax Revenue Agency and Finance Police aimed to understand and address the underlying risk; bilateral co-operation with EU FIUs was highly active and helped trace the final destination of financial flows. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "bilateral co- operation with EU FIUs was highly active, and the intelligence gathered played a crucial role"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The Italian case led to multiple arrests and asset freezes abroad and to reforms of tax legislation, strengthened controls and prevention of further fraud; it is presented as a model of domestic and international co-operation using dedicated secure platforms and meetings. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It stands as a model of effective domestic and international co-operation among FIUs"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: Specific infrastructures and sufficient resources should be dedicated domestically to international co-operation, with sufficient training so officers know available channels and how to use them. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "specific infrastructures and sufficient resources must be dedicated to international co- operation domestically"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Competent authorities should know which network or channel to use depending on the case, such as the Egmont network for AML/CFT disclosure information and financial data, or police/INTERPOL networks for police or criminal records. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "knowing when to use the Egmont network to obtain AML/CFT disclosure information"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: There should be training to ensure competent authorities know how to convert informally obtained intelligence into formal requests. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There should also be training to ensure competent authorities know how to convert and use"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Jurisdictions should ensure adequate human resources to deal with the increased needs of international requests, in terms of speed and volume, in line with their risk profile. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions should ensure that there are adequate human resources to deal with the increased needs"</sub>

## 5.6. Timing

- **expected / expectation**: It is important to engage in informal co-operation as early as possible during an inquiry or investigation, since early requests can avoid requests unlikely to yield results. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is important to engage in informal co-operation as early as possible"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: A lengthy MLA request to obtain bank account information can be avoided if informal information already shows the person of interest holds no bank account in the jurisdiction. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the need to embark on a lengthy MLA request process to obtain bank"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: It can be useful to set expectations and a timeline for response and indicate the urgency of the request, with urgent requests justified and clearly communicated. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It can also be useful to set expectations and identify a timeline for response"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Egmont Principles for Information Exchange between FIUs allow prioritisation of urgent cases, so receiving jurisdictions can assess and prioritise them where appropriate. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Egmont Principles for Information Exchange between FIUs allow for prioritisation of urgent cases"</sub>

## 5.5. Domestic co-ordination, capacity, and resources

- **descriptive / context**: Liaison officers deployed abroad facilitate contacts with foreign authorities, bridge databases in both jurisdictions, and act as focal points unifying domestic actors at critical moments. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Besides facilitating contacts between the foreign authorities and their home ones and forming a bridge to databases"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report notes that many jurisdictions have invested in technological tools and products to accelerate data processing and responses to requests, which it describes as a best practice. (p. 42)[^original]

## 5.6. Timing

- **expected / expectation**: The response deadline should be tailored to the quantity of requested information, its source, the need for domestic co-ordination, and the need for provisional measures. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The deadline for response should be tailored to the quantity of the requested information"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Where urgency justifies it, rapid response programmes such as those of the Egmont Group and INTERPOL can trace criminal proceeds; they succeed through clear scope and principles, focus on specific crime types, and use templates for quick exchange of pre-agreed data fields. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In certain cases, the urgency justifies the use of rapid response programs"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: INTERPOL's I-GRIP (Global Rapid Intervention of Payments) is a global stop-payment mechanism letting member countries share intelligence on cross-border criminal proceeds to intercept illicit flows swiftly before further dissipation. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The INTERPOL Global Rapid Intervention of Payments (I-GRIP) is a global stop-payment mechanism"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In July 2024 NCB Singapore used I-GRIP to ask NCB Timor Leste for help after USD 42.3 million was transferred to a fraudulent account linked to a business email compromise scam; FIU Timor Leste intercepted USD 39.3 million overnight. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "following the transfer of USD 42.3 million to a fraudulent account linked to a business email compromise scam"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The I-GRIP case led to the arrest of 12 individuals suspected of fraud and ML, seizure of a further USD 2.6 million in cash, and interception of USD 200 000 by FIU Indonesia; INTERPOL facilitated co-ordination and the return of funds to the victim. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This led to the arrest of 12 individuals suspected of fraud and ml"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: I-GRIP requests should include sufficient details for the receiving NCB to act, such as date of transaction, currency and amount, account numbers, and names of the financial institutions for beneficiary and remitter accounts. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "I-GRIP requests should include sufficient details upon which the receiving NCB can act"</sub>

## 6. Tools and mechanisms for detection and investigation

- **descriptive / context**: In the detection and investigation phase, authorities are driven by the need to obtain information to assess and substantiate indications of criminal activity and identify all individuals involved, often working with preliminary or incomplete information. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "competent authorities are primarily driven by the need to obtain information to assess and substantiate"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: Even early, LEAs begin securing evidence meeting legal admissibility standards and gather non-evidential information that may later become usable evidence but may not remain accessible; informal co-operation supports these needs. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "LEAs begin securing evidence—such as documents, testimonies, records, or witness statements—that meet legal admissibility standards"</sub>

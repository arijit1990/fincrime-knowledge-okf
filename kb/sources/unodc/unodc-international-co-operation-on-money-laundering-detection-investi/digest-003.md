## Part I: Understanding the operational landscape

- **descriptive / context**: Criminal groups directly access digital services to set up companies and open bank accounts in several jurisdictions, including via money mules, at FIs, e-money and payment institutions registered in different countries, which may reduce the role of traditional intermediaries. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This direct access may further result in the disappearance of the traditional middle- man/intermediaries"</sub>

## 3.2. Increasing challenges in the informal co-operation environment

- **descriptive / context**: Growing interconnectivity lets criminal groups spread out and distance themselves from their operations and assets, so authorities must collaborate multilaterally and at a granular level, including with unfamiliar non-traditional counterparts, to see the whole network. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "competent authorities are increasingly faced with the reality of having to collaborate on a multi-lateral level"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Changes in the criminal environment have intensified long-standing challenges competent authorities face in informal co-operation. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The changes in the criminal environment have also exacerbated some of the perennial challenges"</sub>

## Speed of response

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Financial flows move very fast, so authorities must act swiftly to intercept or preserve illicit funds and secure volatile digital evidence, while inter-jurisdictional information exchange can take days, weeks or months. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the exchange of information between jurisdictions can take days, weeks, or months (speed of law)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Expectations of what is timely differ and may vary with the nature of the crime. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There can also be different expectations on what is “timely”, which may change based on the nature of the crime"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Multi-lateral and regional information sharing platforms are one answer to enabling faster information exchange and operational co-operation. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Multi-lateral and regional information sharing platforms provide one answer for enabling faster information exchange"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Criminals exploit the time lag between jurisdictions by deliberately structuring operations across multiple countries, which increases the need for multilateral information exchange. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Criminals also exploit this time delay by intentionally structuring themselves across multiple jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Multilateral exchanges are harder because differences in legal frameworks and standards compound as more countries are involved; challenges include access to information, vocabulary and language, reciprocity, trust and data protection. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Authorities may face various challenges in the mismatch of access to information, differences in vocabulary and language"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Jurisdictions reported that multilateral relationships helped them develop deeper bilateral co-operation, and that having a variety of informal networks gave them flexibility to co-operate in the best manner possible. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Jurisdictions highlighted leveraging multi-lateral relationships to develop and build deeper bilateral co-operation"</sub>

## Volume of exchanges

- **descriptive / context**: As case volumes rise, authorities must exchange more cross-border data, may need specialised knowledge and tools to analyse it, and face new sources such as electronic forensic data like IP addresses and device IDs. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "competent authorities also find themselves having to exchange an increasing amount of cross-border data"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Growing volumes across jurisdictions force authorities to prioritise cases of most value; resource constraints, lack of manpower and lack of digital-asset investigation skills can slow responses to requests. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "forcing jurisdictions to prioritise and identify cases of most value to them"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The predicate-offence country and the money laundering country may prioritise differently. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There may also be differences in prioritisation between the country where the predicate crime has occurred"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Regional forums such as the Financial Intelligence Consultative Group in Southeast Asia, the Pacific Financial Intelligence Community and the Council of the Heads of FIUs of CIS Member States let members share priorities and agree escalation protocols. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "provide an avenue for members to share priorities and agree on the protocols and mechanisms on how to escalate operational matters"</sub>

## Quality of requests and information

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Proactive intelligence sharing by foreign counterparts is a key factor in successful cross-border ML detection and investigation. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Proactive intelligence and information sharing by foreign counterparts is one of the key factors"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Some counterparts pass on information without additional analysis or value-add, for legitimate reasons such as prioritising speed, broad requests or resource constraints, but this impedes operational value. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There were other instances where information and intelligence were shared directly with counterparts without additional analysis"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Exchanging information on standalone ML without a predicate offence is challenging and can further impede cross-border ML cases, especially where proceeds are laundered across several jurisdictions. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there can be challenges in exchanging information on standalone ML in the absence of a predicate offence"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Faster collaboration is preferred, but it cannot be pursued at the expense of information quality, which the guidance treats as vital so that exchanged information is actionable. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "While faster collaboration is preferable, this cannot come at the expense of quality, which is vital"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Information shared by foreign counterparts can lack sufficient actionable detail or be absent a clear link between the predicate crime and the alleged ML, which is described as one common challenge. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "information shared by foreign counterparts can lack sufficient actionable details or is absent of a clear nexus"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Requests are often not streamlined, lacking templates or specific forms, which delays requests and affects their quality. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "requests and exchanges are not always streamlined (e.g., lack of templates/specific forms, etc.)"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The relevance of requests is often not stated upfront, forcing recipients to spend extensive time analysing voluminous material before judging whether a request is actionable; language barriers add to this. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The relevance of the requests/information may not be clearly stated upfront in requests"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Lack of feedback mechanisms on referrals and responses leaves authorities uncertain whether exchanged information was useful. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the lack of feedback mechanisms on referrals and responses can leave authorities uncertain"</sub>

## Difficulties in identifying counterparts

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Criminals exploit regulatory arbitrage, identifying gaps and vulnerabilities in uneven implementation of AML/CFT Standards; multi-jurisdictional cases have become frequent and significant. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Criminals can exploit regulatory arbitrage opportunities, identifying gaps and vulnerabilities"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Identifying the correct counterpart is difficult, especially without prior engagement or established contact. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it can be difficult for authorities to identify the correct counterpart to request for information"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FIUs find counterpart identification easier because each jurisdiction has only one FIU, whereas law enforcement agencies are numerous with varying authority and competencies. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these challenges appear to be mitigated for FIUs as there is only one per jurisdiction"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: Authorities need to work collectively with domestic and foreign agencies as data and cases cut across sectors. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is now a need to work collectively with different domestic and foreign agencies"</sub>

## 4.1. Informational needs

- **expected / requirement**: Under the FATF Standards, FIUs should have access to the widest possible range of financial, administrative and law enforcement information, including open-source information and, where appropriate, commercially held data. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is important that FIUs have access to the widest possible range of financial, administrative and law enforcement information"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Countries should ensure that LEAs and investigative authorities have timely access to a wide range of information for use in investigations. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries should ensure that LEAs and investigative authorities have timely access to a wide range of information"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Competent authorities generally need information to establish the full details of criminal conduct and the identity and location of persons involved. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Generally, competent authorities require information to establish two key areas"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Criminal information covers judiciary and law enforcement information on subjects or criminal activity, such as criminal records, intelligence from surveillance and informants, and street-level police intelligence. (p. 26)[^original]

- **descriptive / definition**: AML/CFT disclosures include STRs and other national-law reports such as cash transaction reports, wire transfer reports and threshold-based declarations. (p. 26)[^original]

## 4.2. Primary multi-lateral networks and users

- **descriptive / context**: Many multilateral networks exist for informal co-operation and information exchange, varying in governance and structure according to purpose. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There is a myriad of multi-lateral networks available for competent authorities"</sub>

## For FIUs

- **descriptive / context**: The Egmont Group is a body of 177 FIUs covering most jurisdictions, providing the Egmont Secure Web for exchanging financial intelligence and expertise. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is a united body of 177 FIUs covering most jurisdictions in the world"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The Egmont Group does not conduct financial investigations; domestic law enforcement and investigative authorities manage such inquiries. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Egmont Group does not conduct financial investigations. Instead, domestic law enforcement"</sub>

## For LEAs

- **descriptive / context**: FIU.net is a decentralised network connecting all EU FIUs to exchange and pseudonymously cross-match financial information, with each FIU keeping its own local database and no single central database. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Hence, there is no single database in the FIU.net system"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: INTERPOL's I-24/7 network connects law enforcement across its 196 member countries for exchanging sensitive and urgent police information. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Its I- 24/7 secure communications network connects law enforcement officers across its 196 member countries"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: INTERPOL offers operational and analytical support, access to 19 databases, case co-ordination and international alerts through its Notices system; each member has a National Central Bureau. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "access to its 19 databases (containing records on known international criminals"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: INTERPOL established its Financial Crime and Anti-Corruption Centre in 2022 to support members against illicit financial flows. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In 2022, INTERPOL also established its Financial Crime and Anti-Corruption Centre"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Europol supports EU Member States against serious organised crime, cybercrime, terrorism and ML, and has agreements with 38 countries across four continents and 18 international and European organisations. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it has established agreements with 38 countries across four continents and 18 international and European organisations"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Europol exchanges information through SIENA, deploys analysts, runs Operational Task Forces, participates in joint investigation teams and coordinates Joint Action Days. (p. 30)[^original]

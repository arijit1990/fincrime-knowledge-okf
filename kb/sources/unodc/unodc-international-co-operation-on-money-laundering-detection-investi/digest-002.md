## 2.1. Relationship between informal and formal co-operation and their goals

- **descriptive / definition**: International co-operation is typically divided into formal and informal categories. The two are not competing mechanisms; each serves different purposes, so they have different requirements and processes. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Informal and formal co-operation are not competing mechanisms and are each driven by different purposes."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Both formal and informal exchanges are shaped by international standards and instruments such as conventions, but are ultimately governed by each jurisdiction's legislative frameworks to ensure exchanges are legal and legitimate. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "ultimately governed by a jurisdiction’s legislative frameworks, i.e., to ensure all exchanges and co-operation are legal and legitimate."</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Table 1 notes that informal contact is direct, between FIUs, law enforcement, prosecutors or investigating magistrates (where allowed) and their counterparts, whereas formal contact is both direct and indirect, including central authorities and diplomatic channels via foreign ministries and other ministries. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Direct: FIU, law enforcement, prosecutor"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Table 1 notes informal co-operation is generally less onerous and can begin once contact has been established between counterparts, while formal co-operation may be more onerous, with legal requirements and thresholds, including to ensure admissibility of evidence in court. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Generally, less onerous and can begin once contact has been established between counterparts."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 3 cites the FATF Assessment Methodology (2022), Note 5 to Assessors for Immediate Outcome 2, as a source for the distinction. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "See FATF Assessment Methodology (2022); Note 5 to Assessors for Immediate Outcome 2."</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Table 1 lists the purpose of formal co-operation as coercive investigation measures, such as obtaining banking information, and assistance to execute extradition and confiscation orders. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Assistance to execute extradition and confiscation orders."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Successful money laundering enforcement may require combining informal and formal co-operation, and the best results come when they are cohesive, complementary and well sequenced. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Successful ML enforcement outcomes may require combining both informal and formal international co-operation."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Informal co-operation can be used at any phase of cross-border ML detection, investigation and prosecution, usually before or in parallel with formal co-operation. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Authorities can engage in informal co-operation at any phase to conduct cross-border detection"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Informal intelligence can help decide whether an MLA request is needed and can refine and expedite it; the foreign jurisdiction often needs knowledge of the predicate offence to assess an MLA request. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This can serve as a basis to determine if an MLA request is necessary, as well as refine and expedite the MLA request"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Reciprocity is a common theme across formal and informal channels and may affect how jurisdictions prioritise incoming requests and how flexible they are towards counterparts. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A common theme across both informal and formal channels of co-operation is the importance of reciprocity."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Informal co-operation provides a framework to discuss how much information about the underlying predicate offence can be shared, which may help the foreign central authority assess an MLA request. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "discuss the extent and amount of information to be shared on the underlying predicate offence"</sub>

## What is reciprocity?

- **descriptive / definition**: In general terms, reciprocity is the mutual exchange of benefits or concessions between jurisdictions or organisations without the binding mechanisms of treaties, relying on goodwill, shared interests and expected future interaction. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "without the formal binding mechanisms of treaties or agreements."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Key features of reciprocity in informal co-operation include voluntary exchange without legal obligations, trust building, flexibility, and long-term engagement; reciprocity is described as the cornerstone of informal co-operation. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "As such, reciprocity can be considered as the cornerstone of informal co-operation."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Under the Egmont Group Principles of Information Exchange between FIUs, reciprocity means the capacity and practice of FIUs to obtain or access and share similar information or financial intelligence; the Principles are binding on members by virtue of voluntary membership. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "refers to the capacity and practice of FIUs engaged in international co-operation to obtain or access"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: An FIU is expected to share with foreign counterparts the maximum amount of financial intelligence or information it can obtain or access under the laws of the disclosing FIU's jurisdiction. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An FIU is expected to share with its foreign counterparts the maximum amount of financial intelligence"</sub>
- **permissive / requirement**: An FIU can refuse co-operation, as appropriate, for example on grounds of lack of reciprocity or recurring inadequate co-operation. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An FIU can refuse co-operation, as appropriate, for example, on the grounds of lack of reciprocity"</sub>

## 2.2. Overarching characteristics of informal co-operation

- **descriptive / context**: Informal co-operation lets competent authorities obtain and disclose intelligence on threats, assist analysis and investigations, and implement urgent provisional measures where legally authorised. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "implement urgent provisional measures where legally authorised."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Speed is a characteristic of informal co-operation: it is inherently quicker than formal co-operation, which is encumbered by bureaucratic formalities, and is valued in time-critical cases. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Informal co-operation is inherently quicker than formal co-operation"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Agility: informal co-operation reduces red tape, accepts many communication means (phone, e-mail, in person), allows requests to be supplemented and adapted as cases evolve, and can yield higher-quality inputs for transnational cases. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The reduced “red tape” enables agility through informal co-operation"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Spontaneous disclosure of information is cited as an example of agility; it is explicitly encouraged under the UNTOC and the UNCAC. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an approach explicitly encouraged under both the United Nations Convention against Transnational Organized Crime"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Flexibility: jurisdictions can choose appropriate channels, provide assistance ranging from simple data verification to joint analysis, combine multilateral and bilateral channels, and focus on substance over form, for example resolving differences in dual criminality or legal systems. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "focusing on substance over form (e.g., resolving differences in dual criminality or differences in legal systems)."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Focus: practitioners better understand partners' capabilities, requirements and limitations, can focus enquiries more efficiently, and stable partnerships may improve risk understanding and detection. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The stability of partnerships could even improve risk understanding and detection."</sub>

## 2.3. Main types and levels of informal co-operation

- **descriptive / context**: Trust underpins informal co-operation; it is often built from previous positive co-operation or common network membership with established contact points and rules, and motivates authorities to act fast and flexibly. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Informal co-operation is underpinned by trust."</sub>

## Spontaneous disclosures as an illustration of the informal co-operation’s agility

- **permissive / requirement**: Under UNTOC Article 18(4) and (5) and UNCAC Article 46, a State Party may voluntarily share information about covered offences with another State Party without a formal request, and the recipient may then choose to request MLA. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These provisions allow a State Party to voluntarily share information related to criminal offences"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The receiving State is generally required to maintain the confidentiality of spontaneously transmitted information and respect conditions attached to its use, consistent with formal assistance channels. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The receiving State is generally required to maintain the confidentiality of the transmitted information"</sub>
- **permissive / requirement**: Exception: where the information is exculpatory, the receiving State is permitted to disclose it freely within its domestic legal proceedings. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the receiving State is permitted to disclose the information freely within its domestic legal proceedings."</sub>

## 2.3. Main types and levels of informal co-operation

- **descriptive / definition**: There are four main types of informal co-operation: multi-lateral, bilateral, diagonal, and joint analysis and investigations; these can co-exist and reinforce each other. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There are four main types of informal co-operation that competent authorities can engage for assistance"</sub>

## Multi-lateral co-operation networks

- **descriptive / definition**: Multi-lateral networks are platforms facilitating information exchange among counterparts across many jurisdictions, mostly regional or global, set up by partnership agreements or membership in organisations such as INTERPOL, Egmont Group, Europol, Eurojust and the EU AML Authority. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Multi-lateral co-operation networks (multi-lateral networks) generally refer to an established platform"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Multi-lateral networks enable real-time, direct communication between counterparts, accelerating sharing of critical intelligence, which is described as essential for effective ML detection and rapid response. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Such networks facilitate real-time and direct communication between counterparts"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Multi-lateral networks have governance rules that set how information is exchanged, with whom it may be shared, and conditions of use; members must comply with them, which strengthens trust. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "They govern how information can be exchanged, whom the information can be shared with"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Major multi-lateral networks appoint specific contact points; for example INTERPOL's 196 member countries each run a National Central Bureau connected via the I-24/7 network. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "INTERPOL’s 196 member countries each runs a National Central Bureau (NCB)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Multi-lateral networks provide standardised protocols and practices; for example Egmont Group FIUs follow common guidelines and standards for coherent and reliable exchange. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Egmont Group of FIUs adheres to common guidelines and standards"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Commonly used secure platforms include INTERPOL's I-24/7, Egmont Secure Web (ESW) and Europol's SIENA; regional examples include FIU.net, the CLEO platform for FIUs of Southeast Asia and Australia, and a secure system for CIS Member States. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The most commonly used across jurisdictions are: INTERPOL’s I-24/7 global secure communications network"</sub>

## Bilateral co-operation

- **descriptive / context**: Bilateral co-operation is exchange between counterparts in two jurisdictions; its hallmark is deeper trust built through successful exchanges, which fosters flexibility and understanding of each other's legal and operational limits. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A primary hallmark of bilateral co-operation is deeper trust."</sub>
- **permissive / requirement**: Bilateral exchanges may be driven through MoUs, where required by local legislation, and general law enforcement co-operation agreements; some jurisdictions appoint liaison officers or secondees abroad. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "including, where required by the local legislation, establishing Memoranda of Understanding (MoUs)"</sub>

## The role of liaison officers deployed abroad

- **descriptive / context**: Liaison officers officially represent their agency abroad, establish relationships, act as informal facilitators of requests, and act as a human interface; they have no law enforcement powers in the host jurisdiction. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Although they possess no law enforcement powers in the host jurisdiction"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Liaison officers can advise host and home authorities on formulating formal requests and follow up on requests to ensure timely handling; postings are typically reserved for jurisdictions with substantial existing co-operation and may cover neighbouring countries. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Given the significant costs associated with deploying liaison officers abroad"</sub>

## Diagonal co-operation

- **descriptive / definition**: Diagonal co-operation is collaboration between competent authorities of different types (e.g., police, customs, tax, FIUs, prosecutors) in different jurisdictions, occurring directly or indirectly. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Diagonal co-operation5 refers to the process of collaboration between competent authorities of different types"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Direct diagonal co-operation means a receiving authority passes a requested information directly to a non-counterpart foreign authority, with the direct counterpart kept informed rather than bypassed. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The rationale behind direct diagonal co-operation is never to bypass the direct counterpart"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Direct diagonal co-operation faces challenges: identifying the right non-counterpart, lack of secure platforms, legal and operational divergences, strict confidentiality rules, disparate exchange processes and data formats, and trust and governance concerns, which often lead jurisdictions to favour indirect channels. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These factors often contribute to jurisdictions favouring indirect channels for exchanges between non-counterparts."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Indirect diagonal co-operation means information passes through one or more domestic or foreign authorities (for example Police A to FIU A to FIU B) before reaching the foreign requesting authority, leveraging existing counterpart trust. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "requested information passing from the receiving competent authority through one or more domestic or foreign authorities"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Diagonal co-operation widens information availability, lets authorities combine powers and expertise, including on niche crime types, assets or laundering techniques, and improves agility and flexibility. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Diagonal co-operation widens the availability of information as it allows competent authorities"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Diagonal co-operation can produce complementary FIU-LEA analysis yielding more robust, actionable leads, and can place LEAs, prosecutors and judges in direct contact for deeper synchronisation of investigative and evidentiary needs. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "diagonal co-operation can produce complementary analysis that would help generate more robust and actionable leads"</sub>

## Joint analysis and investigations

- **descriptive / definition**: Joint analysis is co-operation within an intelligence-sharing framework, typically by FIUs and/or LEAs, to detect ML targets and methods; joint investigations are typically carried out by LEAs and possibly prosecutors. Both require dedicated, in-depth joint work on specific cases. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "joint analysis refers to co-operation taking place within an intelligence-sharing framework"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Joint work goes beyond general multi-lateral data sharing into co-ordinated analysis and investigation of specific operational targets, allowing participants to integrate sources and obtain a fuller picture. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "joint work entails a deeper level of collaboration"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Foundations for joint work vary with legal systems and may include agency-to-agency MoUs, multi-lateral network frameworks and flexible arrangements, often facilitated by liaison officers; these are generally informal, but joint work may require formal legal agreements, MLA legislation or treaties. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "joint analysis and investigations may require formal legal agreements between competent authorities"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Teething challenges of joint work, especially when recent, include language, working methods, legal frameworks, unequal capacities and resources, misaligned priorities and schedules, difficulty selecting cases, and data security concerns about sharing sensitive data. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Data security concerns are also a challenge against such communications"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Where capacity and legal and operational frameworks exist, joint analysis and investigations have been growing in number due to their benefits. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "joint analysis and investigations have been growing in numbers due to the myriad of benefits in participation"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Integrated approach benefit: combining intelligence lets authorities holistically identify trends, transactions and networks, understand other financial environments, and uncover patterns, assets and entities that single-agency approaches may miss, including professional enablers. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This allows participating jurisdictions to directly obtain an enhanced understanding of the others’ financial environments"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Direct communication benefit: joint work enables direct, real-time communication and assistance, quick issue resolution, regular in-person meetings, and a culture of mutual support and trust. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Joint analysis and investigations allow for direct, real- time communication and assistance."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Pooling expertise and resources: joint collaboration pools financial, IT and human resources and expertise, improving depth and accuracy of analysis and enabling use of advanced technology or specialised techniques unavailable in one jurisdiction. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Joint collaboration allows resources (financial, IT, human etc.) and expertise across various jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Co-ordination and targeted focus: authorities align methodologies, share information directly with those involved, make faster decisions without evaluating every request, and use common templates for information exchange. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Having a harmonised approach also extends to creating common practices and templates for information exchange"</sub>

## 3.1. Changes in the criminal environment

- **descriptive / finding or result**: INTERPOL's 2024 strategic analysis identified money laundering as the second highest global crime threat faced by member countries, just behind drug trafficking. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "INTERPOL’s strategic analysis identified ML as the second highest global crime threat"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Europol's 2024 report found that 68% of the organised crime groups it investigated include members of multiple nationalities, and 76% of the most threatening criminal networks are present or active in two to seven countries. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "found that 68% of the organised crime groups (OCGs) it has investigated are composed of members of multiple nationalities"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Complex and transnational criminality is primarily driven by rapid digitalisation of services, increasing case volumes and sophistication, especially in cyber-enabled financial crime; criminals have been observed using AI and machine learning to develop or refine ML schemes. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "criminals have been observed leveraging Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Communications: criminals operate from anywhere, use decentralised networks, anonymity tools, encrypted platforms, the dark web and cryptocurrencies, so criminally associated information is spread across jurisdictions and held in non-traditional AML/CFT sectors such as e-commerce and telecoms. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "criminally-associated information to be disbursed across multiple jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: New financial developments: funds transfers are increasingly instantaneous, allowing rapid movement of large sums; virtual assets have materially changed laundering techniques, are inherently cross-border, and are increasingly linked to predicate offences and used as a means of payment for illegal goods and services. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The introduction and development of VAs have materially impacted laundering techniques"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: New entrants to financial markets, including digital payment service providers, and new products such as virtual IBANs further complicate detecting, investigating and prosecuting illicit funds transfers. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "New entrants to the financial markets (including digital payment service providers)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Digitalisation has enabled a growing criminal connectivity that the text says manifests in three main areas; the excerpt numbers only two of them, communications and new financial developments including virtual assets. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Digitalisation has also enabled a growing criminal connectivity that manifests in three main areas"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: New entrants to the financial markets, including digital payment service providers, and new financial products such as virtual IBANs further complicate detection, investigation and prosecution of illicit funds transfers. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "New entrants to the financial markets (including digital payment service providers)"</sub>

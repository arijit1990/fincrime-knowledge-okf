## Background

- **descriptive / requirement**: Security Council resolution 2178 (2014), adopted unanimously on 24 September 2014, expressed concern over the growing threat of foreign terrorist fighters and underlined the need to disrupt financial flows supporting them while respecting international human rights, refugee and humanitarian law. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "underlined the need to disrupt financial flows supporting FTFs, while respecting international human rights law"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Resolution 2178 (2014) defines foreign terrorist fighters as individuals who travel to a State other than their State of residence or nationality for terrorist acts, planning, preparation, participation, or providing or receiving terrorist training, including in connection with armed conflict. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "individuals who travel to a State other than their States of residence or nationality for the purpose of the perpetration"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Resolution 2178 (2014) directed CTED to identify issues, trends and developments related to foreign terrorist fighters. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Council also directed CTED to identify issues, trends and developments related to FTFs"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Flows of FTFs to Iraq and the Syrian Arab Republic reached unprecedented levels from 2014 to 2016; by 2017 estimates suggested more than 42,000 individuals from more than 120 countries had travelled to join terrorist organizations. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "By 2017, estimates suggested that more than 42,000 individuals from more than 120 countries travelled to join terrorist organizations"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: By early 2018 thousands of FTFs had left the conflict zones, but the fate of a large part of the Da'esh FTF contingent is unknown, with a large discrepancy between total FTFs and those recorded as killed, detained, returned or relocated. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is a large discrepancy between the total number of FTFs and those recorded as having been killed or detained"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Although there is no large-scale movement of FTFs to Africa, regional FTF recruitment has risen in Central and Southern Africa, and Da'esh presence in Somalia has grown in terms of FTFs and its financial role. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "While there is no large-scale movement of FTFs to Africa, a rise in regional FTF recruitment has been reported in Central and Southern Africa"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: ISIL-K relies on facilitator networks between Afghanistan and Türkiye capable of moving operatives from Central Asia and Afghanistan towards Europe for external operations. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "relies on networks of facilitators between Afghanistan and Türkiye, which is capable of moving operatives from Central Asia"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Several Member States noted increased travel of Arab and Central Asian nationals, and some Indian nationals, to Afghanistan. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Several Member States noted an increase in travellers of Arab and Central Asian nationalities to Afghanistan"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Repatriation from north-east Syrian camps is progressing slowly; at the current rate, risks associated with camps and prisons will persist for several years. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "At the current rate of repatriation, the risks associated with camps and prisons will persist for several years"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Data on numbers remaining in camps and prisons are unreliable, with estimates ranging from 35,000 to 45,000; individuals have reportedly been smuggled out of camps for about $2,000, and about 9,000 Da'esh detainees, of whom 2,000 are foreign fighters, are in prisons. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Approximately 9,000 Da’esh detainees (of whom 2,000 are foreign fighters) are in prisons"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Da'esh-inspired FTF financial activity typically involves supporters collecting or sending small sums abroad or financing travel, usually from legal sources such as personal savings, sometimes collected on behalf of others. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This money typically comes from legal means, often personal savings, and sometimes may be collected on behalf of others"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Funds may be coordinated through encrypted mobile applications, sent as virtual assets, wired abroad through fiat money service businesses, or passed to couriers if collected in cash. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Individuals may coordinate the donations through encrypted mobile applications, send the funds in the form of virtual assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Financing patterns of FTFs have shown remarkable adaptability, shifting from simple fund transfer methods to increasingly sophisticated and technologically advanced approaches. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the financial patterns associated with FTFs have demonstrated remarkable adaptability"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A regional nature of FTFs in African conflict zones is an emerging theme, though it may be hard to clearly tell 'foreign' from 'domestic' actors because communities span several countries. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it may be challenging to clearly discern “foreign” from “domestic” actors, especially as communities span several countries"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In other regions, the decline in FTFs has been offset by recruitment from local communities. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In other regions, a decline in FTFs has been offset by recruiting from local communities"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Approximately 49,100 persons were in Hawl camp in July 2023 according to the Special Rapporteur, comprising about 15,000 women, 3,000 men and 31,000 children. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Hawl camp contained approximately 49,100 persons comprising approximately 15,000 women, 3,000 men, and 31,000 children"</sub>

## Milestones

- **descriptive / finding or result**: In 2011-2016, FTF flows to Iraq and Syria involved heavy reliance on personal funds and traditional money transfers, mainly for travel costs and some equipment, with easily traceable financial footprints such as ATMs, card terminals and remittance agents in border regions, and physical transport of below-threshold amounts. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Heavy reliance on personal funds and traditional money transfer methods"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Later milestones include bank cards issued with false identities or registered in third countries, increased prepaid card use, continued hawala-like services, complex cash courier routes mixed with formal services, expansion of regional financial hubs, new networks in conflict zones, and growing significance of mobile money and virtual assets. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Increased use of prepaid cards to move money between countries"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Larger sums were sent to returning FTFs to hand over to terrorist groups or pay traffickers and smugglers, and reintegration costs appeared as a financing need. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Larger sums sent to returning FTFs for handover to terrorist groups before departure or to pay for traffickers and smugglers"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: From March 2019, Da'esh supporters ran online fundraising campaigns on messaging and social media platforms. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "ONLINE FUNDRAISING CAMPAIGNS ON MESSAGING AND SOCIAL MEDIA PLATFORMS BY DA’ESH SUPPORTERS"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Funding and fighter movements from 2018 onwards show wide use of blockchains and varied obfuscation techniques, and transfers to final destinations via hawala networks; at least 35,000 to 45,000 FTF-associated individuals remain in camps and prisons. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Wide range of blockchains and varied obfuscation techniques"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: Present and forecasted trends include regional FTF recruitment, decentralization of financing methods, further sophistication in exploiting new financial technologies, convergence of old and new fund-raising and moving methods, and further convergence with organized crime networks. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Further increase and sophistication in exploitation of new financial technologies"</sub>

## Key evolutionary trends

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Early FTFs relied heavily on personal funds for travel, raised through savings, loans, maxed credit cards with no intention of repayment, false tax refunds, early welfare and superannuation payouts, or vehicle purchases on credit, often on promises of provision by Da'esh. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FTFs relied heavily on personal funds to pay for their travel to conflict zones"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Pre-departure arrangements could include cash withdrawals, reducing account balances close to zero with no further transactions, and buying equipment from specialty shops. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Pre-departure arrangements could involve withdrawing cash, bringing accounts balances close to zero"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Reports describe FTFs on the periphery of foundations and non-profit organizations, with people linked to foundations travelling to Syria with large amounts of cash. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "people associated with the foundations have travelled to the Syrian Arab Republic with large amounts of cash"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Some FTFs raised funds through criminal activities including theft, fraud and drug dealing. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Some FTFs engaged in criminal activities to raise funds, including theft, fraud or drug dealing"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Support networks before and after departure, such as family, diaspora communities and recruitment or facilitation networks, were a prominent funding source. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The role of support networks, both pre- and post- departure, also featured prominently as a source of funding"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Neighbouring countries with advanced banking systems, widely available ATMs and card payment terminals served as key transit hubs, and purchases or cash withdrawals typically occurred near final destinations. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "served as key transit hubs for these flows"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: According to FATF and Global Network typology studies, funds primarily followed a straightforward path from countries of origin to Syria, Iraq or neighbouring countries, mirroring FTF movements. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the flow of funds primarily followed a straightforward path from countries of origin to the Syrian Arab Republic and Iraq"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: FTFs often carry relatively small amounts of cash below suspicion thresholds across borders themselves, or use other transfer methods such as traditional banking, hawala or other informal value transfer systems. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "who often transport relatively small amounts of cash (below suspicion thresholds) across borders themselves"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Cash transfers sent by support networks via money remitters used agents operating in border areas. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "cash transfers sent by support networks via money remitters involved the use of agents operating in border areas"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Related fundraising was sometimes disguised as legitimate charitable or humanitarian activity, including through shell non-profit organizations, while collected funds were used to enable FTF travel. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "related fundraising was disguised by claims of engagement in legitimate charitable or humanitarian activities, including through shell non-profit organizations"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Research shows early financial footprints of FTFs or supporters sending donations from third countries were quite easily traceable, but trends later diversified and evolved. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the financial footprints of the FTFs or their supporters sending donations from third countries were quite easily traceable"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Both funds and fighters have diversified in destinations and origins, with flows traversing wider geography including Africa (Sahel-Sahara, Horn of Africa, Mozambique, Central and Southern Africa), Afghanistan, and Central and South-East Asia. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The financial flows now traverse a wider geographical expanse"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: ISIL-K and the Eastern Turkistan Islamic Movement continue to abuse Türkiye as a transit hub for financial and logistical purposes. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "continue to abuse Türkiye as a transit hub for financial and logistical purposes"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Da'esh in Somalia, overseen by the Al-Karrar office, served as a financial and communication hub for the global Da'esh enterprise, moving funds via mobile money, cash, hawala and business money laundering. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "has served as a financial and communication hub for the global Da’esh enterprise"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Some researchers indicate that financial networks in Somalia, Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania and South Africa helped pool funds to support ISCAP in Congo. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "financial networks and cells in Somalia, Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, and South Africa have helped pool resources"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In South-East Asia, a network of female financial facilitators, typically spouses of FTFs, has become instrumental in regional fund flows. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a network of female financial facilitators, typically spouses of FTFs connected to Da’esh, has become instrumental"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Funding sources have generally remained relatively static, though with some diversification; self-funding remains central, while reliance on social media and crowdfunding, often using virtual assets, has increased. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is an increased reliance on fundraising tools, including social media and crowdfunding, often with the use of virtual assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The anonymity offered by some online fundraising services makes it challenging to trace the flow of funds. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The anonymity offered by some of these services makes it challenging to trace the flow of funds"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Traditional financial channels, international transfer systems, cards and wire transfers continue to play a key role, with funds often cashed out in territories bordering conflict zones. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "traditional financial channels continue to play a key role"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: After increased due diligence on transactions, especially in regions bordering conflict zones or high-risk jurisdictions, financers shifted to bank cards allowing small transactions instead of large electronic transfers. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "financers adopted a method of making transactions using bank cards, which permit transactions in small amounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: To conceal financial trails, financers used bank cards with false identities and forged documents, or cards issued by institutions registered in third countries, linked to electronic wallets. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "financers have used bank cards with false identities and forged documents"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Prepaid cards, often loaded in one country and used in another, allowed cross-border money movement with even less scrutiny on the ultimate use of funds. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This shift to prepaid cards, which were often loaded with funds in one country and used in another"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Cross-border cash transport combined with informal transfer services such as hawala remained among the most prominent methods used by FTF financers and facilitators. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Cross-border cash transportation coupled with informal cash transfer services like hawala have remained among the most prominent methods"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Cash couriers often use complex routes to avoid detection and to transport relatively small amounts. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Cash couriers often use complex routes to avoid detection and to transport relatively small amounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Money value transfer services continue to be exploited despite increased scrutiny including licensing and AML/CFT supervision; facilitators use multiple small transactions and mix cash with formal banking to avoid detection. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "facilitators often operate through multiple small transactions and by mixing physical cash movements and formal banking services"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: MVTS agencies located near areas where terrorist groups operate are the second most preferred method for moving cash. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "MVTS with agencies located near areas where terrorist groups operate are the second most preferred method to move cash"</sub>

## IN DIFFERENT JURISDICTIONS

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 1 of this Annex is designed to help firms approach their assessment of other jurisdictions' ML/TF risk. It discusses jurisdictions where a presumption of low risk may be made, those where such a presumption may not be appropriate without further investigation, and issues a firm should consider in all cases. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "how they might approach their assessment of other jurisdictions, to determine their level of ML/TF risk"</sub>

## Implications of an assessment as low risk

- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 2: assessing a jurisdiction as low risk only allows some easement of the level of due diligence; it is not a complete exemption from CDD measures for customer identification, nor from ongoing monitoring of the business relationship, nor from other procedures (such as monitoring) needed to fulfil responsibilities under the Proceeds of Crime Act 2002. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is not a complete exemption from the application of CDD measures in respect of customer identification"</sub>

## Categories of country

- **recommended / recommendation**: Paragraph 4 (EU/EEA member states): when identifying lower risk jurisdictions, FATF encourages firms to take into consideration country risk factors, namely countries identified by credible sources (such as mutual evaluation or detailed assessment reports) as having effective AML/CFT systems, and countries identified by credible sources as having a low level of corruption or other criminal activity. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FATF encourages firms to take into consideration country risk factors"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: Paragraph 4: in making a risk assessment, countries or financial institutions could, when appropriate, also take into account possible variations in ML and TF risk between different regions or areas within a country. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "possible variations in money laundering and terrorist financing risk between different regions or areas within a country"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 5: all EU Member States are required to enact legislation and financial sector procedures in accordance with the EU Fourth Money Laundering Directive, which implements the revised 2012 FATF standards. All EEA countries have undertaken to implement it and all are members of FATF or the relevant FATF style regional body (MONEYVAL for Europe). (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "All EEA countries have undertaken to implement the fourth money laundering directive"</sub>

## Implications of an assessment as low risk

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 3: each firm decides on a jurisdiction's risk level in light of its own circumstances, but senior management answers for that judgment to the regulator or, where needed, to a court. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "senior management is accountable for this judgment – either to its regulator, or, if necessary, to a court"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 3: it is important that the reasons for treating a particular jurisdiction as low risk (other than those where a presumption of low risk may be made) are recorded when the decision is taken, and that the decision rests on relevant, up to date data or information. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "documented at the time the decision is made"</sub>

## Categories of country

- **permissive / context**: Paragraph 6: given the commitment to implement the Fourth Money Laundering Directive, firms may initially presume EU and EEA member states to be low risk, though significant variations may exist in how the directive (and predecessors) was transposed into national law and effective implementation will also vary. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "firms may initially presume EU and EEA member states to be low risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraphs 7-8: all FATF members, including members of FATF style regional bodies, undertake to implement the FATF AML and counter-terrorism Recommendations as part of membership obligations; however, unlike EU transposition of the directive, implementation cannot be mandatory and members approach obligations in different ways and under different timetables. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "implementation cannot be mandatory, and all members will approach their obligations in different ways"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 9: information on the effectiveness of implementation in these jurisdictions may be obtained from Mutual Evaluation reports published on the FATF website, and from the FATF public statement, compliance statement and advisory notices issued by HM Treasury. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Information on the effectiveness of implementation in these jurisdictions may be obtained through scrutiny of Mutual Evaluation reports"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 10: a majority of countries and territories do not fall within the lists that can be presumed low risk; this does not necessarily mean their AML/CTF legislation and due diligence standards are lower, but standards vary significantly and firms will need to carry out their own assessment. Beyond the firm's own knowledge and experience, particular attention should be paid to any FATF-style or IMF/World Bank evaluations. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "particular attention should be paid to any FATF-style or IMF/World Bank evaluations that have been undertaken"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: Paragraph 11: as a result of due diligence carried out, for jurisdictions that are low risk in the firm's judgment, firms may rely, for the purposes of carrying out CDD measures, on other regulated firms situated in such a jurisdiction. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "firms may rely, for the purposes of carrying out CDD measures, on other regulated firms situated in such a jurisdiction"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 6: if a firm has substantive information showing that a presumption of low risk for EU/EEA states cannot be sustained, whether generally or for particular products, it will need to consider whether to strengthen its procedures to reflect that information. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they will need to consider whether their procedures should be enhanced to take account of this information"</sub>

## Factors to be taken into account when assessing other jurisdictions

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Paragraph 14 lists the factors for assessing other jurisdictions: geographical risk factors; membership of groups admitting only those that meet a set benchmark; contextual factors such as political stability and the level of endemic corruption; evidence of relevant public criticism, including HMT/FATF advisory notices; independent, public assessment of the jurisdiction's overall AML regime; the need for any assessment to be recent; implementation standards, including quality and effectiveness of supervision; and the incidence of trade with the jurisdiction, which needs to be proportionate, especially where very small. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Membership of groups that only admit those meeting a certain benchmark"</sub>

## Geographical risk factors

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Paragraph 15: geographical risk factors include countries identified by credible sources (mutual evaluations, detailed assessment reports, published follow-up reports) as lacking effective systems to counter ML/TF; countries identified by credible sources as having significant corruption or other criminal activity such as terrorism, money laundering, and production and supply of illicit drugs; and countries subject to sanctions, embargos or similar measures issued by, for example, the EU or UN. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries subject to sanctions, embargos or similar measures issued by, for example, the European Union or the United Nations"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Paragraph 15 (continued): further geographical risk factors are countries providing funding or support for terrorism, and countries with organisations in their territory designated by the UK government as proscribed organisations under Schedule 2 to the Terrorism Act 2000, or by other countries, international organisations or the EU as terrorist organisations. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries that have organisations operating within their territory which have been designated"</sub>
- **expected / limitation**: Firms should bear in mind that the presence of one or more risk factors may not always indicate a high risk of ML or TF in a particular situation. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the presence of one or more risk factors may not always indicate that there is a high risk of money laundering or terrorist financing"</sub>

## Membership of an international or regional ‘group’

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 16: a number of international and regional groups of jurisdictions admit only those that have demonstrated a commitment to the fight against ML and TF and have an appropriate legal and regulatory regime to back it. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "admit to membership only those jurisdictions that have demonstrated a commitment to the fight against money laundering and terrorist financing"</sub>

## Contextual factors

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 17: political stability and position in corruption tables are relevant to whether a jurisdiction is likely to be low risk. It will seldom be easy for firms to assess these themselves and they are likely to rely on external agencies' evidence, whether general or prepared for the firm. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "political stability of a jurisdiction, and where it stands in tables of corruption are relevant"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 17: where a firm relies on publicly available evidence, it is important that the firm knows something of the criteria behind the assessment. A firm may not rely only on the fact that a list was independently produced, even by a respected third-party agency. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the firm cannot rely solely on the fact that such a list has been independently prepared"</sub>

## Evidence of relevant (public) criticism

- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 18: because the FATF periodically issues statements of concern about jurisdictions lacking comprehensive AML/CTF/CPF systems, financial sector firms should have regard, when building internal procedures, to the need for additional monitoring of transactions from any country listed in those statements. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "financial sector firms should have regard to the need for additional monitoring procedures for transactions"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 18: the guidance states that additional monitoring procedures will also be required for correspondent relationships with financial institutions from countries listed on FATF statements of concern. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Additional monitoring procedures will also be required in respect of correspondent relationships"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 18: the FATF from time to time issues statements of concern about lack of comprehensive AML/CTF/CPF systems in jurisdictions. When constructing internal procedures, financial sector firms should have regard to the need for additional monitoring procedures for transactions from any country listed on these statements. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "firms should have regard to the need for additional monitoring procedures for transactions from any country that is listed"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 19: commercial agencies also produce reports and lists of jurisdictions, entities and individuals involved, or alleged to be involved, in activities that cast doubt on their AML/CTF integrity. These can provide useful and relevant evidence, which may or may not be conclusive, on whether a jurisdiction is likely to be low risk. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "can provide some useful and relevant evidence – which may or may not be conclusive"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 20: particular attention should be paid to mutual evaluation assessments undertaken by standard setting bodies such as FATF and by international financial institutions such as the IMF. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Particular attention should be paid to assessments that have been undertaken by standard setting"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 21: FATF members monitor their progress through regular peer mutual evaluation. In 1998 FATF extended mutual evaluation beyond its membership by endorsing FATF-style programmes of regional groups: GIFCA, CFATF, APG, MONEYVAL (Council of Europe countries not in FATF), GAFILAT, MENAFATF, EAG, ESAAMLG, GIABA and GABAC. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In 1998, FATF extended the concept of mutual evaluation beyond its own membership"</sub>

## FATF

- **expected / limitation**: Paragraph 22: firms should bear in mind that mutual evaluation reports are at a 'point in time' and should be interpreted as such. Follow-up actions are usually reviewed after two years, but there can be long intervals between reports for a jurisdiction; changes in train at evaluation time are not reflected. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "mutual evaluation reports are at a ‘point in time’, and should be interpreted as such"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 24: summaries of FATF and FATF-style evaluations appear in FATF Annual Reports. Mutual evaluation reports from some FATF-style regional bodies may not have been done fully to FATF standards. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "mutual evaluation reports prepared by some FATF- style regional bodies may not be carried out fully to FATF standards"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 24: firms should keep in mind the possible shortfall against FATF standards if a decision that a jurisdiction is low risk relies on a mutual evaluation report from a FATF-style regional body. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "firms should bear this in mind if a decision on whether a jurisdiction is low risk"</sub>

## Implementation standards (including effectiveness of supervision)

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 26: how far a jurisdiction complies with Recommendations 17, 23, 29 and 30 can tell a firm about the extent and quality of AML/CTF supervision there. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Recommendations 17, 23, 29 and 30"</sub>

## FATF

- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 22: there can be subsequent changes to a jurisdiction's regime (in response to criticisms or otherwise) which firms should seek to understand and factor into their assessment of whether the jurisdiction is low risk. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "which firms should seek to understand and to factor into their assessment of whether the jurisdiction is low risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 23: compliance with certain FATF Recommendations may be more relevant to risk assessment than others. Those particularly relevant are, for the legal framework, Recommendations 1, 3, 4 and 5; for measures to be taken by firms, Recommendations 9, 10, 11, 17 and 20; for the supervisory regime, Recommendations 26, 27 and 35; and for international co-operation, Recommendations 2 and 40. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may have more relevance than others"</sub>

## IMF/World Bank

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 25: as part of financial stability assessments, the IMF and World Bank have agreed with FATF a detailed methodology for assessing AML/CTF compliance based on the FATF Recommendations. Some countries have undergone IMF/World Bank assessments in addition to FATF's; where these relate to FATF members they are formally adopted by FATF and appear on its website. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the IMF and the World Bank have agreed with FATF a detailed methodology for assessing compliance with AML/CTF standards"</sub>

## Incidence of trade with the jurisdiction

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 27: the level and extent of due diligence needed to judge a jurisdiction's risk level will be influenced by the volume and size of the firm's business with that jurisdiction relative to its overall business. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "will be influenced by the volume and size of the firm’s business with that jurisdiction"</sub>

## Prohibition notices

- **permissive / context**: Paragraph 28: under certain circumstances HM Treasury may, under Schedule 7 of the Counter-terrorism Act 2008, issue directions to a firm in relation to customer due diligence, ongoing monitoring, systematic reporting, and limiting or ceasing business. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "HM Treasury may, pursuant to the Counter-terrorism Act 2008, Schedule 7, issue directions to a firm"</sub>

## Advisory notices

- **descriptive / definition**: Paragraph 34: in the UK the ML Regulations treat a country as a high-risk third country if it is named on either of the two FATF lists described in paragraph 31. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the ML Regulations determine that a high-risk third country is a country named on either of the FATF lists"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraphs 29-30: HM Treasury issues advisory notices expressing the UK's full support for FATF work on jurisdictions of concern, and FATF issues periodic announcements about its concerns regarding lack of comprehensive AML/CTF systems in various jurisdictions. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "HM Treasury issues advisory notices in which it expresses the UK’s full support of the work of the FATF"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 31: FATF maintains two lists of countries with strategic deficiencies in AML, CTF or counter proliferation financing: High-risk Jurisdictions Subject to a Call for Action, and Jurisdictions under Increased Monitoring. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF maintains two lists that identify countries with strategic deficiencies"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 32: FATF also maintains a statement, Improving Global AML/CTF Compliance: On-going Process, listing jurisdictions with strategic AML/CTF deficiencies that have developed an action plan with FATF; each has given a written high-level political commitment to address them. FATF will closely monitor implementation and encourages members to consider the statement. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "each has provided a written high-level political commitment to address the identified deficiencies"</sub>

## FCA

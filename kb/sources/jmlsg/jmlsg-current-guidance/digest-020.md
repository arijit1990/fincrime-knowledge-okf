(p. 176)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is an offence for a nominated officer to consent to a"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 6.46 states that where consent is needed urgently, firms should use the process described in paragraph 6.41. (p. 176)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Where urgent consent is required, use should be made of the process referred to in paragraph 6.41 above."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.46 states that reporting before or reporting after the event are not equal options between which a firm can choose. (p. 177)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Reporting before or reporting after the event are not equal options"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Under JMLSG paragraph 6.46, where a customer instruction is received before a transaction or activity takes place, or arrangements are put in place, and there are grounds for knowledge or suspicion that the transaction, arrangements or the funds/property may relate to money laundering, a report must be made to the NCA and consent sought to proceed. (p. 177)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a report must be made to the NCA and consent sought to proceed with that transaction or activity"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.47 says that when a concerning transaction is already within an automated clearing or settlement system, where delay would breach a contractual obligation or market settlement or clearing rules, the nominated officer may need to let the transaction proceed and report it later. (p. 177)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the nominated officer may need to let the transaction proceed and report it later"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: JMLSG paragraph 6.47 states POCA provides a defence from making a report where there is a reasonable excuse for not doing so, but this defence is untested by case law and would need to be considered on a case-by-case basis. (p. 177)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this defence is untested by case law, and would need to be considered on a case-by- case basis"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: JMLSG paragraph 6.48 says a defence request to undertake a future transaction or activity, or enter an arrangement, should be sent electronically (with the consent-request tick box marked) or, if electronic methods are unavailable, faxed to the NCA UKFIU Consent Desk immediately the suspicion is identified. (p. 177)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the disclosure should be sent electronically (ensuring that the tick box for a consent request is marked)"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: JMLSG paragraph 6.48 says defence requests should not be sent by post due to the timings involved, and additional postal copies are not required after electronic or fax submission. (p. 177)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Defence requests should not be sent by post due to the timings involved"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: JMLSG paragraph 6.48 describes the Consent Desk process: it applies NCA policy, makes internal enquiries and contacts the appropriate law enforcement agency where necessary for a consent recommendation; the firm is informed of the decision by telephone and given a reference number, which should be recorded, followed by a formal letter.   <sub>anchor: "be given a reference number, which should be recorded"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.49 states that if the NCA does not refuse a defence request within seven working days following the working day after the disclosure, the firm may process the transaction or activity, subject to normal commercial considerations. (p. 177)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the firm may process the transaction or activity, subject to normal commercial considerations"</sub>

## Consent under Terrorism Act

- **mandatory / requirement**: Paragraph 6.51 (Terrorism Act s21ZA): in the circumstances described, an authorised officer commits an offence by consenting to a transaction or arrangement going ahead inside the seven working day notice period, counted from the working day after the disclosure to the NCA, unless the NCA has given consent. (p. 177)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is an offence for an authorised officer to consent to a transaction or arrangement going ahead"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.51 states that a person does not commit an offence under the Terrorism Act where, before becoming involved in a transaction or arrangement relating to money or property suspected or believed to be terrorist property, a report is made to the NCA and consent sought to proceed. (p. 178)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a report is made to the NCA and consent sought to proceed with that transaction or arrangement"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.52 says that where a concerning transaction is already within an automated clearing or settlement system, where delay would breach a contractual obligation or market settlement or clearing rules, the authorised officer may need to let it proceed and report it later. (p. 178)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the authorised officer may need to let the transaction proceed and report it later"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: JMLSG paragraph 6.52 states the Terrorism Act provides a defence for delayed reporting where there is a reasonable excuse, so long as the report is made on the person's own initiative and as soon as reasonably practical; the defence is untested by case law and would need to be considered case by case. (p. 178)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "so long as the report is made on his own initiative and as soon as it is reasonably practical"</sub>

## General

- **expected / expectation**: JMLSG paragraph 6.53 says that when consent is needed for a future transaction, activity or arrangement, the disclosure should be sent electronically (consent tick box marked) or, if secure electronic methods are unavailable, faxed to the NCA UKFIU Consent Desk immediately the suspicion is identified; consent requests should not be sent by post due to timings. (p. 178)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Consent requests should not be sent by post due to the timings involved"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: JMLSG paragraph 6.53 says that once the NCA decision is reached the disclosing firm is informed by telephone and given a consent number, which should be recorded; a formal consent letter follows. (p. 178)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "be given a consent number, which should be recorded"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.54 states that if the NCA does not refuse consent within seven working days following the working day after the disclosure, the firm may proceed with the transaction or arrangement, subject to normal commercial considerations. (p. 178)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the firm may proceed with the transaction or arrangement, subject to normal commercial considerations"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: JMLSG paragraph 6.54 says that where consent is refused, the law enforcement agency refusing consent should be consulted to establish what information can be provided to the customer. (p. 178)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the law enforcement agency refusing consent should be consulted"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.55 states that NCA consent (a 'notice' in the Terrorism Act), or the absence of a refusal within seven working days following the working day after disclosure, gives the person handling the transaction or arrangement, or the firm's nominated officer, a defence against a possible later Terrorism Act charge in respect of it if it proceeds. (p. 178)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "provides the person handling the transaction or arrangement, or the nominated officer of the reporting firm, with a defence"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 6.57 states that, where a customer pushes hard for a transaction or activity to go ahead, the NCA will connect the firm with the investigating law enforcement agency to get guidance that avoids alerting the customer to the suspicion and the disclosure, and that each case is handled on its own facts. (p. 178)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the NCA will put the firm in touch with the investigating law enforcement agency for guidance"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.56 states the consent provisions can only apply where there is prior notice to the NCA; the NCA cannot provide consent after the transaction or activity has occurred. (p. 179)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the NCA cannot provide consent after the transaction or activity has occurred"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.56 states a SAR received after the transaction or activity has taken place is dealt with as an ordinary standard SAR, and absent contrary instruction a firm is free to operate the customer's account under normal commercial considerations until the LEA determines otherwise through its investigation. (p. 179)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "will be dealt with as an ordinary standard SAR"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.57 states that where urgent action is needed on an account and the seven working day consent notice period applies, the NCA will endeavour to respond in the shortest timeframe, where possible sooner than seven working days. (p. 179)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the NCA will endeavour to provide a response in the shortest timeframe"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 6.58 says that, to secure a defence against later prosecution for not reporting, the grounds for any deliberate decision not to report should be written down or recorded electronically. (p. 179)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the reasons for any conscious decision not to report should be documented, or recorded electronically"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 6.58 also says a suitable report should go in as soon as practicable once the event has taken place, setting out the full details of the transaction, the circumstances that ruled out advance notice, and where any money or assets were sent. (p. 179)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An appropriate report should be made as soon as is practicable after the event"</sub>

## Tipping off, and prejudicing an investigation

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 6.60 explains that POCA and the Terrorism Act each contain two offences of tipping off and prejudicing an investigation, one concerning disclosure that an internal or external report has been made and the other concerning disclosure that an investigation is contemplated or under way; the offences resemble and overlap each other but differ in important ways, each applies to information that reached the person in the course of regulated sector business, and the permitted disclosures in paragraphs 6.63 to 6.66 do not trigger them. (p. 179)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These offences are similar and overlapping, but there are also significant differences between them."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 6.60 states that it is important for those working in the regulated sector to know the conditions that must be met for each of the two offences. (p. 179)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to be aware of the conditions precedent for each offence"</sub>

## General

- **descriptive / limitation**: JMLSG paragraph 6.59 states that the UK consent regime as it then operated is a difficult one for financial practitioners, with operational challenges and legal uncertainties about what can realistically constitute a 'pre-event' transaction. (p. 180)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is a difficult one for financial practitioners to work with"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: JMLSG paragraph 6.59 lists customer service implications of the consent regime: potentially litigious consequences of declining a customer's instructions, inability to explain because of tipping-off risk, and the problematic requirement in 6.73 for (in particular, large) deposit-taking institutions to seek consent for all post-disclosure transactions over £250. (p. 180)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "seek consent for all post-disclosure transactions over £250"</sub>

## Tipping off, and prejudicing an investigation

- **descriptive / definition**: JMLSG paragraph 6.60 states that POCA and the Terrorism Act each contain two separate offences of tipping off and prejudicing an investigation: the first relates to disclosing that an internal or external report has been made; the second to disclosing that an investigation is being contemplated or carried out. (p. 180)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The first offence relates to disclosing that an internal or external report has been made"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.61 states that once an internal or external suspicion report has been made, it is a criminal offence for anyone to disclose information about that report likely to prejudice an investigation that might follow; no offence is committed if the person does not know or suspect the disclosure is likely to prejudice such an investigation, or if the disclosure is a permitted disclosure. (p. 180)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is a criminal offence for anyone to disclose information about that report which is likely to prejudice an investigation"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.61 states that reasonable enquiries of a customer, conducted in a tactful manner, regarding the background to a transaction or activity inconsistent with the normal pattern of activity are prudent practice, form an integral part of CDD measures, and should not give rise to the tipping off offence. (p. 180)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Reasonable enquiries of a customer, conducted in a tactful manner"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.62 states that where a money laundering investigation is being contemplated or carried out, it is a criminal offence for anyone to disclose this fact if the disclosure is likely to prejudice the investigation; no offence if the person does not know or suspect the disclosure is likely to prejudice it, or the disclosure is a permitted disclosure. (p. 180)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is a criminal offence for anyone to disclose this fact if that disclosure is likely to prejudice that investigation"</sub>

## Permitted disclosures

- **descriptive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.63 states no offence is committed if the disclosure is made to the FCA (or other relevant supervisor) for the purpose of detecting, investigating or prosecuting a criminal offence (in the UK or elsewhere), an investigation under POCA, or enforcing a court order under POCA. (p. 180)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An offence is not committed if the disclosure is made to the FCA (or other relevant supervisor)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.64 states an employee, officer or partner of a firm does not commit an offence under POCA s333A or Terrorism Act s21A if the disclosure is to an employee, officer or partner of the same firm. (p. 181)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "if the disclosure is to an employee, officer or partner of the same firm"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.65 states a person does not commit an offence if the disclosing firm and recipient firm belong to the same group (as defined in directive 2002/87/EC), the disclosure is to a credit institution or financial institution, and the recipient is situated in an EEA State or a country imposing equivalent money laundering requirements. (p. 181)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "belong to the same group (as defined in directive 2002/87/EC)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.66 states no offence under POCA s333A or Terrorism Act s21D is committed by a disclosure from one credit institution to another, or one financial institution to another, where it relates to a shared customer or former customer, a transaction involving both, or a service provided to both. (p. 181)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "if the disclosure is from one credit institution to another, or from one financial institution to another"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.66 adds further conditions for the inter-institution permitted disclosure: it must be only for the purpose of preventing an offence under Part 7 of POCA or Part III of the Terrorism Act; the recipient must be in an EEA State or a country imposing equivalent money laundering requirements; and both firms must be subject to equivalent duties of protection of personal data (within the meaning of the Data Protection Act 1998). (p. 181)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the disclosure is for the purpose only of preventing an offence under Part 7 of POCA"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.67 states that pre-event notification to the NCA, followed by no refusal of consent within seven working days or no restraint order within the 31 day (or extended) moratorium period, does not alter the position regarding tipping off. (p. 181)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "does not alter the position so far as ‘tipping off’ is concerned"</sub>
- **descriptive / requirement**: JMLSG paragraph 6.68 states a firm cannot at the time tell a customer that a transaction is being delayed because a report is awaiting NCA consent. (p. 181)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "cannot, at the time, tell a customer that a transaction is being delayed because a report"</sub>
- **descriptive / requirement**: JMLSG paragraph 6.68 states a firm cannot later tell a customer that a transaction or activity was delayed because a report had been made under POCA or the Terrorism Act, unless law enforcement/the NCA agrees or a court order permits disclosure, and cannot tell the customer that law enforcement is conducting an investigation. (p. 181)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "cannot tell the customer that law enforcement is conducting an investigation"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: JMLSG paragraph 6.69 cites K v Natwest [2006] EWCA Civ 1039, which confirmed the application of the tipping-off provisions and stated that the existence of suspicion is a subjective fact with no legal requirement for reasonable grounds; the bank employee either suspects or does not, and if suspecting must inform the authorities (himself or through the nominated officer). (p. 181)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The existence of suspicion is a subjective fact. There is no legal requirement that there should be"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: JMLSG paragraph 6.69 records that the Court in K v Natwest appears to have approved the 7 and 31 day scheme, saying that many people would think a reasonable balance has been struck, and that this view was upheld in Shah and another v HSBC Private Bank Ltd [2012] EWHC 1283 (QB). (p. 181)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "upheld in Shah and another v HSBC Private Bank Ltd [2012] EWHC 1283 (QB)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.70 states a firm receiving a complaint in these circumstances may be unable to give the customer a satisfactory explanation, and the customer may complain to the Financial Ombudsman Service (FOS), which has a secure process and specialist AML-trained teams for such complaints. (p. 181)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "specialist AML trained teams who only deal with these types of complaints"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 6.70 explains that a firm unable to give a customer a satisfactory explanation may face a complaint to the Financial Ombudsman Service, which runs a secure process with specialist AML-trained teams, and that it is important these complaints are spotted early so they reach those teams and move quickly; it gives a dedicated email address, Legal_6732@financial-ombudsman.org.uk, for sending sensitive information to the FOS. (p. 181)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is important that these complaints are identified early"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.71 states the NCA has confirmed that in such cases a firm may tell the FOS's legal department about a report to the NCA and its outcome, on the basis that the FOS will keep it confidential; a firm may wish to take legal advice on what to pass on, and FOS communications with the customer remain in the name of a case handler/ombudsman so the customer is not alerted. (p. 182)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a firm may tell the FOS’s legal department"</sub>

## Transactions following a disclosure

- **mandatory / requirement**: JMLSG paragraph 6.73 (POCA s339A) states that, for deposit-taking institutions alone, following a suspicion report any subsequent transactions (including 'lifestyle' payments) involving the customer or account may only proceed if they meet the 'threshold' of £3,000 or less; above £3,000, permission to vary the threshold payment is required from the NCA before proceeding. (p. 182)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may only proceed if it meets the ‘threshold’ requirement of £3,000 or less"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.74 states that if regular transactions exceed the £3,000 threshold, the deposit taker can apply to the NCA for a Threshold Variation to impose a higher threshold on the account for regular payments; the NCA requires the deposit taker to specify the 'lifestyle' payments, the named accounts they come from and go to, and the amount of each transaction. (p. 182)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the deposit taker can apply to the NCA for a Threshold Variation"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.75 states the disclosure provisions in POCA and the Terrorism Act protect persons making SARs from potential breaches of confidentiality imposed under contract, statute (for example the Data Protection Act) or common law, applying inside and outside the regulated sector and to voluntary reports as well as reports made to fulfil reporting obligations. (p. 182)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "protect persons making SARs from any potential breaches of confidentiality"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.75 states the NCA has established a SARs Confidentiality Hotline (0800 234 6657) for reporters and end-users to report breaches. (p. 182)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "SARs Confidentiality Hotline (0800 234 6657)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.76 states NCA consent following a disclosure is given solely in relation to the money laundering offences, providing the staff involved with a defence against a charge under ss 327-329 of POCA or a terrorist finance offence under ss 15-18 of the Terrorism Act. (p. 182)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Consent provides the staff involved with a defence against a charge of committing a money laundering offence"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.76 states NCA consent is not intended to override normal commercial judgement, and a firm is not committed to continuing the relationship with the customer if that would place the reporting institution at commercial risk. (p. 182)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is not intended to override normal commercial judgement"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.77 states that whether to terminate a relationship is essentially a commercial decision and firms must be free to make such judgements. (p. 182)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Whether to terminate a relationship is essentially a commercial decision"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Paragraph 6.72 states that firms must stay alert for further transactions by, or instructions from, any customer or account for which a disclosure has already been made. (p. 182)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Firms must remain vigilant for any additional transactions by, or instructions from, any customer or account"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 6.72 adds that if suspicion persists, firms should send further disclosures and consent applications to the NCA, as appropriate. (p. 182)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should submit further disclosures, and consent applications, to the NCA, as appropriate"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Paragraph 6.77 says that, before ending a relationship in the circumstances it describes, a firm should consider liaising with the investigating law enforcement officer to judge whether termination would be likely to alert the customer or otherwise prejudice an investigation. (p. 182)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to consider whether it is likely that termination would alert the customer or prejudice an investigation"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 6.77 states that if suspicion about the customer, transaction or activities continues and funds have to be handed back to the customer when the relationship ends, firms should ask the NCA for consent to return the funds. (p. 182)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "firms should ask the NCA for consent to repatriate the funds"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: JMLSG paragraph 6.78 states that where the firm knows the funds in an account derive from criminal activity, or arise from fraudulent instructions, the account must be frozen. (p. 183)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "or that they arise from fraudulent instructions, the account must be frozen."</sub>
- **permissive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.78 states that where the account holder is believed possibly to be involved in the fraudulent activity being reported, the account may need to be frozen, but the need to avoid tipping off would have to be considered. (p. 183)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the need to avoid tipping off would have to be considered"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.79 states that when an enquiry is under investigation, the investigating officer may contact the nominated officer to ensure they have all relevant supporting information, including seeking supplementary information or documentation from the firm and from other sources by court order; the nominated officer will usually receive direct feedback on progress. (p. 183)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the investigating officer may contact the nominated officer to ensure that he has all the relevant information"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: JMLSG paragraph 6.79 notes there may be cases where the nominated officer cannot be informed of the state of the investigation, either because of its confidential nature or because it is sub judice. (p. 183)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "because of the confidential nature of the enquiry, or because it is sub judice"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: JMLSG paragraph 6.80 says that where a firm does not wish to make a payment requested by a customer, it should notify the NCA and ask them to identify information they are prepared to allow the firm to disclose to the court and the customer in any proceedings the customer brings to enforce payment. (p. 183)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it should notify the NCA of this fact and request them to identify any information"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: JMLSG paragraph 6.80 says the NCA should be reminded that the court may ask the officer to appear to justify his position if he refuses to consent to adequate disclosure, and that refusing adequate disclosure is likely to make it apparent to the customer that the firm's refusal is due to a law enforcement investigation. (p. 183)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the court may ask him to appear before it to justify his position"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.81 states that if the investigating officer consents to disclosure of adequate information to let the firm defend proceedings brought by the customer, that information may be shown to the court and customer without a tipping off offence; if agreement cannot be reached, an application can be made to the court for directions and/or an interim declaration. (p. 183)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an application can be made to the court for directions and/or an interim declaration"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 6.82 states that, in any proceedings the customer brings, the firm may disclose to the court and the opposing side only the information the investigating officer or the court has agreed to. (p. 183)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the firm may only disclose to the court and the other side such information"</sub>

## Constructive trusts

- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 6.86 says that where a firm making a SAR knows, or doubts, that the funds in question belong to someone other than its customer, that fact and the action the firm proposes to take should be included in the report sent to the NCA. (p. 183)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should form part of the report that is forwarded to the NCA"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.83 states the duty to report suspicious activity and avoid tipping off could in certain circumstances lead to a potential conflict between the firm's criminal law responsibilities and its civil law obligations, as a constructive trustee, to a victim of fraud or other crimes. (p. 184)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could, in certain circumstances, lead to a potential conflict between the reporting firm’s responsibilities under the criminal law"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.84 states a firm's liability as a constructive trustee under English law can arise when it knows the funds do not belong to its customer or is on notice that they may not; if it pays the money away other than to the rightful owner and is deemed to have acted dishonestly, it may be held liable for knowingly assisting a breach of trust. (p. 184)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may be held liable for knowingly assisting a breach of trust"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: JMLSG paragraph 6.85 states that a suspicion considered necessary to report may in certain circumstances indicate the firm knows or is on notice that funds do not belong to its customer, but such suspicion may not itself be enough to make the firm a constructive trustee; case law suggests a constructive trust only arises when there is some evidence that the funds belong to someone other than the customer. (p. 184)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a constructive trust will only arise when there is some evidence that the funds belong to someone"</sub>

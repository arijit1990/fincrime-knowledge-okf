## PREFACE

- **descriptive / context**: The JMLSG preface states that the UK has long had an obligation to have effective procedures to detect and prevent money laundering. The UK Money Laundering Regulations applying to financial institutions date from 1993, and the current Regulations are those of 2017 (as amended). (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the current Regulations being those of 2017 (as amended)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Many of the procedures appropriate to meeting money laundering and terrorist financing obligations are similar, and firms can often employ the same systems and controls to meet them. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "firms can often employ the same systems and controls to meet them"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Terrorism Act 2000 set out the specific obligations for combating terrorist financing. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Specific obligations to combat terrorist financing were set out in the Terrorism Act 2000."</sub>

## Purpose of the guidance

- **descriptive / context**: The guidance has four purposes: to outline the legal and regulatory framework for AML/CTF requirements and systems across financial services; to interpret the relevant law and regulations and how they may be implemented in practice; to indicate good industry practice through a proportionate, risk-based approach; and to assist firms to design and implement systems and controls that mitigate the risk of the firm being used for money laundering and terrorist financing. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "indicate good industry practice in AML/CTF procedures through a proportionate, risk-based approach"</sub>

## Scope of the guidance

- **descriptive / context**: The guidance sets out what is expected of firms and their staff in preventing money laundering and terrorist financing, but allows them some discretion in how they apply the UK AML/CTF requirements to the firm's circumstances, products, services, transactions and customers. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "allows them some discretion as to how they apply the requirements of the UK AML/CTF regime"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The guidance relates solely to how firms fulfil their AML/CTF obligations. Customers should understand that producing the required evidence of identity does not automatically qualify them for the product or service sought, because firms apply other commercial considerations in deciding whether to take on customers. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "production of the required evidence of identity does not automatically qualify them for access to the product or service"</sub>

## What is the offence of money laundering?

- **descriptive / definition**: JMLSG paragraph 5 lists forms of money laundering: turning money raised through criminal activity into 'clean' money (classic money laundering); handling the benefit of acquisitive crimes such as theft, fraud and tax evasion; handling stolen goods; being directly involved with criminal or terrorist property or entering arrangements to facilitate its laundering; and criminals investing proceeds of crime in the whole range of financial products. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "handling the benefit of acquisitive crimes such as theft, fraud and tax evasion"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The techniques used by money launderers constantly evolve to match the source and amount of funds to be laundered and the legislative, regulatory and law enforcement environment of the market in which the launderer wishes to operate. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The techniques used by money launderers constantly evolve to match the source and amount of funds"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: There are three broad groups of money laundering offences that firms need to avoid committing: knowingly assisting (in specified ways) in concealing, or entering arrangements for the acquisition, use and/or possession of, criminal property; failing to report knowledge, suspicion, or reasonable grounds for knowing or suspecting, that another person is engaged in money laundering; and tipping off, or prejudicing an investigation. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There are three broad groups of offences related to money laundering that firms need to avoid committing."</sub>
- **descriptive / requirement**: It is a separate offence under the ML Regulations not to have adequate and appropriate policies and procedures in place to forestall and prevent money laundering, regardless of whether money laundering actually takes place. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "not to establish adequate and appropriate policies and procedures in place to forestall and prevent money laundering"</sub>

## The guidance also covers terrorist financing

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 9 of the JMLSG guidance says there can be considerable similarities between the movement of terrorist property and the laundering of criminal property, and notes that some terrorist groups are known to have well established links with organised criminal activity. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There can be considerable similarities between the movement of terrorist property and the laundering of criminal property"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Two major differences between terrorist property and criminal property: often only small amounts are needed for individual terrorist acts, which makes tracking harder; and terrorists can be funded from legitimately obtained income, making it extremely difficult to identify the stage at which legitimate funds become terrorist property. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "often only small amounts are required to commit individual terrorist acts, thus increasing the difficulty of tracking the terrorist property"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Terrorist organisations can require quite significant funding and property to resource their infrastructure; they often control property and funds from a variety of sources and use modern techniques to manage these funds and move them between jurisdictions. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "employ modern techniques to manage these funds, and to move them between jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / requirement**: In combating terrorist financing, the obligation on firms is to report any suspicious activity to the authorities. This supports law enforcement by allowing seizure and/or freezing of property where there are grounds to suspect it could finance terrorist activity, and depriving terrorists of it when links are established. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the obligation on firms is to report any suspicious activity to the authorities"</sub>

## What about other financial crime?

- **descriptive / context**: ML and TF risks are closely related to risks of other financial crime such as fraud. Fraud and market abuse, as separate offences, are not dealt with in the guidance, but the guidance does apply to dealing with any proceeds of crime arising from them. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Fraud and market abuse, as separate offences, are not dealt with in this guidance."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: When considering ML and TF issues, firms should consider their procedures against fraud and market abuse and how these might reinforce each other. Where responsibilities sit in different departments, there will need to be strong links between those managing and reporting on these areas of risk. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "firms should consider their procedures against fraud and market abuse and how these might reinforce each other"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: When measures involving the public are taken specifically as an anti-fraud measure, the distinction should be made clear. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "measures involving the public are taken specifically as an anti-fraud measure, the distinction should be made clear"</sub>

## Who is the guidance addressed to?

- **expected / expectation**: The JMLSG guidance is addressed to firms in the industry sectors represented by its member bodies and to FCA-regulated firms. All such firms, including JMLSG trade association members not regulated by the FCA, should have regard to its contents. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should have regard to the contents of the guidance"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: Financial services firms that are neither JMLSG trade association members nor FCA-regulated may choose to have regard to the guidance as industry good practice. Firms outside the financial sector but subject to the ML Regulations, particularly where no specific guidance is issued to them, may also find it helpful. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may choose to have regard to this guidance as industry good practice"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The guidance is of direct relevance to senior management, nominated officers and MLROs, who set the firm's risk management policies and procedures. Operational areas are expected to be guided by the firm's own, often more detailed, internal arrangements tailored to the firm's risk profile. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is expected that these areas will be guided by the firm’s own, often more detailed and more specific"</sub>

## How should the guidance be used?

- **descriptive / context**: The guidance gives firms a degree of discretion in how they comply with AML/CTF legislation and regulation and in the procedures they put in place. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The guidance gives firms a degree of discretion in how they comply with AML/CTF legislation and regulation"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The guidance is not intended to be applied unthinkingly as a checklist. Firms should encourage staff to 'think risk'. The FCA expects FCA-regulated firms to manage risk thoughtfully and to maintain systems and procedures appropriate and proportionate to the risks identified. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is not intended that the guidance be applied unthinkingly, as a checklist of steps to take."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Where the guidance directly describes statutory or FCA regulatory requirements it uses 'must', indicating the provisions are mandatory. 'Should' indicates ways in which the statutory and regulatory requirements may be satisfied, allowing for alternative means of meeting them. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it uses the term ‘must’, indicating that these provisions are mandatory"</sub>

## The content of the guidance

- **descriptive / context**: The guidance emphasises senior management's responsibility to manage the firm's ML and TF risks on a risk-based approach. It sets out a standard approach to identifying and verifying customers, separating basic identity from other aspects of customer due diligence measures, and gives guidance on the obligation to monitor customer activity. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "separating out basic identity from other aspects of customer due diligence measures"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: JMLSG guidance is in parts: Part I contains generic guidance for the UK financial sector; Part II gives guidance for specific industry sectors, supplementing Part I; Part III gives additional guidance on specific areas of activity. Part I has eight chapters, a Glossary and appendices. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The main text in Part I contains generic guidance that applies across the UK financial sector."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Part I covers: senior management responsibility (Chapter 1); appropriate controls in the context of financial crime (Chapter 2); the role of the nominated officer and MLRO (Chapter 3); the risk-based approach to CDD (Chapter 4); carrying out CDD obligations including monitoring (Chapter 5); identifying and reporting suspicious activity (Chapter 6); staff awareness, training and alertness (Chapter 7); and record keeping (Chapter 8). (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the role and responsibilities of the nominated officer and the MLRO (Chapter 3)"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The sectoral guidance in Parts II and III is incomplete on its own and must be read in conjunction with the main guidance in Part I. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It must be read in conjunction with the main guidance set out in Part I of the guidance."</sub>

## Status of the guidance

- **descriptive / context**: POCA requires a court to take account of Treasury-approved industry guidance when considering whether a person in the regulated sector committed the offence of failing to report knowledge or suspicion (or reasonable grounds) of money laundering. The Terrorism Act similarly requires a court to take account of such guidance for failure to report under that Act. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "POCA requires a court to take account of industry guidance that has been approved by a Treasury minister"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The ML Regulations provide that a court must decide whether similar industry guidance was followed in determining whether a person or institution in the regulated sector has complied with any ML Regulations requirement. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a court must decide whether similar industry guidance was followed"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FCA Handbook confirms that the FCA will have regard to whether a firm followed relevant provisions of the guidance when considering action against an FCA-regulated firm for breach of relevant SYSC provisions (SYSC 3.2, SYSC 5.3, DEPP 6.2.3), and when considering whether to prosecute a breach of the Money Laundering Regulations (EG 12.1). (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FCA will have regard to whether a firm has followed relevant provisions of this guidance"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The guidance provides a sound basis for meeting legislative and regulatory obligations when tailored to the firm's business risk profile. Departures from the guidance, and the rationale for them, should be documented, and firms will have to stand prepared to justify departures, for example to the FCA. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Departures from this guidance, and the rationale for so doing, should be documented"</sub>

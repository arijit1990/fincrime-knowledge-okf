## I. CUSTOMER RISK FACTORS

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Customer risk factor (business activity): whether the customer or beneficial owner has links to sectors associated with higher ML, TF or PF risk, for example certain Money Service Businesses, casinos or dealers in precious metals or the defence industry; and whether they have links to sectors involving significant amounts of cash. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "links to sectors that are associated with higher ML, TF or PF risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Customer risk factor: where the customer is a legal person, the purpose of its establishment, for example the nature of its business. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Where the customer is a legal person, what is the purpose of their establishment?"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Customer risk factor (political connections): whether the customer is a PEP or their beneficial owner is, whether they have other relevant links to a PEP (for example directors who are PEPs and exercise significant control), and in what jurisdiction the PEP or their connected business is located. A footnote states domestic PEPs have an inherently lower risk (ML Reg 35(3A)). (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Domestic PEPs have an inherently lower risk (ML Reg 35(3A))"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Customer risk factor (other public positions): whether the customer or beneficial owner holds another public position that might enable abuse of public office for private gain, for example senior or regional public figures able to influence contract awards, decision-making members of high profile sporting bodies, or individuals known to influence government and other senior decision-makers. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "might enable them to abuse public office for private gain"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Customer risk factor: whether the customer is a credit or financial institution from a jurisdiction with an effective AML/CTF regime and supervised for compliance, and whether there is evidence of supervisory sanctions or enforcement in recent years for failure to comply with AML/CTF or wider conduct requirements; also whether the customer is a public administration or enterprise from a jurisdiction with low levels of corruption. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Is there evidence that the customer has been subject to supervisory sanctions or enforcement"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Customer risk factor: whether the customer's or beneficial owner's background is consistent with what the firm knows about their former, current or planned business activity, business turnover, source of funds and source of wealth. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "background consistent with what the firm knows about their former, current or planned business activity"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Customer risk factor: whether the customer is a beneficiary of a life insurance policy (that the firm is aware of) where risk may be increased, for example complex products with potential multiple investment accounts, those allowing early surrender with a surrender value, or beneficiaries with no obvious links to the policy holder. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Is the customer a beneficiary of a life insurance policy"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Customer risk factor: whether the customer is a third country national applying for residence rights in or citizenship of an EEA state in exchange for transfers of capital, property purchase, government bonds, or investment in corporate entities in that state. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "applying for residence rights in or citizenship of an EEA state in exchange for transfers of capital"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: One customer risk factor question in the list is whether the customer is a legal person bound by enforceable disclosure requirements that make reliable information on its beneficial owner publicly available, for example a public company listed on a stock exchange where such disclosure is a condition of listing. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Is the customer a legal person subject to enforceable disclosure requirements that ensure that reliable information"</sub>

## B. Reputation

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Reputation risk factor: adverse media reports or other information sources about the customer, for example allegations of criminality or terrorism against the customer or beneficial owners, and whether they are credible. (p. 62)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Are there any adverse media reports or other relevant information sources about the customer?"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Firms should determine the credibility of allegations on the basis of the quality and independence of the source data and the persistence of reporting, among others. The absence of criminal convictions alone may not be sufficient to dismiss allegations of wrongdoing. (p. 62)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The absence of criminal convictions alone may not be sufficient to dismiss allegations of wrongdoing"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Reputation risk factors: whether the customer, beneficial owner or anyone publicly known to be closely associated has had assets frozen due to administrative or criminal proceedings or allegations of terrorism, TF or PF (and whether the firm has reasonable grounds to suspect a past freeze); whether the customer or beneficial owner has been subject to a suspicious activity report in the past; and whether the firm has in-house information about their integrity, for example from a long-standing relationship. (p. 62)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "had their assets frozen due to administrative or criminal proceedings or allegations of terrorism, terrorist financing, or proliferation financing"</sub>

## C. Nature and behaviour

- **descriptive / context**: Nature and behaviour risk factors may be relevant to the risk of a customer or beneficial owners; not all will be apparent at the outset and may emerge only once a business relationship has been established. (p. 62)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "not all of these risk factors will be apparent at the outset, but may emerge only once a business relationship has been established"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Nature and behaviour factors: whether the customer has legitimate reasons for being unable to provide robust identity evidence (for example an asylum seeker); whether the firm has doubts about the veracity or accuracy of identity; and whether there are indications the customer might seek to avoid establishing a business relationship, for example carrying out one or several one-off transactions where a relationship might make more economic sense. (p. 62)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Are there indications that the customer might seek to avoid the establishment of a business relationship?"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Nature and behaviour factors on structure: whether ownership and control structure is transparent and makes sense, and if complex or opaque whether there is an obvious commercial or lawful rationale; whether the customer issues bearer shares or has nominee shareholders; whether it is a legal person or arrangement that could be used as an asset holding vehicle; and whether there is a sound reason for changes in ownership and control structure. (p. 62)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Does the customer issue bearer shares or have nominee shareholders?"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Nature and behaviour factors on behaviour: transactions that are complex, unusually or unexpectedly large or with an unusual pattern without apparent economic or lawful purpose or sound commercial rationale, including grounds to suspect threshold evasion; unnecessary or unreasonable secrecy (for example reluctance to share CDD information or disguising the true nature of business); source of wealth/funds not easily explained (for example through occupation, inheritance or investments); and products and services not used as expected when the relationship began. (p. 62)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Are there grounds to suspect that the customer is trying to evade certain thresholds?"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Nature and behaviour factors: where the customer is a non-resident, whether their needs could be better serviced elsewhere and whether there is a sound economic or lawful rationale for the financial service sought; and whether the customer is a non-profit organisation whose activities expose it to particularly high risks of abuse for terrorist financing. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Is the customer a non-profit organisation whose activities expose it to particularly high risks of abuse for terrorist financing purposes?"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: In identifying risk linked to countries and geographic areas, firms should consider the risk connected with three places: (a) where the customer or beneficial owner is based, (b) where their main place of business is, and (c) any jurisdiction with which they have relevant personal links. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "When identifying the risk associated with countries and geographic areas, firms should consider the risk related to"</sub>

## III. PRODUCTS, SERVICES AND TRANSACTIONS RISK FACTORS

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Transparency risk factors: the extent to which products or services facilitate anonymity or opaqueness of customer, ownership or beneficiary structures (for example pooled accounts, bearer shares, fiduciary deposits, offshore and certain trusts, foundations structured to exploit anonymity, shell companies or companies with nominee shareholders), and the extent to which a third party outside the business relationship can give instructions (for example certain correspondent banking relationships). (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "pooled accounts, bearer shares, fiduciary deposits, offshore and certain trusts"</sub>

## III. PRODUCTS, SERVICES AND TRANSACTIONS RISK FACTORS

- **recommended / recommendation**: In identifying the risk of their products, services or transactions, firms should consider the risk connected with (a) how transparent or opaque the product, service or transaction is, (b) its complexity, and (c) its value or size. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "When identifying the risk associated with their products, services or transactions, firms should consider the risk related to:"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Under value or size risk factors, one question is whether a transaction relates to oil, arms, precious metals, tobacco products, cultural artefacts, ivory or other items tied to protected species, or other items of archaeological, historical, cultural or religious importance or rare scientific value, where this raises ML/TF risk. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Is there a transaction related to oil, arms, precious metals, tobacco products, cultural artefacts, ivory and other items related to"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Firms should consider whatever relevant information they hold on ML/TF risks from the transactions listed in ML Regulation 33(6)(b)(vii), and should consider their exposure to potentially high-risk transactions involving those items, as revealed by risk-based CDD on their customers. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Firms should consider all relevant information at their disposal concerning ML/TF risks arising from transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Complexity risk factors: the extent to which a transaction is complex and involves multiple parties or jurisdictions (for example certain trade finance), versus straightforward transactions such as regular payments into a pension fund; the extent products allow third-party payments or unforeseen overpayments, and whether the firm knows the third party's identity (for example a state benefit authority or guarantor) or products are funded only by transfers from the customer's own account at an institution under comparable AML/CTF standards. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "To what extent do products or services allow payments from third parties or accept overpayments where this is not normally foreseen?"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Complexity risk factor: whether the firm understands the risks associated with its new or innovative product or service, in particular where this involves new technologies or payment methods. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Does the firm understand the risks associated with its new or innovative product or service"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Value/size risk factors: the extent products or services are cash intensive (such as many payment services and certain current accounts); the extent they facilitate or encourage high value transactions, and whether caps on transaction values or premium levels limit ML/TF use. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "To what extent are products or services cash intensive, such as many payment services but also certain current accounts?"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: In this context a "transaction" involves two parties making, benefiting from or executing it (the customer and the firm), and includes a firm facilitating a transaction between two third parties. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "includes a firm facilitating a transaction between two third parties"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: A risk-based approach should be adopted in interpreting "related to". Where such a transaction is identified, firms should consider the closeness of the link between the item and the transaction, and between the transaction and the customer and/or firm. Transactions should be considered on a risk basis in all instances, and the examples that follow are not exhaustive. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A risk-based approach should be adopted in the interpretation of “related to”"</sub>
- **recommended / typology or red flag**: Oil: the guidance says transactions to and/or from parties in the oil production process, including sales of oil to exploration companies and refiners, are in scope, and that firms should consider terrorist financing methodologies. See also FATF Reports. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Transactions made to and/or from parties in the oil production process"</sub>
- **expected / typology or red flag**: Oil: retail customers who buy refined oil products from petrol retailers should not be included within this scope. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Retail customers purchasing refined oil products from petrol retailers should not be included"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Cultural artefacts and other items of archaeological, historical, cultural or religious significance or rare scientific value: firms should adopt their definitions of these items by reference to Annex 1 of the EU's 2019 Regulation on the Import of Cultural Goods. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Firms should adopt definitions of these items considering Annex 1 of the EU’s 2019 Regulation on the Import of Cultural"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: For cultural artefacts and similar items, firms should also consider the ML/TF risks identified in the EU's 2019 Supra National Risk Assessment, which include looting and trafficking of antiquities and other artefacts. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "consider the ML/TF risks identified in the EU’s"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Ivory or other items related to protected species: firms should consider the CITES definitions of ivory and other protected species. EDD should be performed on transactions involving CITES items on a risk basis of potential illegal wildlife trafficking (IWT), which can mean the domestic or international trade of CITES species in contravention of national or international laws. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "EDD should be performed on transactions involves CITES items on a risk basis of potential illegal wildlife trafficking"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Specific IWT considerations could include in/outbound transactions involving zoos, pet stores selling animals, safari companies, hunting reserves and timber importers; large deposits/withdrawals from government officials in environment or related departments overseeing stockpiles of seized ivory, rhino horn or timber, or in forestry agencies, wildlife management authorities or CITES Management Authorities; and transactions involving Asian nationals operating import/export, international trading or transport companies in Africa and suspected of transporting CITES products. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Transactions involving Asian nationals operating import/export, international trading, or transport companies in Africa"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Examples of traders who may sell CITES products indirectly, and transactions with whom are not subject to EDD, include cosmetic retailers selling products with fragments of orchid or cacti, food retailers selling products with caviar extract, and musical instrument manufacturers selling products containing rosewood or ivory. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "who may sell CITES products indirectly, and are not subject to EDD include"</sub>

## IV. DELIVERY CHANNEL RISK FACTORS

- **recommended / recommendation**: In identifying risk linked to how the customer obtains products or services, firms should consider the risk connected with (a) how far the business relationship is conducted non-face to face without appropriate controls, and (b) any introducers or intermediaries the firm might use and how they relate to the firm. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "When identifying the risk associated with the way the customer obtains the products or services they require"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Delivery channel factors firms should consider include whether the customer is physically present for identification and, if not, whether the firm used reliable non-face to face CDD, took steps to prevent impersonation or identity fraud, and used an electronic identification process that is secure from fraud and misuse and gives an appropriate level of assurance. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Has it taken steps to prevent impersonation or identity fraud?"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Delivery channel factors firms should consider include, for a customer introduced from elsewhere in the same financial group, how far the firm can rely on the introduction as reassurance that the customer will not expose it to excessive ML/TF/PF risk, and what the firm has done to satisfy itself that the group entity applies CDD to UK standards. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "What has the firm done to satisfy itself that the group entity applies CDD measures to UK standards?"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Delivery channel factors firms should consider include, for a customer introduced by a third party such as a bank outside the group, whether the third party is a financial institution or has a main business unrelated to financial services, and what the firm has done to be satisfied that: (i) the third party applies CDD and keeps records to UK standards and is supervised for comparable AML/CTF obligations; (ii) it will hand over copies of identification and verification data immediately on request; and (iii) its CDD is good enough to be relied upon. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the third party will provide, immediately upon request, relevant copies of identification and verification data"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Delivery channel factors firms should consider include whether a customer was introduced through a tied agent with no direct firm contact, and how far the firm can be satisfied the agent gathered enough information for the firm to know its customer and the relationship's risk level. Where independent or tied agents are used, firms should also consider how far they are involved in ongoing conduct of business and how that affects the firm's knowledge of the customer and its ongoing risk management. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Has the customer been introduced through a tied agent, i.e. without direct firm contact?"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Where a third party is based in a high-risk third country identified by the UK, firms must not rely on that intermediary. Reliance may, however, be possible where the intermediary is a branch or majority-owned subsidiary undertaking of another firm established outside the UK and the firm is confident the intermediary fully complies with group-wide policies, controls and procedures in line with UK requirements. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "firms must not rely on that intermediary"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Note: with effect from 1 September 2023, firms must take appropriate steps to identify and assess the proliferation financing risks to which their business is subject, and establish and maintain policies, controls and procedures to mitigate and manage these risks (Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing (Amendment)(No.2) Regulations 2022, Reg 18A; see also Part II Sector 15 Annex 15-V). (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "With effect from 1 September 2023, firms must take appropriate steps to identify and assess the risks of proliferation financing"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Delivery channel factors firms should consider where an intermediary is used include whether it is a regulated person subject to AML obligations consistent with the UK regime; whether it is subject to effective AML supervision, including whether there are signs of inadequate compliance, for example sanctions for AML/CTF breaches; and whether it is based in a jurisdiction associated with higher ML/TF/PF risk. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Are there any indications that the intermediary’s level of compliance with applicable AML legislation or regulation is inadequate"</sub>

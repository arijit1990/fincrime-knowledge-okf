## 5 Integrate


### 5. Integrate


[pp. 47-48]

Criminals hide the proceeds of crime through complex financial networks, moving money at speed across institutions, asset types and borders. No single agency or country can disrupt these flows alone; effective action depends on sustained coordination across the public and private sectors and with international partners. The UK has established world-leading AML/CTF public–private partnerships through initiatives such as the Joint Money Laundering Intelligence Taskforce (JMLIT) and Data Fusion. We have also strengthened the UK Financial Intelligence Unit (UKFIU), delivered major Companies House reforms, and advanced international engagement including on asset recovery. This provides a strong foundation, which we will now build on – scaling what works and deepening collaboration – to deliver stronger, system-wide outcomes as the threat evolves. Supported by an uplift of over 150 new additional staff dedicated to strengthening financial intelligence and international cooperation, we will do this by:

Building a unified national financial intelligence capability: We will establish a new NECC-hosted National Financial Intelligence Service, integrating national and international public-private financial intelligence capabilities to enable earlier identification of criminality, faster action and more effective disruption. Building on SARs reform, we will strengthen the UKFIU through AI-enabled technology, increased staffing and enhanced information-gathering powers, ensuring its intelligence is fully integrated within the new service.

Enhancing exploitation of financial intelligence in wider policing: We will strengthen policing's ability to exploit financial intelligence generated through the new National Financial Intelligence Service by expanding specialist intelligence development teams across Policing. We will also improve access to intelligence through developing a national financial intelligence platform for policing and reviewing options for faster access to basic bank account information, supporting more effective investigations, asset tracing and asset recovery.

Developing a clearer view of asset ownership: We will undertake a comprehensive review of asset ownership in the UK to identify if there are any vulnerabilities and gaps, working with law enforcement and other agencies to explore how to enhance data interoperability and utility, enabling more decisive action against complex laundering arrangements.

Deepening our global partnerships: Complex laundering networks operate across borders, exploiting differences in systems and access to data. We will strengthen international and cross-sector collaboration to improve information sharing, align operational responses, and drive coordinated enforcement action against cross-border money laundering.

Improving international asset recovery cooperation: Criminal assets are routinely moved across jurisdictions as part of sophisticated laundering networks. Differences in legal frameworks and processes can delay or prevent recovery. We will strengthen international coordination to support earlier tracing, faster restraint, and more effective recovery of assets in complex cross-border cases.

##### Building a united national financial intelligence capability

[p. 48]

Financial intelligence sits at the heart of the UK’s future response to countering money laundering and increasing asset recovery. The existing SARs regime provides essential intelligence that underpins investigations, disrupts criminal activity, identifies assets to seize and supports wider system outcomes. However, to respond to the evolving threat, the UK needs a more proactive and integrated financial intelligence system, bringing together multiple sources of intelligence and ensuring it can be accessed and acted upon by the right people at the right time.

The UK has started to do this through our globally pioneering public-private partnership activity. This chapter sets out how we will build a more integrated financial intelligence ecosystem, strengthening the public-private intelligence capabilities and the SARs regime and bringing them closer together to deliver greater operational impact. We will do so in a way that safeguards the confidentiality of SARs information and maintains the trust on which the system depends.

###### Progress to date

88. Over the past decade, public and private bodies have worked more closely on financial crime, improving how intelligence is shared and acted on. The UK now has strong financial intelligence arrangements, supported by Economic Crime Levy funding and a robust legislative framework. Since 2015, JMLIT+ has grown to over 200 organisations and by 2024 it had led to around 400 arrests, more than 1,200 court orders, over £248m identified and restrained, and over 100 alerts shared with industry. The NCA and financial sector have also developed a pioneering Data Fusion capability which enables the sharing of bulk but targeted datasets from the banking sector to the NCA and has led to new targets of interest being developed and new analytical products being produced.

89. Meanwhile, the SARs Reform Programme has strengthened the UKFIU and the technology that underpins the SARs regime. UKFIU capacity has more than doubled since 2018, supported by new strategic analysis tools and SARs Intelligence Development Teams in Regional Organised Crime Units (ROCUs) to better link SARs to investigations and identify new investigative opportunities. A new SAR reporting portal has been delivered, making reporting easier for businesses, and the SARs Digital Service (SDS) is being delivered to support end - to - end analysis, storage, dissemination and feedback. The new portal has already delivered significant improvements in data quality, and SDS will enable faster and more effective use of SARs.

###### Next steps

90. But these capabilities are not yet strong enough for the scale and speed of the threat. We need to move faster to improve national information sharing, give local policing better tools to act quickly, and bring intelligence together more effectively across the system.

91. The strategy will build on the foundations laid through ECP2 and move towards a joined up, end to end framework linking key actors, data and operational activity. At its centre will be a new national financial intelligence service within the NECC, supported by investment in wider policing capability and international partnerships.

[pp. 50-52]

###### A new National Financial Intelligence Service (NFIS)

92. A new public-private national financial intelligence service will lead this modernisation. It will sit in the NCA, be supported by the FCA, and be overseen by a public-private board. It will bring together information from across sectors to give a clearer picture of how illicit money moves in the UK and internationally. More specifically, it will:

- identify criminals and networks earlier, support live investigations, and help disrupt activity and recover assets, and;

- improve how quickly high-quality intelligence is shared with national, regional and local agencies and with private-sector partners.

93. It will do this by:

- bringing together Fusion, JMLIT and aligning the UKFIU intelligence functions;

- using AI tools to process large datasets and spot suspicious patterns;

- embedding specialists from the NCA, law enforcement and supervisors;

- extending established bank partnerships to other sectors, including cryptoasset firms, Money Service Businesses and Electronic Money Institutions;

- building stronger international links to share data and shared threats.

94. The NFIS and the UKFIU will operate as closely aligned capabilities, sharing resources, technology and expertise to deliver an end-to-end financial intelligence capability. The UKFIU – equipped with enhanced capabilities, staffing and powers – will continue to be responsible for receiving, enriching and disseminating SARs intelligence. The UKFIU will work alongside NFIS who will provide a more proactive response, using advanced tooling, SARs and a wide range of other financial intelligence feeds to conduct complex enquiries. This approach will ensure the effective management of each SAR on its own merits, whilst also enabling the UK system to proactively identify and disrupt sophisticated criminal networks operating within the UK’s financial system.

95. Over the lifetime of this strategy, the NECC will progress the convergence of the functions of the NFIS and the UKFIU into a single national financial intelligence capability. This will create a more connected and coherent operating model, bringing together people, technology and intelligence while preserving the trusted public-private partnerships that underpin operational success. It will also provide a strong platform for continued SARs reform, ensuring improvements to the regime support a more integrated and effective financial intelligence response.

96. The NFIS will be one of two complementary public-private partnership capabilities in the NECC, with the other being the Online Crime Centre (OCC). While they will have different purposes, missions and threat profiles – with NFIS acting as an all-threats financial intelligence capability and the OCC designed to accelerate the UK’s response to online crime, initially focussed on fraud and high-volume cyber crime – they will share common functionality and ultimately feed into shared public-private governance to maximise our collective impacts on these threats.

###### Exploiting money laundering intelligence from wider AML/CTF-regulated sectors

97. Engagement on NFIS has so far focused mainly on the financial sector, reflecting established partnerships and its central role in generating financial intelligence. Work is also advancing with Virtual Asset Service Providers, Money Service Businesses and Electronic Money Institutions. Sectors including legal services, accountancy, gambling, and insurance, however, can also provide important insight into emerging patterns of illicit activity. Engagement is at an earlier stage, but better integration of these sectors into a national financial intelligence service would strengthen risk understanding, support earlier prevention, and help these sectors meet their obligations. Supported by the Latimer Network50 , an independent network of organisations dedicated to fighting economic crime, we will produce a review in the first year of the strategy, setting out opportunities, challenges, benefits and next steps for wider national financial intelligence service integration.

###### SARs Digital Service

98. We will complete the delivery of the SARs Digital Service (SDS), embedding the service into business - as - usual, completing the transition away from legacy systems, and continuing to enhance analytical capability and usability so that SARs data can be exploited more quickly and effectively by the UKFIU and law enforcement.

99. Wider technological improvements will also be embedded through the development of enhanced AI capability within the NCA, which will complement improvements stemming from SDS by integrating AI usage, where appropriate, into key intelligence and operational workflows.

###### UKFIU Target Operating Model

100. As the SARs Digital Service and wider reforms are rolled out, the UKFIU will move to a new technology-enabled operating model, realising efficiencies that enable the redeployment of existing resource and allowing growing headcount in priority areas focused on improving the utility and exploitation of SARs and the UKFIU’s wider designated functions.

101. A core component of the new model will be an enlarged UKFIU intelligence function which will strengthen the quality of UKFIU analysis and better support both operations and longer-term threat assessments. This function will sit in the UKFIU but be integrated with the NFIS, so SARs data is exploited as effectively as possible. It is expected these changes will result in more than a doubling of UKFIU intelligence outputs.

102. There will also be enhanced feedback loops between the UKFIU and SARs reporters, underpinned by an expanded engagement team. This will allow the UKFIU to reach more partners and provide enhanced support to reporters, regulators and law enforcement through a wider range of feedback products including: sector alerts, impact analysis on poor quality SARs, and statistical products such as SARs reporting trends at a sectoral or individual reporter level.

103. The benefits of this transformation will also extend to the UKFIU’s international function. The UKFIU will continue to lead the UK's international FIU-to-FIU cooperation, maintaining access to the Egmont Network51 and Egmont Secure Web and fulfilling its statutory information sharing responsibilities in support of domestic and international partners. These capabilities are strategic assets, enabling the UK to securely obtain, share and exploit financial intelligence while contributing to a more effective global response to illicit finance. The UKFIU’s International team will be put on a more sustainable footing, maintaining the timeliness and improving the comprehensiveness of responses to

104. More broadly, pipeline efficiencies will also be realised, including within the DAML SAR and Defence Against Terrorist Financing (DATF) SAR process, supporting earlier identification of opportunities to interdict illicit finance.

###### UKFIU powers

105. In addition to ensuring that the UKFIU is allocated with the human resources that it needs, we will also ensure that the UKFIU has access to the legislative powers that it needs to perform its functions effectively. Specifically, following a targeted industry engagement exercise in 2025, we will now enhance the UKFIU’s ability to obtain information from industry by granting the UKFIU a new legislative power to compel information without the requirement for court approval. This will ensure that the UKFIU can obtain timely and accurate information. We will also seek to design and develop a power to enable the UKFIU to suspend transactions at the request of domestic law enforcement agencies or foreign counterparts in line with updated requirements under FATF’s Recommendation 4052 .

##### Enhancing exploitation of financial intelligence in wider policing

While policing across the country will benefit from the powerful, joined up national intelligence picture that the NFIS will produce, intelligence teams within policing are also needed to translate this into local action and to develop, analyse and exploit wider intelligence. Forces across England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland further need modern financial intelligence tools and data aggregation capabilities to give investigators faster, more consistent access to information, enabling quicker threat identification, asset tracing and operational decision-making.

###### Progress to date

[pp. 55-56]

106. Through ECP2, we created the SARs Intelligence Development Teams (SIDTs), set up in 2020 through the ROCU network to turn SARs into intelligence for investigations and asset recovery. Between 2020 and FY 2024/25, they delivered over 600 intelligence developments and helped deny more than £23 million in criminal assets. We have also created a new AMLAR Intelligence Development Team, hosted by the NPCC and due to reach full capability in 2026, focused on cash-based money laundering and mule networks. Despite being in its early stages, the team has already delivered operational results, tackling money laundering and demonstrating how financial intelligence can be applied to address wider criminality.

###### Next Steps

Expanding Intelligence Development Teams in Policing

107. Through this Strategy, we will expand both the SIDT and AMLAR Intelligence Development Teams. Their focus will be to turn enhanced financial intelligence into more actionable intelligence packages for law enforcement. These teams will be closely integrated with NFIS, including through policing embeds.

###### Improving and standardising wider policing access to financial intelligence platform

108. While a national financial intelligence service aims to improve public-private financial intelligence, investigators also need fast, standard access to other data for live cases (including open - source material) that sits outside this. At present, access to these sources across policing is fragmented, relying on manual searches and inconsistent local tools, which means intelligence cannot be consistently or quickly exploited at local and regional level.

109. To address this, NPCC will develop a single open-source financial intelligence system for Policing. This will bring key data sources into one tool for investigators across England and Wales, integrate with a national financial intelligence service where appropriate, and align with wider plans for police reform. It will support quicker triage, asset identification and serious and organised crime investigations, with the potential to significantly reduce average research time.

###### Options to improve law enforcement speed of access to basic bank account information

110. Law enforcement needs fast access to basic bank account information to support investigations and asset recovery. Criminals spread funds across multiple accounts and banks, but current access can be slow and inconsistent at times, impacting efforts to trace and restrain assets. Despite existing databases/mechanisms, the UK lacks consistent ways for law enforcement agencies and financial institutions to identify all the bank accounts a suspect may have in the UK.

111. Different jurisdictions approach this common challenge in different ways. The UK will undertake a review of possible mechanisms available to address this challenge. This work will be led by the Home Office, with commitment from the private sector to engage with and supply expertise and insight. This work will be built from an agreed and specific problem statement, and parameters. It will ensure that ultimate policy decisions are informed by a clear overview of the options and also consider the trade-offs in any solution that would reduce current judicial oversight of information sharing.

112. One option that will be considered is the creation of a Bank Account Portal which could provide secure access to a limited set of bank account identifiers, allowing law enforcement to establish whether accounts exist and which institution holds them, without access to transaction data. Other options include the use, or evolution, of existing mechanisms, such as third-party information aggregators.

##### Developing a clearer view of asset ownership

Criminals, hostile state actors and kleptocrats hide assets behind complex ownership structures to disguise illicit wealth and frustrate asset recovery. Better ownership information helps investigators trace assets faster while UK corporate and asset ownership registers also underpin transparency and business confidence.

###### Progress to date

113. Significant progress has been made in strengthening the UK corporate system. The Economic Crime, Transparency and Enforcement Act 2022 established the Register of Overseas Entities, improving transparency over overseas ownership of UK property. The Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023 gave Companies House and the Insolvency Service (INSS) new powers and funding to verify and improve Register data, challenge suspicious filings and intervene earlier, while strengthening oversight of Limited Partnerships, tightening filing controls and reforming accounts filing.

###### Next Steps

[pp. 57-58]

114. These reforms have laid important foundations, but realising their full potential requires them to be fully embedded across the system. The Register needs to be supported by strong partnerships and extended to improve the completeness, accuracy and usability of ownership information, ensuring it can be accessed and exploited effectively to disrupt economic crime.

###### Reform of Companies House

115. Since 2023 Companies House and the Insolvency Service have strengthened their AML/CTF capabilities, by creating specialist teams, deploying enhanced analytical tools and improved access to intelligence to enable more rigorous scrutiny of company information and earlier identification of high-risk behaviour. With a focus on cleansing the register, Companies House has:

- introduced mandatory identity verification for new directors and People with Significant Control in November 2025.

- removed 3,279 entities from the Register of Overseas Entities.

- removed over 135,000 officer addresses, almost 110,000 PSC addresses and over 170,000 Registered Office Addresses from the PSC register.

116. More remains to be done. Given the scale, complexity and reach of these changes, implementation was deliberately phased, allowing over five million companies to prepare and adapt and enabling extensive system, operational and legislative changes to be delivered. Looking ahead, full implementation of the reforms will create a coherent end-toend system that strengthens compliance, improves the reliability and usability of ownership data, and better enables its exploitation to disrupt economic crime, while mitigating risks associated with opaque ownership structures. Taken together, these reforms position Companies House to fulfil its 2030 vision: to be the trusted guardian of corporate transparency, acting as a catalyst for economic growth, while protecting business and people from harm.

###### Asset Ownership Review

117. As part of our commitment in the Anti-Corruption Strategy 2025, we have launched a government-led review into asset ownership in the UK. The review will examine how we can better identify any vulnerabilities and gaps which can be exploited by criminals and bad actors to ensure our systems work efficiently and are robust against misuse.

118. Building on engagement with departments, law enforcement and the private sector, the review is focussing on:

- Searchability : making it easier to find relevant information, so users can more quickly identify people or assets through clear, consistent and well-structured data.

- Accuracy : ensuring the data is correct, up-to-date and verified.

[pp. 58-59]

- Scope: considering whether the range of information covered should be expanded, so more asset types, ownership details and relationships are captured.

- Accessibility : ensuring appropriate users can access and use the data easily, while maintaining safeguards where needed.

119. The review will conclude in late 2026. Recommendations developed as part of the review will focus on technical, tangible improvements to existing asset registers and set out longer-term opportunities for more fundamental reform to inform future policy development.

##### Deepening our global partnerships

As the threat from money laundering becomes increasingly global, fragmented national responses are limiting our ability to prevent, detect and disrupt criminal activity at scale. Building on the UK’s strong domestic foundations, this section sets out how we will strengthen international collaboration, enhancing public-private partnerships, improving cross-border information sharing and driving more coordinated enforcement action, particularly in relation to crypto-enabled crime and asset recovery opportunities.

###### Progress to date

120. Through ECP2, the UK committed to reducing the threat posed by international illicit finance to UK interests by supporting capability, cooperation and resilience overseas. As set out in the 2025 ECP2 Outcomes Report, between 2023 and 2025 HMT delivered 28 Technical Assistance programmes53 , supporting partner countries to strengthen legislative frameworks, supervision and operational capability. This activity has been reinforced by commitments in the UK’s Fraud Strategy and Anti-Corruption Strategy as well as progress in our bilateral relationships, including with the UAE, and by supporting the Financial Action Task Force’s new standards on asset recovery. Together, this activity has strengthened international partnerships and improved alignment in tackling shared illicit finance risks.

121. More broadly, close partnership with the Crown Dependencies (CDs) and Overseas Territories (OTs) is key to reducing the abuse of complex corporate and financial structures, which are a common feature of sophisticated cross-border money laundering schemes. The commitments on implementing high-quality accessible registers of beneficial ownership made in the 2025 Anti-Corruption Strategy remains the UK’s ambition for the CDs and OTs. Alongside this, there is positive ongoing law enforcement collaboration between the UK, CDs and OTs including efforts to enhance access to relevant information and further strengthen investigative capabilities to mitigate the threat.

###### Next Steps

[pp. 59-60]

122. Building on these foundations, the UK must now go further by strengthening public sector collaboration internationally, unlocking more effective information sharing across the private sector, and ensuring its response to fast moving threats such as crypto-enabled crime is coordinated, operational and global by design. A key, related milestone in driving forward this agenda will be the UK-hosted Illicit Finance Summit, which is part of a wider package of UK global leadership on combating illicit finance and economic crime, including the UK’s Presidency of the Financial Action Task Force.

###### Strengthen collective action against shared illicit finance risks

123. Illicit finance is increasingly transnational, with criminal networks exploiting differences between jurisdictions, gaps in information sharing and weaknesses in international systems to move and conceal the proceeds of crime. This includes risks linked to money laundering, fraud, corruption, sanctions evasion and the misuse of new financial technologies, including cryptoassets.

124. The UK has an established role in shaping international responses to illicit finance, including through its previous presidencies of the Financial Action Task Force (FATF) and the G7, and through its engagement in the G20 and other multilateral fora. Building on this experience and its existing bilateral and multilateral relationships, the UK will work with international partners to strengthen collective understanding of shared illicit finance risks and identify opportunities for more coordinated action.

###### International information sharing

126. The UK has made good progress on domestic public - private partnerships, but public - sector cooperation with other countries is still limited. Without sustained collaboration, it is harder to spot shared threats early, understand weaknesses across regions, and build a clear global picture. This makes it easier for international money laundering and fraud networks to exploit gaps between jurisdictions.

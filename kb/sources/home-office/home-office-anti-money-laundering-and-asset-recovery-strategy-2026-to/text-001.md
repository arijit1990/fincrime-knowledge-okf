### Ministerial foreword


[p. 7]

Money laundering is the lifeblood of crime, powering drug trafficking, people smuggling, fraud and corruption. It enables criminals, corrupt elites and hostile actors to move and exploit their illegal gains, compromising our national security, eroding trust in our institutions, undermining our prosperity and causing real harm to the public across the UK. This Government will not tolerate it.

We have made clear progress. Reforms to our economic crime system, and closer collaboration across government, law enforcement, supervisors and industry are delivering stronger results, with operations like Destabilise, which dismantled billion-dollar Russian money laundering networks, and Machinize, which raided hundreds of high street criminal fronts, showing what is possible when we act together with ambition and coordination. But the threat is evolving rapidly. If we do not keep up, we will fall behind. That is not something this Government is prepared to accept.

This Strategy marks a turning point. Backed by at least £550m of investment between 2026–29, it is a step change in how we confront money laundering, built around a simple but powerful idea: crime should not pay. With asset recovery at the heart of our response, our aim is to take the profit out of criminality, disrupt criminals, and make the UK a hostile environment for dirty money.

We will sharpen our focus on the most harmful activity and drive a stronger, more coordinated cross-system effort. We will build a new national financial intelligence service to detect and disrupt criminal funds as never before and give law enforcement the tools and technology they need to keep pace with an evolving threat. We will clean up our high streets, cracking down on the businesses and cash-based fronts that criminals rely on in plain sight of our communities. We will reform anti-money laundering and counter-terrorist financing supervision to raise standards and support the vast majority of professionals who act as responsible gatekeepers. We will intensify action against the enablers who facilitate crime, because complicity is culpability; and we will extend our reach to trace and seize illicit funds, including those hidden in cryptoassets.

This Government was elected to restore trust and deliver for working people. Tackling money laundering is part of that mission. It protects our communities, strengthens our economy and sends a clear message to those who seek to exploit our country: there is no safe haven here.

Dan Jarvis MBE MP Security Minister

Lucy Rigby KC MP Economic Secretary to the Treasury

## 1 Executive summary


### 1. Executive summary


[p. 9]

1. Money laundering enables criminals, corrupt elites and hostile actors to hide and use the proceeds of crime. It undermines core Government priorities on national security, growth and safer streets by damaging the integrity and reputation of the UK’s financial and legal systems and by sustaining serious and organised crime, terrorism and hostile state activity.

2. The UK has made strong progress over the last decade, and three years on from the publication of Economic Crime Plan 2 (ECP2), performance is improving across the board. In the financial year 2025/26, there were 3,158 system-wide illicit finance disruptions1 (up 15% on 2024/25), and 4,085 money laundering convictions2 (up 11% on 2024/25). Asset recovery is also increasing with £345.3 million recovered3 (up 9% on the six-year median), £1.1 billion denied to criminals (up 17% on the six-year median), and £26.1 million returned to victims (up 29% on the six-year median).

3. Alongside these headline results, significant operational successes, including Operation Machinize, demonstrate a shift towards intelligence-led disruption of money laundering in local communities. Targeted enforcement against cash-intensive businesses such as barber shops, vape stores and tobacco outlets has resulted in 924 arrests , over £10.7 million in suspected criminal funds seized or restrained, and more than £2.7 million in illicit goods destroyed.4

4. Transparency has also improved, with over 33,000 entities registered on the Register of Overseas Entities and more than 106,000 addresses removed from the Companies House register where personal data was used without consent, as of end FY2025–2026.

5. But the threat is evolving fast. Online criminality, as well as newer technologies such as AI, cryptoassets and wider fintech are making it easier to move and conceal money at scale, including across borders. This strategy sets the direction for the UK’s anti - money laundering and asset recovery system over the next three years: it defines what the system will prioritise, how partners will work together, and where we will invest to stay ahead of the threat. The strategy has three core objectives:

6. This strategy is built around three pillars that mutually reinforce each other and explain how we will deliver it:

Target: We will focus time and effort on the criminals and activities that cause the most harm, cutting back low-value “box-ticking” work.

Integrate: We will share information and work together better, so we can spot suspicious activity sooner and coordinate action to stop it.

Empower: We will give the system the right people, training, technology and legal powers to act quickly, and remove barriers that slow things down.

7. In practice, this means we will: focus on the biggest threats, join up the information we already have, and give investigators the tools to act faster. Backed by at least £520m of investment over the next three years from the Economic Crime Levy, a further £30m from the high streets package announced at Budget 2025, and more than 500 new officers, the strategy will deliver the following:

Year 1 – 2026/27 : Year 1 establishes the architecture the whole strategy depends on. It brings together the capabilities the UK has developed over the past decade and connects them into a single, coherent system.

Year 2 – 2027/28 : Year 2 is where the architecture built in Year 1 will move to the next stage. Intelligence moves faster. Disruption increases. The prosecution and recovery pipelines start to be strengthened end to end.

Year 3 – 2028/29 : Year 3 is where structural change translates into a system that can deliver impact. Every part of the system will be operating at a higher level, and the results should be visible.

8. By the end of strategy, we aim for the system to be having greater impact across the board, including:

- More assets recovered , including cross-border cryptoasset seizures.

- More networks dismantled, with the professional money laundering taskforce and NECC multinational intelligence partnership generating results.

[p. 14]

- The prosecution pipeline is better understood and in the process of being addressed.

- High streets that are harder to launder through, backed by strengthened closure powers.

- Our supervision is intelligence-led and more effective, as a result of reform.

- Ownership data is more accurate, searchable and exploited to trace assets linked to hostile actors and corrupt elites.

- Highly trained police and prosecutors nationwide and more specialists in role.

- AI and advanced analytics improving the identification, investigation and disruption of money laundering.

- The UK’s international leadership delivering measurable domestic outcomes through stronger cooperation against illicit finance.

9. Taken together, these reforms aim to improve the UK’s overall ability to spot and act on illicit finance risks, by strengthening intelligence flows, sharpening the focus on those who enable money laundering, and making supervision more consistently effective. This will give the system a clearer view of where risk sits across the whole economy, not only within the anti-money laundering and counter terrorist financing (AML/CTF) regulated perimeter.

10. This AMLAR Strategy has been created through extensive and iterative engagement led by the Home Office and HM Treasury, drawing on input from across the private sector, law enforcement, supervisors and civil society to ensure it is evidence based, shaped by diverse perspectives and supported across the system. Alongside the UK Anti-Corruption Strategy and UK Fraud Strategies, this Strategy forms part of the Government’s overall approach to economic crime as will be set out in the forthcoming Economic Crime Plan 2026–29.

11. This AMLAR Strategy also supports ongoing progress towards Police Reform, which aims to establish a new National Police Service (NPS) as part of a modernised policing system with fewer local forces. National responsibility for economic crime, including money laundering and asset recovery, will move into the NPS. The National Crime Agency (NCA) will also move into the NPS as part of these reforms.

## 2 Why tackle money laundering and recover assets?


### 2. Why tackle money laundering and recover assets?


[p. 17]

The scale and impact of money laundering

12. Money motivates crime across the UK, from organised gangs, people smugglers and fraudsters to corrupt elites and hostile actors. It damages communities, threatens national security, and harms the UK’s reputation as a safe place to do business – around a third of respondents (32%) in a recent Home Office public survey were concerned about money laundering in the UK5 .

13. The UK continues to face a high level of money - laundering risk across all regions6 , and it is a realistic possibility that more than £100bn is laundered through and within the UK, or through UK - registered company structures, each year (around 10% of annual government spending)7 . In England and Wales, identified proceeds of certain profit - driven crime in 2023/24 is estimated by the Home Office to have generated at least £21.9bn. However, owing to the scope of crime types included and geographical coverage, the total amount – including unidentified and overseas proceeds – remains unknown. Conservative estimates suggest that between £6.1bn to £8.9bn may have been reinvested into further crime, money used to fund the next wave of offending. The majority, between £13.0bn to £15.8bn, may have been spent on broader consumption, including on assets such as real estate and luxury goods8 .

14. Around 2% of businesses with employees reported known or suspected money laundering in the 12 months prior to the 2024 Home Office Economic Crime Survey (equating to approximately 33,500 businesses), with an estimated 225,000 incidents across the business population9 .

15. Understanding the true scale of the problem is the foundation for tackling it, and the government is committed to building a clearer, more complete picture of money laundering activity so that our response is targeted where it matters most.

###### Harms the UK’s international reputation and growth

16. The UK is determined to protect its standing as a trusted global financial centre, and this strategy sets out how we will work with international partners to close the gaps that criminals exploit across borders.

17. Strong institutions and effective regulatory frameworks are associated with greater investor confidence, as they signal a transparent and predictable investment environment. Money laundering can undermine this by damaging a country’s reputation and increasing perceived financial risk.10 Money laundering can also distort investment by directing funds into sectors that are easier to launder through, rather than those that are most productive. This can reduce economic efficiency and distort fair competition.11 Studies also suggest that higher levels of money laundering are linked to slightly weaker economic growth over time, although the exact size of the effect is uncertain.12

18. Dirty money fuelling crime and corruption is also a transnational challenge, and many of the threats the UK faces originate or operate across overseas jurisdictions. When the UK’s financial system or UK companies are involved in transnational money laundering cases this undermines the UK’s international credibility.

###### Denigrates and undermines the British high street

19. The government is committed to protecting legitimate businesses and the communities they serve, ensuring that high streets remain places of genuine enterprise rather than cover for criminal activity.

20. Money launderers can use “front” businesses, mixing illegal cash with genuine takings, fake invoices, or inflated sales to make it look legitimate. This distorts competition: they can undercut honest firms or trade at a loss13 because the aim is to clean money, not run a viable business. Over time, this may displace lawful and productive businesses from the market and could result in a street dominated by illegal businesses. ###### Threatens UK national security

21. Disrupting the financial flows that sustain serious and organised crime is a national security priority, and the UK's law enforcement and intelligence agencies are working in closer coordination than ever to identify and cut off the money that makes serious and organised crime possible.

22. Money laundering helps serious and organised crime survive and grow. It can also help fund terrorism and hostile state activity by making it easier to move, store and use money without being detected. As the UK’s National Security Strategy 2025 makes clear: “all elements of our security are supported by our ability to tackle illicit finance”.16 Evidence suggests the largest impact of money laundering to society is likely to be the facilitation of future crime, which have economic and social harms in themselves.17 Dirty money can also be used to buy strategic assets, influence public life, or support extremism, undermining democratic institutions and the UK’s resilience.

###### Exacerbates the financial and psychological harm to victims

23. Protecting victims, and preventing new ones, sits at the heart of this strategy. That means not only disrupting laundering at source but ensuring that when crime does occur, we recover assets faster and return them to those who have suffered.

24. Money laundering is a key enabler of profit driven offences, which have economic and social harms in themselves. The existence of money laundering helps offenders keep what they have stolen, and it makes it harder and slower for victims to get money back, especially once it has been moved through complex transactions, offshore structures, professional enablers or cryptoassets. When money cannot be recovered quickly, people can be left in hardship for longer and lose trust that the system will protect them.

25. Money laundering can also cause direct physical and emotional harms to individuals involved in the laundering process itself, for example, “money mules” who in some cases can be pressured or tricked into moving money through their bank accounts. ###### A coordinated response to a complex threat

[p. 20]

26. The scale and breadth of these harms make clear that money laundering is not a victimless or abstract threat. It corrodes communities, distorts markets, funds further criminality and undermines the institutions that people rely on. Addressing it requires a response that is as ambitious and coordinated as the threat itself. This strategy sets out how the UK will do that: by making the system’s defences more effective and efficient, by disrupting the criminal networks that launder money at scale, and by recovering more of the assets that sustain them.

##### The role of asset recovery

27. Taking money away from criminals is one of the most important tools available to combat money laundering and other economic crimes. Asset recovery powers, capabilities and resources enable authorities to seize illicit gains. Taken together, this activity aims to:

- a. deprive criminals of their profit, removing the reward that makes crime worthwhile;

- b. disrupt criminal networks, limiting their ability to fund activity and move goods;

- c. deter criminality, sending a clear message criminal profits are not safe;

- d. discredit criminality, showing it is not a reliable or legitimate way to succeed, and;

- e. deliver recovered assets to victims and the public, by returning money to those harmed and supporting wider public benefit.

28. While the scale of money laundering is widely thought to reach into the hundreds of billions, only a proportion of these illicit flows are ultimately recoverable in practice. Asset recovery is inherently complex and has become more so over time: criminals exploit opaque ownership structures and weak transparency around beneficial ownership, move assets rapidly across jurisdictions to evade enforcement, and increasingly use new technologies such as cryptoassets along with privacy - enhancing tools that obscure tracing19 . These challenges are compounded by the globalisation of financial systems, the professionalisation of criminal enablers, and the relatively low barriers to accessing sophisticated laundering techniques, all of which make identifying, freezing and recovering assets more difficult and resource intensive.

29. Assets are returned to victims as a priority, helping repair harm and build confidence in the justice system. In some cases, money is reinvested in law enforcement capabilities to strengthen capability, boost investigations, and drive further recovery activity, creating a cycle of disruption and enforcement. A portion of funds is also returned to the public purse, recognising that serious and organised crime imposes real costs on society beyond individual victims. ###### Case Study: Landmark Cryptoasset Seizure

A landmark Metropolitan Police Service (MPS) and Crown Prosecution Service investigation into a complex crypto-enabled money laundering scheme linked to a global fraud resulted in the conviction and sentencing of three individuals and the largest cryptoasset seizure in the UK.

An investigation was launched in 2018 upon receipt of intelligence about the transfer of criminal assets, including attempts to purchase high value London property where “know your customer” questions were asked under anti-money laundering regulations. The operation required extensive financial tracing and cryptoasset analysis, the use of international legal assistance and cross law enforcement working. Using state of the art technology and digital forensic capabilities, the dedicated cryptoasset team established in the MPS were able to trace and ultimately seize 61,000 Bitcoin.

The MPS worked extensively with the Chinese authorities, utilising both formal and informal international routes to information sharing, to obtain further evidence which was decisive for the criminal investigation.

Three defendants were convicted in 2024–25 of various money laundering offences under the Proceeds of Crime Act 2002 and sentenced to 11 years and 8 months, 6 years and 8 months, and 4 years and 11 months imprisonment.

The CPS is leading on the subsequent civil recovery proceedings for the 61,000 Bitcoin, having already secured confiscation orders for over £3 million and £5.6 million.

##### Improving international asset recovery cooperation

The UK already has strong international partnerships to trace and recover criminal assets, but challenges persist within both the UK system and those of our international counterparts. Differences in legal frameworks, capability and operational processes can delay or block asset recovery, particularly in complex cross-border cases. This section sets out how we will deepen international cooperation to improve the identification, tracing and recovery of criminal assets in the UK and overseas.

###### Progress to date

[pp. 61-62]

129. Since ECP2, the UK has strengthened international asset recovery through improved digitisation, case management and multilingual Mutual Legal Assistance (MLA) guidance, alongside an Account Freezing Order pilot to support international restraint. The UK has also played a leading role on international efforts including Interpol’s Silver Notice Pilot and Europol’s Asset Search and Seize Enforcement Taskforce, while the Anti-Corruption Strategy sets out our ambition to recover corrupt assets and use them to reduce harm overseas, including through capacity-building to affected states and exploring compensation for victims in foreign bribery cases. The UK further hosts the International Anti-Corruption Centre, a world-leading capability supporting cross-border asset tracing and recovery, particularly with lower-income countries.

###### Next Steps

130. As international asset recovery activity remains a significant and complex area, the UK must ensure its systems keep pace with demand and evolving expectations. This requires sustained focus on capacity, coordination and specialist capability to support timely cooperation, early asset tracing and effective international outcomes.

Asset Recovery Office

131. International best practice increasingly expects Asset Recovery Offices (AROs) to act as a clear national hub for asset tracing and responding to intelligence requests. The UK’s ARO, based within the UKFIU, already provides this key coordination and engagement function. However there remain opportunities to strengthen current arrangements, improve access to specialist capabilities, ensure earlier asset tracing and make it clearer for

international partners to know where to direct enquiries (including ARO and MLA pre-order requests).

132. We will address this by establishing a new dedicated ARO function within the NCA. This will provide an improved capability to process international requests and strengthen early asset identification and tracing to reduce the risk of dissipation as well as supporting prosecution agencies with seizing and recovering assets.

## 6 Empower


### 6. Empower


[pp. 65-66]

The UK has significantly invested in law enforcement’s capacity to tackle money laundering and strengthen asset recovery, delivering clear progress and strong results. However, the threat continues to grow in scale, sophistication, and harm. To remain effective, we must build on what works while addressing persistent barriers that limit investigation, disruption, and recovery. Reforms to improve system agility, integration, and intelligence will only deliver impact if the operational system has the capability, capacity, and specialist expertise to act, otherwise opportunities to target the highest-harm money laundering and recover criminal assets will continue to be missed .

Progress has already been delivered through Economic Crime Plan 2, including through the recruitment of 475 financial investigators (99% now in post), targeted training uplifts, significant enhancements to asset recovery such as the creation of the Asset Management Office for Policing, and operational success against complex money laundering networks. We will build on this progress, including through the recruitment of over 500 new additional enforcement specialists across the system, to enhance operational capability in money laundering and asset recovery by:

Enhancing our ability to tackle complex money laundering : We will ensure the system has the operational capacity, capability, powers, and understanding to pursue high-harm, professional money laundering networks, enhancing its ability to act decisively and deliver impact against the worst offenders.

Strengthening the end-to-end asset recovery system: We will ensure the asset recovery system has the powers, capability, capacity, and partnerships needed to operate effectively across law enforcement and maximise asset recovery outcomes, including for victims.

Investing in people and technology: We will ensure the system is equipped with the skilled workforce, specialist expertise, and technological capability needed to tackle cross-cutting money laundering threats. This includes investing in training, funding, and shared data, technology, and AI capabilities to support more effective action across the whole system.

##### Enhancing our ability to tackle complex money laundering

Money laundering is a diffuse and highly complex crime, especially when carried out at scale. As highlighted in the NCA’s National Strategic Assessment 2025 and the NRA international laundering networks use sophisticated methods to provide professional laundering services across multiple crime types, enabling activity from fraud to sanctions evasion. Tackling money laundering in its own right is therefore critical to disrupting the broader criminal ecosystem and removing the infrastructure that enables a wide range of harms. However, this complexity makes identifying, disrupting and prosecuting money laundering challenging and can limit our ability to increase asset recovery. This section sets out the measures we will take to better detect, target and bring to justice those responsible for these complex cases.

###### Progress to date

[pp. 66-67]

135. As a system, we have already made progress in our ability to disrupt complex money laundering. In 2021, the CPS significantly strengthened its approach to prosecuting money laundering by publishing updated Money Laundering Offences Legal Guidance which clarified that standalone charges may be brought without proving a predicate offence. More broadly, under ECP2, the NCA and OPBAS jointly published a professional enablers strategy which marked a major step forward which strengthened a whole - system response. This strategy, which ended in April 2026, provided a clearer framework for reducing the threat posed by complicit professionals. Operationally, increases in specialist capability and capacity have driven significant advances in identifying, targeting and dismantling sophisticated money laundering networks

###### Case Study: Operation Destabilise

NCA-led Operation Destabilise disrupted a multi-billion-dollar Russian-speaking money laundering network supporting a range of threat actors, including ransomware groups and transnational drugs networks. This represented one of the most substantial interventions of its kind, resulting in the seizure of over £25 million in cash and crypto, 128 arrests and a range of other disruptions. Crucially, this money laundering network now finds the UK a much less permissive operating environment.

The investigation also exposed clear links between street-level money laundering in the UK and wider national security threats. Individuals linked to Russian intelligence services used UK-connected networks to fund espionage activity in Europe, including support to a Bulgarian network convicted of spying for Russia, while another network acquired a bank in Kyrgyzstan to facilitate sanctions evasion and payments linked to Russian military activity.

###### Next Steps

136. Building on the progress already made, there is an opportunity to go further to address persistent barriers translating disruption into prosecutions and deterrence. Alongside this, we will more tightly integrate intelligence, operational activity and asset recovery, and

target the professional and corporate infrastructures that underpin complex, cross - border money laundering networks, including those exploited by hostile states and linked to wider national security threats.

###### Tackling international money laundering network

137. Cross - border money laundering networks move the proceeds of serious crime, such as drugs, fraud and corruption, and in some cases facilitate the movement of illicit finance linked to kleptocratic regimes and hostile actors. They use hard to disrupt infrastructure, including crypto, informal value transfer and cash-based laundering54 . The impact of disrupting individual actors is inherently limited. We need to target the systems that keep these networks operating to achieve bigger impact and enable major seizures. We will build on Operation DESTABILISE by targeting more high - harm networks, focusing on the highest threat jurisdictions, and setting up a dedicated taskforce model that brings together intelligence, operational activity and asset recovery to deliver sustained disruption.

[pp. 67-68]

138. We will provide significant funding to strengthen capability across the system, including NCA intelligence and command and control functions (with teams focussed on priority jurisdictions), digital forensics, more proactive money laundering teams in additional police forces, and increased specialist asset recovery capability in the NCA. This will increase the pace of disruption, make it harder and riskier to launder money through the UK, reduce illicit cash and crypto flows, increase asset recovery – including through the property sector – and strengthen our ability to target and recover funds linked to both.

###### Review of money laundering prosecutions

142. While money laundering prosecutions are brought forward, there are continued inherent challenges in pursuing these offences. Modern money laundering is often complex, involving companies, professional enablers and cross - border activity, which can make cases more difficult to investigate, prosecute and present to juries.

143. We will carry out an evidence-based review of the money laundering prosecution pipeline to identify what would most improve outcomes. It will examine where cases drop out from investigation to charge, whether charging practice supports deterrence (including use of standalone money laundering charges), and what is preventing effective prosecutions of companies and other legal persons. On the latter, it will consider the impact of the Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023 changes to the identification doctrine when looking at barriers to corporate prosecutions. The review will align with related work, including consideration of relevant recommendations in the Independent

Review of Disclosure and Fraud Offences. It will focus on improving use of existing powers and controls, not creating new offences.

##### Strengthening the end-to-end asset recovery system

The UK’s asset recovery system is a central pillar of our response, enabling the deprivation of the profits that sustain money laundering. The UK is internationally recognised for its comprehensive criminal and civil asset recovery regime, with over 250 agencies provided with the tools and powers to seize criminal assets through the Proceeds of Crime Act (POCA) 2002. Building on significant reforms since 2020, this section sets out the policy, legislative and operational changes we will take to remove structural barriers to effective recovery, strengthen specialist capability across law enforcement, and deliver a more proactive and coordinated response to crypto asset recovery. It also sets out how we will explore stronger public-private partnerships and legislative reform to adapt to increasingly complex methods and technological change.

###### Progress to date

145. Significant policy and legislative reforms in recent years have strengthened the UK’s asset recovery regime, making it more targeted and resilient, contributing to increases in assets recovered. Since 2020, the Financial Services Act 2021, the Economic Crime (Transparency and Enforcement) Act 2022 and the Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023 have expanded civil recovery powers, including reforms to Unexplained Wealth Orders. The Crime and Policing Act 2026 will also deliver the biggest reform of the confiscation regime since it began, streamlining processes, reducing burdens on courts, strengthening victims’ interests (including allowing compensation orders to be amended when confiscation orders change), and improving enforcement. The Act also introduces cost protections to support agencies taking on high - harm, complex cases.

[pp. 70-71]

146. Asset recovery was also a core part of Economic Crime Plan 2, where improvements were delivered through the Anti Money Laundering and Asset Recovery programme. This included creating the Asset Management Office for Policing to improve the handling and disposal of seized assets; a new performance dashboard and social research function; and a new secure IT system to replace Joint Asset Recovery Database (JARD). Further, the programme funded research into the system. Alongside this strategy, the Government has published a report that highlights issues such as lack of awareness of POCA powers amongst agencies and delays in the court system as barriers to effective Asset Recovery55 . ARIS Top Slice funding has also supported innovative work across law enforcement, including Asset Confiscation Enforcement Teams, asset denial activity in the waste sector, and research into offender behaviour to improve deterrence and disruption.

###### Next Steps

147. While these reforms have strengthened the UK’s asset recovery system, further action is now needed, ensuring the system operates effectively across law enforcement, makes better use of partnerships, remains fit for the evolving threat, delivers stronger outcomes for victims, and maximises asset recovery impact.

###### Uplifting key resource

148. To increase the volume, pace and impact of asset recovery, we need to strengthen the system’s ability to convert intelligence into operational outcomes. While intelligence and powers have improved, delivery is constrained by shortages and uneven distribution of specialist capability, particularly financial investigators, legal expertise, asset tracing, confiscation enforcement capacity and forensic accounting. Without targeted investment in these operational roles, we cannot consistently pursue more cases, tackle greater complexity, or move quickly from intelligence to action.

149. We will therefore prioritise a focused uplift in specialist resource across the asset recovery system, aligned to the above-mentioned new legislative duties and expanding use of civil and criminal recovery tools. This includes:

- Uplifting NCA asset recovery capability: We will increase financial investigator and legal capacity, expand Proceeds of Crime Centre staffing, and strengthen specialist asset-tracing teams, enabling more complex cases to be progressed at pace and sustaining a pipeline of accredited financial investigators.

- Growing confiscation enforcement teams to deliver Crime and Policing Act duties: We will improve cross-agency coordination and front-load enforcement earlier in cases, alongside increased staffing across nine ROCUs’ Asset Confiscation Enforcement teams, including roles engaging offenders in custody and revisiting older orders where circumstances may have changed.

- Expanding CPS civil recovery capacity: We will increase funding for the CPS civil recovery team to amplify action against high-harm individuals who are hard to prosecute and support wider partner use of civil recovery powers as demand grows.

[pp. 71-72]

- Establishing a nationally coordinated forensic accounting capability (NCA-hosted): We will provide dedicated funding to recruit qualified forensic accountants, set clear operating models, and align support across the NCA, ROCUs, local policing, the SFO and specialist CPS teams, following the Expert Laundering Evidence Cadre model to support high-complexity cases end-to-end.

###### Expanding the Asset Management Office

152. The Asset Management Office (AMO) for Policing has started to evidence how vital a centralised supporting function is for policing; however, there remains a capability gap for other POCA-enabled agencies. Against the backdrop of progress in policing and the opportunity provided by the Council of Europe mandate to create an AMO, we will create the first UK AMO that supports all POCA agencies. This will significantly extend our capacity to support a wider range of agencies and investigations, strengthening the management and realisation of assets, increasing the use of POCA restraint and freezing powers, and delivering improved asset recovery outcomes and value for money.

153. We will expand the AMO for policing to fulfil the functions of a multi-agency AMO by the end of 2028/29. The expanded AMO will include secondees from other agencies and maintain a new asset management register to better record asset data and performance.

###### Legislation to strengthen asset recovery

154. POCA has been central to the UK’s approach to ensuring crime does not pay, and its core aim – recovering illicit assets – remains right. But the threat has changed. Criminals now use more complex laundering methods, including digital assets, and operate through sophisticated, cross - border networks. This can make it hard to prove criminality or intent, especially where key evidence sits overseas in jurisdictions that will not cooperate. The UK has already made several improvements to its legislative framework, but it is important to continue to review and ensure we have the tools and powers to address the developing threats faced. To strengthen asset recovery, we will consider whether further legislative change is needed by consulting on potential new powers and changes.

We will expand the listed assets regime to include vehicles and high-value clothing and accessories.

###### Increasing the role of the private sector to improve asset recovery

156. Alongside increasing public sector capacity through investment in people, powers and structures, we recognise that the private sector holds significant expertise and unique capabilities and powers that could support efforts to tackle the scale and complexity of asset recovery. While public - private collaboration in this area has taken place to date, it has been largely based on procurement, focusing on discrete technical activity. We see scope to build on this collaboration in a more structured way, while maintaining clear public sector leadership and accountability.

157. To explore this further, we have worked closely with private sector stakeholders to explore options to go further. Firstly, we will develop a robust evidence base, drawing on close engagement with private sector partners, to map existing public–private collaboration across the asset recovery lifecycle. This will provide a clearer understanding of current

practice, identify gaps and barriers, and highlight where there is the greatest scope to strengthen collaborative working, informing the design of future interventions.

158. Following this, we will establish a pilot to test greater public - private asset recovery collaboration, building on the evidence from our engagement with private sector partners and the mapping of existing collaboration across the asset recovery lifecycle. The pilot will test whether a deeper collaboration models can unlock greater strategic use of private sector capabilities and improve asset recovery outcomes.

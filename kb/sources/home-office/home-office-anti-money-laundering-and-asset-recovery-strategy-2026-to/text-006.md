###### Victims

159. We have strengthened the link between asset recovery and victim compensation. Where an offender cannot pay both compensation and confiscation, compensation to victims is paid first. The Crime and Policing Act has gone further by enabling additional compensation when a confiscation order is later increased. But victims can still face barriers to getting better outcomes, across both criminal and civil cases, in the UK and internationally. We will carry out targeted research to understand the problems victims still face and where the system falls short. We will use the findings to close gaps and give victims more chances to recover their losses from offenders.

###### Criminal Justice System

160. As activity across the system to tackle money laundering increases, including through enhanced asset recovery, we recognise that there are consequential pressures on the criminal justice system, driven by increased demand on courts and the wider system. However, these challenges are not unique to anti money laundering and asset recovery measures but are indicative of broader pressures facing the system as a whole. As such, alongside the measures set out, this strategy will support and align with the forthcoming Economic Crime Plan 2026–2029, which will take forward a cross-system programme to improve case progression and capacity so the system can better sustain increased disruption and asset recovery activity.

##### Investing in people and technology

Disrupting money laundering and recovering criminal assets increasingly depends on system-wide capability keeping pace with evolving threats and wider reform across the economic crime response. This means tackling crypto-enabled crime, strengthening core skills across the system, and maintaining critical operational technology. As complexity and ambition increase, gaps in people, capability or systems can limit impact. This section sets out how sustained investment in people, skills and technology will support effective disruption and asset-recovery outcomes.

###### Progress to date

[pp. 75-76]

161. Under ECP2, the UK strengthened its economic crime workforce, with 99% of the 475 additional officers now recruited and more than 2,000 accredited financial investigators in place. Building on this, a cross-government strategy to establish an Association of Economic Crime Professionals has been agreed, with delivery and further detail to be set out in Economic Crime Plan 2026–29. In parallel, the Home Office and the NCA are already working to enhance the response to economic crime through the development of AI enabled tools that assist law enforcement to disrupt criminality, share intelligence and pursue criminal funds.

###### Next Steps

162. To continue building on this progress and go further, the system must strengthen training and skills, further uplift the response to crypto - enabled money laundering, and make more effective use of enabling technologies. As threats evolve and become more technical and international, sustained investment in people, specialist capability and core systems is essential to maintain momentum and deliver effectively.

###### Training uplifts

163. Ensuring the system has the right skills and training to remain agile and respond effectively to evolving economic crime threats is central to the UK’s approach. Since 2012, the Economic and Cyber Crime Academy, led by the City of London Police, has delivered specialist training that strengthens law enforcement capability to identify money laundering, exploit financial intelligence and pursue complex investigations. Building on this foundation, we will provide funding to enhance and expand money laundering investigation training through the Economic and Cyber Crime Academy.

###### Exploiting AI opportunities with law enforcement

165. As this Strategy has previously set out, AI has a dual role: it presents a risk where exploited by criminals, but also a significant opportunity to strengthen our response. The government is committed to driving the safe and ethical adoption of artificial intelligence to enhance public service delivery. This is a fast and ever-evolving landscape, and we are committed to continuously exploring opportunities to enhance operational activity through technical innovation and adoption.

166. In this spirit, we will work with partners to accelerate adoption across intelligence, investigative and defensive capabilities, scaling what works, testing new approaches and ensuring we keep pace with evolving criminal methods. This includes:

- Exploring an integrated AI-enabled approach that uses open source intelligence to identify online activity (including money mule recruitment) and chatbot tools to engage offenders to gather intelligence on methods, networks and financial infrastructure. Working with the NCA and other operational partners, this would generate more timely, targeted intelligence to improve detection and disruption.

- Providing funding to develop AI tools targeted at supporting money laundering investigations, improving workflow efficiencies, piloting tools and deploying them into live operational environments.

- Developing a technology horizon-scanning capability, based in the NCA, to identify and raise awareness of criminal exploitation of emerging financial technologies, with the ability to research and test new tools.

## 7


### 7. Outcomes and performance


[pp. 79-80]

Measuring progress is essential to show whether this strategy is working. Our approach builds on what we know works. Economic Crime Plan 2 demonstrated the benefits of strong performance tracking, and we will continue this approach in alignment with the forthcoming Economic Crime Plan 2026–2029. This chapter explains how we will assess progress and who we will work with to deliver our desired outcomes.

##### Assessing progress and impact

167. The performance approach for this Strategy builds on the foundations established under ECP2. Under ECP2, partners developed a Theory of Change and outcomes framework. Our 2025 outcomes progress report set out how we assess impact, including improvements in performance monitoring and data collection, enabling progress against outcomes to be tracked.

168. We are now developing a similar performance system for this Strategy. It will carry forward relevant ECP2 actions and outcomes, while expanding the framework to include new short and long term outcomes and the indicators needed to measure them, covering both ongoing ECP2 activity and new actions in this Strategy – while also aligning with the forthcoming Economic Crime Plan 2026–2029.

169. We will develop a Theory of Change with key public and private sector stakeholders, setting out the Strategy’s inputs, outputs, outcomes (short to medium term) and impacts (long term). We will then develop an outcomes framework to prioritise outcomes and indicators and monitor performance.

170. Both products will be iterative and updated as evidence improves. ECP2 helped address key data gaps, including more detailed criminal justice outcomes data (including principal and non - principal offences), a new data collection framework to evaluate AML/CTF supervision, the Economic Crime Survey 202457 , and a new estimate of proceeds of crime in England and Wales58 .

171. However, longstanding data challenges remain. Further work is needed to assess feasibility of collecting new data and to develop indicators for new actions. We will continue working with private sector partners to strengthen data and metrics. We will integrate existing ECP2 data and indicators into the AMLAR outcomes framework to ensure continuity, including:

- system-wide illicit finance disruptions (NCA)

- 57 Home Office (2025) Economic Crime Survey 2024, available at: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/economic-crime-survey-2024

- 58 Home Office (2026) The proceeds of crime in England and Wales, 2023 to 2024, available at: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-proceeds-of-crime-in-england-and-wales-2023-to-2024

- criminal justice outcomes, including money laundering prosecutions and convictions (MoJ)

- SARs metrics (NCA)

- assets recovered (HO)

[p. 80]

172. We will work with public-private stakeholders to develop indicators for new priorities such as tackling high street money laundering.

173. Evaluation will also support understanding of impact. Under ECP2 this included: a Home Office process evaluation of the 475 AMLAR law enforcement staffing uplift (due later in 2026); and BIST/Companies House evaluation of ECCTA implementation, with findings published by 2028 (ECCTA) and 2027 (ROE). Our AMLAR performance approach will help identify where further targeted evaluation is needed.

174. This approach, and wider oversight of the Strategy, will align with the Government’s economic crime governance framework and be overseen through the structures set out in the forthcoming Economic Crime Plan 2026–2029. This will include oversight through, but not limited to, the Public - Private Steering Group (PPSG), the Economic Crime Delivery Board (ECDB) and the Economic Crime Strategic Board (ECSB).

##### Key deliverables

175. We will publish update reports on progress and impacts from the AMLAR Strategy, including improvements in data and measurement, mid - way and at the end of the Strategy. We will also publish an updated ECP progress report by end March 2027, as committed in the ECP 2025 outcomes progress report.

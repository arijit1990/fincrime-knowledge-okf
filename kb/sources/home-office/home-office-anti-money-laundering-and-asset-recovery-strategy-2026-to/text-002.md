# Anti-money laundering and asset recovery strategy: 2026 to 2029

## 3


### 3. Our approach


[pp. 23-24]

An evolving and effective approach to tackling money laundering and recovering criminal assets is therefore essential. This strategy provides a clear framework for achieving that, building on strong foundations, setting focused objectives, and directing effort where it will deliver the greatest improvements across the system.

##### The UK system

30. The UK’s anti-money laundering and asset recovery system is wide and includes organisations across the public and private sectors, working alongside international partners. It operates as an end-to-end response: preventing criminals from accessing financial and professional services; detecting and reporting suspicious activity; disrupting networks and seizing illicit funds; and using what we learn to strengthen defences over time. Because criminals exploit gaps between organisations and across borders, collaboration, shared priorities and timely information sharing are essential.

31. The regulated sector is the first line of defence, acting as a gatekeeper, applying controls and generating intelligence through suspicious activity reporting. Supervisors reinforce this by setting clear expectations, guarding the regulated perimeter, and intervening where poor practice or failures in controls increases the risk of gatekeepers becoming enablers. Law enforcement then uses intelligence from firms, supervisors and other sources to identify and disrupt laundering and recover assets. This is not a one-way flow: operational insights on emerging threats when fed back to supervisors and the regulated sector, sharpen red flags and improving prevention and reporting. This loop: prevention, intelligence, enforcement and learning, is what makes the system work.

32. The 2025 National Risk Assessment of Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing (NRA) sets out this system in detail. Section 2 describes the UK’s AML and CTF framework, while Annex B details the respective roles of legislation, law enforcement agencies and supervisors across the system.

##### Progress to date

33. Over the past decade, the UK has already made progress against this Strategy’s core objectives, particularly through the delivery of Economic Crime Plans 1 & 2. ECP2 strengthened how progress is measured and tracked, improving accountability and putting a clear focus on demonstrable impact20 . Evidence points to several indications of positive trends in performance:

- a. 3,158 system wide illicit finance disruptions in 2025/26, a 15% increase on the previous year21 .

- b. 4,058 convictions in 2025/26 for money laundering, an 11% increase from 2024/2522 .

- c. £345.3 million of assets recovered in 2025/2026, a 20% increase from 2024/2523 .

- d. £26.1 million paid in compensation to victims in 2025/26, 29% above the median value for the past six years24 .

[p. 24]

- e. £1.1 billion denied from criminals in 2025/26, an increase of 30% from 2024/2525 .

- f. £382.6 million denied from Defence Against Money Laundering (DAML) SARs in 2024/25, a 59% increase on the previous financial year26 .

- g. Over 33,000 entities registered on the Register of Overseas Entities (as of March 2026).

- h. Over 106,000 addresses removed from the Companies House register where personal data was used without consent as of end FY2025–26.

##### Risks

34. While there are positive signs of progress, the 2025 National Risk Assessment of Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing (NRA) sets out that the UK remains exposed to a high level of risk due to being a global financial centre. The NRA concludes that this is driven both by enduring structural vulnerabilities and by criminals’ increasing adoption of new technologies. Enduring risks that continue to be driven by well-established methods include:

- The continued prevalence of cash - based money laundering in the UK , including through cash smuggling, cash-intensive businesses, money mule networks, and the exploitation of legitimate channels (including the Post Office).

35. Meanwhile, emerging risks and the growing adoption of new technologies are reshaping the threat:27

- The rapid growth of fintech, particularly Electronic Money Institutions and Payment Service Providers, is creating greater opportunity for illicit activity to be concealed within high volumes of legitimate transactions.

- The growing use of privacy enhancing cryptoassets typologies has increased money laundering risk, particularly linked to fraud and ransomware.

- The accelerating development of AI is creating new money laundering risks. While current criminal use remains limited, and AI also offers opportunities to strengthen detection and response, the pace of change is expected to give criminals significantly greater capability to evade AML/CTF controls.

##### Current state of play – what have we done to address our risks?

36. Against this backdrop, as the indications of positive trends in system performance suggest, the UK has made good progress in tackling our risks especially through ECP 1 and 2. We have:

###### Cash based money laundering

- Led two phases of enforcement activity targeting business premises such as barber, vape and tobacco shops, and other UK cash intensive businesses, with 2,734 premises visited and raided; 924 individuals arrested; over £10.7m of suspected criminal proceeds seized or restrained; and more than £2.7m worth of illicit commodities destroyed28 .

- Established a cross-government unit to develop a strategic long term policy response to money laundering and associated criminality on UK high streets.

- Funded a Multi-Agency Cash Cell at the border, which brings together government and law enforcement agencies to disrupt the flow of criminal finance into the country. In 2024/2025, this cell seized £7 million in cash and a further £4 million in assets, including gold and luxury watches29 .

###### The use and abuse of financial and professional services firms

- Strengthened AML/CTF controls across multiple sectors, informed by public-private partnership activity.

- Committed to substantial reform of the UK’s AML/CFT supervisory system, strengthening the UK’s defences by streamlining a complex regulatory regime.

- Launched a Professional Enablers Strategy strengthening the whole-system response and providing a clearer framework to address complicit professionals.

###### The misuse of UK corporate structures

- Delivered legislative reforms to move Companies House from a passive registrar to an active gatekeeper, with stronger powers to tackle abuse of UK companies.

- Legislated to close gaps in our regime for the formation of ‘off-the-shelf’ companies.

###### Technology enabled money laundering via Fintech, Cryptoassets and AI

- Updated our asset recovery regime, including bringing in new powers to seize cryptoassets and enhance non-conviction-based forfeiture.

- Extended the cryptoasset regulatory regime and legislated to extend counterparty due diligence checks to cryptoasset service providers.

- Strengthened the operational response to crypto-enabled illicit finance, with UK law enforcement and bodies including the Office of Financial Sanctions Implementation (OFSI) working through multi-agency partnerships to trace, identify and disrupt the use of cryptoassets for sanctions evasion and money laundering.

###### To address cross cutting system vulnerabilities, we have also:

- Strengthened system coordination through publishing clear economic crime priorities for the regulated sector and piloting delivery of these priorities.

- Invested in specialist capability through enhanced, sustainable funding via the Economic Crime (Anti-Money Laundering) Levy. This includes recruiting 475 new financial crime investigators (99% of which are now in post) and the provision of advanced technology to analyse and share threat data in real time.

- Built a new performance framework for asset recovery agencies with a new social research team to identify ‘what works’, understand operational challenges, and support more effective interventions.

###### While we have made progress, structural and cultural challenges remain, including:

- a. Significant public and private investment goes into AML/CTF and asset recovery but too much activity, particularly in the private sector, remains focused on low-value ‘tick-box’ compliance.

[pp. 26-29]

- b. While data-fusion initiatives and public-private information sharing have improved collaboration and data exploitation, elements of the UK’s underlying infrastructure remain outdated, insufficiently digitised and poorly connected.

- c. Even with a strong legislative framework and improved partnerships, pressures in the criminal justice and courts system slow prosecutions and constrain effective asset recovery.

- d. Tools, training and powers have not kept pace with the speed and sophistication of evolving money laundering threats, limiting the ability to target high-harm criminals’ assets.

##### AMLAR Strategy Objectives

37. The progress set out above demonstrates that the UK has strengthened its response to money laundering and recovering criminal assets. However, as the National Risk Assessment and wider system evidence show, serious risks remain. To address these challenges, the next phase of our approach must go further: focusing effort on the highest-harm threats, reducing low-value activity, and ensuring that the system is better equipped to detect, disrupt and recover the proceeds of crime.

38. Reducing the harms caused by money laundering and protecting the UK, its people, and its institutions requires a stronger approach that deters and disrupts criminal activity more effectively and limits criminals’ ability to profit from it. To achieve this aim, the strategy sets three key objectives:

- Make the UK’s defences more effective and efficient , while making it simpler and safer for honest businesses to grow.

- Disrupt money laundering , dismantling high-harm criminal networks at home and abroad, including those operating on our high streets.

- Recover more criminal assets , returning money to law enforcement, victims and the public.

39. Together, these objectives drive a stronger and more integrated system that not only responds to money laundering but deters criminal activity. They shape the outcomes and actions delivered in this strategy, with each action tagged to the objective(s) it supports.

##### Strategy pillars

40. The strategy sets the direction for the UK’s anti - money laundering and asset recovery system over the next phase, defining what the system will prioritise, how partners will work together, and where we will invest to stay ahead of the threat through three mutually reinforcing delivery pillars: Target, Integrate and Empower .

41. Together, these pillars focus effort on the highest harm threats, join up data and activity across the system, and strengthen the skills, tools and powers to act. Progress in each area reinforces the others, creating a cycle of improvement that drives stronger defences, more disruption and increased asset recovery.

###### How the pillars work together

[p. 29]

42. The three pillars are designed to reinforce each other in a continuous cycle. Target frees up capacity by cutting lower-value activity and focuses the system on what matters most, giving Integrate a clearer picture of where to direct data collection, partnerships and joint working. Integrate converts that focus into sharper, more actionable intelligence, giving Empower the information it needs to pursue high-harm actors and recover assets at pace.

43. The results and learning from action then feed back into Target, refining priorities and strengthening the cycle over time. Together, the pillars drive greater disruption, stronger defences and increased asset recovery across the whole system.

##### Measuring impact

44. Each action in the strategy has clear milestones, set out in Annex A, to ensure delivery and accountability. To help ensure the Strategy drives real change, we will track progress against an outcomes framework, supported by robust performance monitoring, and aligned with performance monitoring via the forthcoming Economic Crime Plan 2026–2029. The framework will build on the extensive work undertaken with a range of partners to measure progress of ECP2, ensuring performance appropriately reflects both the continuation of ECP2 activity and the new actions introduced under this Strategy. Further information on our approach to assessing progress and impact can be found in Section 7.

## 4 Target


### 4. Target


[pp. 31-32]

Money laundering in the UK involves multiple methods from traditional cash-based laundering to complex structures and new technologies. Given the scale of the threat, a shared focus on our priorities is needed across the AML/CTF system, with each part focusing resource on where it will deliver the greatest impact. We have already begun to sharpen the system’s focus by publishing system priorities with pilot action plans, updating the 2025 National Risk Assessment, announcing major supervisory consolidation, and reducing low value activity.

Through this strategy we will further strengthen this targeted and agile approach, building a system that systematically deprioritises low-value activity and ensures collective effort translates into greater impact to bolster our defences, improve prevention, increase disruption and recover more assets. To improve our targeting, we will prioritise:

Concentrating cross-system action on our highest risks: To focus on the biggest threats, we will align the system around a single, shared view of priorities and a practical mechanism for joint action. This will create a common direction for intelligence, operational activity and private-sector controls, concentrating effort on the highest-harm threats and delivering clearer disruption and asset recovery outcomes.

Adapting to emerging risks: The threat picture shifts quickly. We will strengthen how the system spots these changes and responds proportionately at pace, using a more structured approach to assessing evidence and closing gaps that criminals exploit. This will help the regulatory perimeter, policy tools and operational capabilities keep pace, enabling earlier intervention, disruption and asset recovery, including through new digital channels.

Delivering effective supervision: We will strengthen supervision to be more consistent, intelligence-led and outcomes-focused. A simpler supervisory landscape and clearer expectations will help supervisors focus on the firms, products and behaviours that drive the greatest harm and scale their activity appropriately, while supporting firms to take proportionate, risk-based decisions.

Reducing low-value activity: Low value processes and reporting divert resources from prevention and disruption. We will reduce this burden through a more effective, technologyenabled approach, freeing capacity across firms, supervisors and the public sector to focus on genuinely suspicious activity and higher-impact interventions. This will also reduce unnecessary friction for legitimate customers and improve the quality of intelligence and action, supporting stronger disruption and better asset recovery.

##### Concentrating cross-system action on our highest risks

Tackling money laundering is a cross-system challenge that depends on effective public private collaboration to deliver a coordinated and impactful response. This chapter sets out how we will align collective effort to the highest harm risks identified in the 2025 National Risk Assessment (NRA) and reflected in the system priorities, especially cash-based money laundering, so that resources are focused on activity that delivers the greatest value and low value activity is reduced.

###### Progress to date

[pp. 32-33]

45. Under Economic Crime Plan 2 (ECP2), public sector prioritisation has begun to strengthen action against key NRA risks. This Strategy will build on that progress by addressing remaining system gaps and further improving our collective impact.

46. In 2025, the NCA and the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA), working with the private sector, the Home Office and HM Treasury, agreed and published a set of priorities to translate the NRA’s core risks into shared, practical areas for public-private action. This has begun to concentrate effort where joint action can have the greatest impact, redirecting away from lower value activity. The NRA and system priorities identify cashbased laundering as a core risk which is increasing in everyday settings, such as the high street and Post Office. These forms of cash-based money laundering require a step change in our response, alongside continued action on other priority risks.

###### Strengthening public-private targeting

47. To operationalise these priorities, action plans have been agreed with a group of financial institutions for three pilot areas: cash-based money laundering, money mules and priority jurisdictions. These action plans are the primary mechanism for driving joint activity and will enable the public and private sectors to target resource effectively against the threats. The pilots have initially been limited to a small number of financial sector participants to test and refine the approach before scaling up. Delivery will be driven through the System Prioritisation Governance Group, which will oversee progress and maintain cross system alignment.

###### High Street Money Laundering

48. Alongside the System Prioritisation action plan targeting cash-based money laundering, we are taking forward further targeted measures to tackle money laundering through high street businesses. The NCA assesses that at least £1 billion is now being laundered per year through businesses operating on UK high streets, which include rogue barber shops,

vape stores, mini-marts and nail bars30 . Just over half of respondents in a recent Home Office survey (54%) thought money laundering was negatively affecting their local high street31 . In addition to enabling a wide range of criminality, these businesses can distort fair competition by undercutting legitimate firms32 and can negatively impact the authorities’ ability to tackle money laundering effectively. Recent law enforcement activity has demonstrated the value of coordinated, intelligence - led action against this threat33 .

###### Case Study: Operation MACHINIZE

[p. 33]

Officers visited 2,734 premises across UK high streets, arrested 924 suspects, seized or restrained over £10.7 million in suspected criminal proceeds, and destroyed illicit goods worth more than £2.7 million

49. Building on this evidence and prioritisation, the Government will intensify coordinated action against high street money laundering through £30 million of funding provided in the November 2025 Budget (£10 million per year over three years). This is expected to result in thousands more premises visited, hundreds more arrests made, and millions of pounds worth of assets seized.34 Alongside this boosted operational response, a new cross - government High Street Organised Crime Unit, housed in the Home Office, has been established to identify what more is needed – from stronger powers to better co-ordination – to stop this criminal activity from happening in the first place. In particular, the Unit will develop interventions designed to:

- Build on the announced extension of closure orders from six months to up to 12 months and consider whether further powers are required for Police and Trading Standards to close dodgy shops.

- Improve operational partners’ access to data.

- Make it harder for new criminal enterprises to set up on the high street, including through exploring options on licences or permits.

- Improve public and business awareness of high street crime.

- Support landlords to act against dodgy tenants.

###### Post Office

50. The Post Office network plays an important role in allowing the millions of people who depend on cash to continue withdrawing and depositing it, especially due to the closure and reduction of bank branches in both urban and rural areas. £2–3 billion is deposited every month. However, as the 2025 NRA sets out, this service can also be exploited by criminals. In recent years, there has been increasing use by Organised Criminal Groups (OCGs) of the Post Office’s everyday banking facilities to deposit large volumes of criminal cash across the UK, with cash deposits continuing to rise despite the broader societal shift away from cash.

51. Collaboration between banks, law enforcement and the FCA has already led to some progress, including limits on Post Office cash deposits and strengthened reporting and intelligence sharing. Building on this, we will:

- Further enhance data and intelligence sharing between Post Office, the banking sector and law enforcement to strengthen understanding of threat and enable more targeted action.

- Strengthen the Post Office’s systems and controls, including through exploration of the feasibility of targeted customer identification requirements, introduction of CCTV and potential further limits on cards or deposits to better detect and reduce the threat. In parallel, we will continue to explore how systems and controls within the banking sector can be strengthened to reduce the flow of illicit cash into the financial system via the Post Office.

###### Illicit Cash in the Economy

[p. 35]

52. Cash in circulation continues to grow35 , despite a sustained decline in its use for everyday transactions36 . While this may partly reflect benign factors – such as slower circulation of cash in lower-activity periods or increased precautionary holdings by individuals – it also raises the risk that illicit cash is being stored and moved outside the formal economy. We will therefore strengthen the evidence base to better understand these drivers and distinguish legitimate from illicit demand. In parallel, we will identify and test targeted operational and policy interventions to reduce the role of criminal cash in the system.

##### Adapting to emerging risks

The UK’s risk-based approach depends on a shared, current understanding of risk, grounded in the 2025 NRA and the ability to respond at pace. This chapter sets out how we will strengthen the system’s agility to identify and act on emerging risks early including where new technologies are involved, focusing resources on the highest-harm threats, closing gaps before they are exploited at scale, and improving disruption and asset recovery .

###### Progress to date

53. We have strengthened our ability to identify and respond to emerging risks through reforms to our cryptoasset regime, introducing the Travel Rule37 , which requires firms to collect and share information on the parties behind cryptoasset transfers. We are also better able to spot new risks early because our approach is built on close partnership with the private sector, leveraging the unique vantage point of regulated firms through public - private governance and operational collaboration. The UK Anti-Corruption Strategy 2025 reinforced this approach, committing the Government to consult on the scope of the Money Laundering Regulations (MLRs) to respond to emerging risks38 .

Responding to the National Risk Assessment

55. The 2025 NRA highlights risks from new technologies, evolving typologies, and vulnerabilities in sectors that sit partly or wholly outside the Money Laundering Regulations. Without a structured way to identify, test and respond to these issues, there is a risk of undermining the effectiveness of the UK’s risk-based approach.

56. Where the evidence supports it, we will act to close gaps in the regulatory perimeter in a targeted way. These gaps currently include: property developers, offshore virtual asset service providers, antiques and antiquities dealers, football clubs and agents, donationbased crowdfunding, and changes to the current scope of regulation for letting agents and higher-risk high-value goods dealers. In line with the Government commitment in the Regulation Action Plan39 to ensure the Money Laundering Regulations are both effective and proportionate, sectors will only be added to the Regulations where necessary and effective to address a real risk of money laundering or terrorist financing. This consultation will consider whether the Regulations are the correct tool to address the risks identified in the NRA within these sectors, and if not, which alternative approaches may be better suited.

###### Understanding the future of financial payments

57. As payment systems evolve and new financial tools emerge, they bring with them vulnerabilities that criminals are quick to identify and act upon. By anticipating how innovation may reshape the threat landscape, we can build a system that is ready to respond with confidence and at pace, rather than one that is perpetually catching up.

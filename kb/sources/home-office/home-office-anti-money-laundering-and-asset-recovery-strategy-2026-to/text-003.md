###### Stablecoins

59. The government has committed to making the UK a leading global destination for digital assets, recognising their role in maintaining the UK’s position as a world - leading financial services hub. As stablecoins grow, offering benefits such as faster cross - border transfers and price stability, they also present increasing risks of criminal exploitation, with industry estimates suggesting they account for 84% of illicit cryptoasset transaction volume globally40 . While already in scope of the Money Laundering Regulations, the existing framework needs updating to better manage these risks while supporting innovation.

60. To put in place a clear regulatory foundation, the government legislated in February through the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 (Cryptoassets) Order 2026, creating a new regulated activity for stablecoin issuance. To address enforcement gaps, measures contained in the Financial Services and Markets Bill will, if passed, enable cryptoasset recovery powers to be updated more easily. If the measures are passed, the government intends to amend powers to tackle cryptoassets held on overseas exchanges outside the current ‘UK nexus’, enabling more effective asset recovery by law enforcement.

61. To further improve recovery outcomes in practice, we will engage with Stablecoin issuers to agree UK processes for the recovery of cryptoassets to develop and share best practices and recovery, identify intervention mapping and share operational information sharing.

##### Delivering effective supervision

The UK’s AML/CTF regime relies on supervisors to support over 90,000 businesses to detect, prevent and report money laundering and terrorism financing. Effective supervision is core to unlocking a more targeted approach. Supervisors must empower firms to use new technologies and work collaboratively on the biggest threats, while taking swift, robust action against businesses which are negligent or complicit in enabling money laundering or terrorist financing.

###### Progress to date

64. The Government is taking decisive action to strengthen the AML/CTF supervisory system. In 2025, HM Treasury announced41 significant structural consolidation of the system, with the FCA to take on supervision of legal, accountancy, and trust and company services providers. This major reform will reduce the number of AML/CTF supervisors from 25 to 3, simplifying the supervisory landscape and enabling stronger and more consistent supervision across the regulated sector.

65. Economic Crime Plan 2 has delivered a number of other actions aimed at improving the effectiveness of AML/CTF supervision. For instance, HM Treasury has implemented an enhanced oversight strategy, expanding the metrics used to measure the performance of supervisors (reflected in the most recent annual supervision report42 ). The Office for Professional Body Anti-Money Laundering Supervision (OPBAS) issued its first formal enforcement action43 against a Professional Body Supervisor in 2025.

###### Empowering Supervisors

[pp. 39-40]

69. Structural reform is a major step forward for the UK’s supervisory regime. To realise its benefits, the three remaining supervisors – the FCA, HMRC and the Gambling Commission – must have the right powers to operate effectively in a modern AML/CTF system. As criminals adopt increasingly sophisticated methods, supervisors and firms must be able to respond quickly. To deliver this response, HM Treasury will work with the supervisors to ensure they are supported and able to encourage collaboration and information sharing, drive the adoption of innovative technologies, and discourage blanket or excessive controls that disrupt legitimate customers, through the commitments set out below.

70. The FCA will:

- Publish examples of good and poor practice on the use of AI in 2026–27 to support firms’ understanding of appropriate governance and adoption of AI tools.

- Undertake targeted engagement through roundtables with firms in the payments industry in 2026–27 to promote the use of the Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act (ECCTA) 2023 information sharing gateway.

- Update its Financial Crime Guide to provide examples of good practice on information sharing and the use of AI tools in 2026–27.

71. HMRC will:

- Host and utilise attendance at forums and conferences to encourage and share best practise in relation to collaboration and information sharing, including through interactive sessions.

- Undertake outreach to promote good practice regarding the use of tech-based and AI compliance tools within supervised business.

- Through guidance and outreach, promote and continue to monitor the implementation of a risk-based approach to anti-money laundering checks by supervised businesses, aiming to ensure they do not adopt disproportionate, blanket or excessive approaches.

72. The Gambling Commission (GC) will:

- Collaborate with the gambling industry through targeted industry events and forums to encourage collaboration and information sharing to tackle money laundering across 2026–27.

- Update its casino guidance in 2026–27 to encourage information sharing by reflecting the new provisions contained within ECCTA and share industry learnings from casework on a regular basis.

- Develop policies and processes on AI and innovative technologies, monitor performance and provide assurance.

- Continue to monitor the implementation of a risk-based approach to anti-money laundering checks by casinos to ensure excessive, blanket approaches are not taken.

73. HM Treasury will continue to work with regulated sectors and supervisors to assess where legislative change is needed and consult on any amendments to the Money Laundering Regulations.

###### Supervisory Enforcement Powers

74. Where regulated sector risks are most acute, some supervisors consider existing enforcement powers inadequate to take robust action against high-risk sectors such as Money Service Businesses (MSBs), and firms with persistent non-compliance or complicity in organised crime. To address this, HM Treasury will consult on measures to strengthen supervisory enforcement powers under the MLRs, including:

- Expanding supervisors’ powers to make unannounced visits to regulated firms and search premises for cash.

- Strengthening supervisors’ ability to hold directors accountable for MLR breaches.

- Enabling the Gambling Commission to enforce MLR breaches directly, without relying on Gambling Act 2005 powers.

- Reviewing which bodies can prosecute MLR breaches, including whether to add the Insolvency Service.

###### Supervisory Risk Assessments

75. Effective supervisory risk assessments require consistent methodology, a clear purpose and scope, and access to targeted intelligence to give firms genuinely useful, sectorspecific insights that supplement the NRA and support their role as gatekeepers. The FCA, HMRC and the Gambling Commission have each developed their own approaches to this, and there is scope to build on these further whilst improving the sources of intelligence that support findings. HM Treasury will therefore work with:

- Supervisors to agree common principles for supervisory risk assessments, covering purpose and scope, methodology, evidence base, publication, and update frequency.

- Law enforcement to consider options to enhance the ability of SARs to provide insight and intelligence on specific regulated activities and the threats firms within them face.

###### Companies House filing restrictions

78. Further improvements to the UK’s AML/CTF system will be implemented by Companies House when restrictions on filing on behalf of a company come into force. Those filing information with Companies House will need to be an identity-verified employee or officer of the firm, an Authorised Corporate Service Provider (ACSP) or an officer or employee of an ACSP. This will prevent unsupervised agents from submitting filings, strengthen the integrity of company records, and improve data sharing between AML/CTF supervisors and Companies House, supporting risk-based supervision and the UK’s defences against economic crime.

##### Reducing low-value activity

Anti-money laundering requirements can place significant compliance obligations on a wide range of businesses and it is critical that the resources firms invest is directed at tackling the highest threats. Through System Prioritisation, we will identify low value activity – for example regulatory requirements which are not genuinely effective in preventing money laundering – that can be deprioritised to enable the system to focus resource against the highest harm threats. We will also support the use of new technologies such as AI and digital ID to make customer due diligence and other aspects of AML/CTF compliance quicker and easier.

###### Progress to date

[pp. 42-43]

79. Under Economic Crime Plan 2, the Government took steps to reduce low-value AML/CTF activity, including amending the Money Laundering Regulations so regulated sectors are empowered to take a risk-based approach when applying enhanced due diligence to high-risk jurisdictions. This change, which came into force in July 2026, is estimated to

save the regulated sector £178.1 million per annum in compliance costs that can be better targeted to higher value activity45 .

80. The Government has also kept the SARs regime under review and introduced reforms to reduce low-value reports and burdens on firms, including through changes in the Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act (ECCTA) 2023. Additionally, in 2025, the section 339A POCA threshold was raised from £1,000 to £3,000, reducing low-value DAML SARs and easing pressure on firms and the UKFIU. It is estimated this change will eliminate 25,000 DAML requests each year, freeing up around £4 million per year of costs to the regulated sector that can be focused on better targeted activity46 .

###### Further reforms to the Suspicious Activity Reporting regime, including suspicion threshold review

81. Despite the legislative changes to the SARs regime, volumes remain high – 866,616 SARs were submitted to the UKFIU in 2024–2547 . We will explore further SAR reforms to reduce low-value activity and unnecessary burdens on businesses and individuals. The recently closed call for evidence on economic crime information sharing will refresh the evidence on how SARs are accessed and used, identify duplication with other regimes, and inform how the regime can be better targeted and resourced. Specifically, we will examine the evidence base for raising the suspicion threshold (for example to ‘reasonable grounds to suspect’), working with high volume reporters to assess impacts of such a change on intelligence collection, system capacity and customer outcomes.

###### Money Laundering Regulations

[pp. 43-44]

82. The Government will further target the UK’s Money Laundering Regulations on genuinely high-risk activity, so firms can focus resources on detecting, preventing and reporting meaningful suspicion rather than low - value compliance. We will maintain a robust, Financial Acton Task Force (FATF) compliant48 regime and support growth by reducing unnecessary friction in key sectors such as financial and legal services. These should also reduce delays for legitimate customers, including small businesses and voluntary organisations. We will consult on reforms to improve proportionality.

###### Discrepancy Reporting

83. The private sector plays a vital role in improving the integrity of the Companies House register by comparing Companies House data with its own due diligence records and reporting discrepancies. The Government made changes to the regime in 2022 to better target the Discrepancy Reporting Regime but it remains burdensome for firms, whilst the persistently high volume of low-value reports creates barriers to effective use of the intelligence generated. In the context of introduction of the identity verification regime (IDV), we will review how to make the regime more effective while reducing burdens on business.

###### Digital ID

84. The Government recognises both the potential benefits and risks of new technologies and how they interact with customer due diligence checks. In February 2026, the Government published guidance on digital identity use under the MLRs, interaction with the UK digital verification services trust framework, and governance under the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025. This provides certainty over which digital identity services providers can be relied upon as a reliable and independent source of information for customer due diligence purposes.

85. Sector guidance bodies are developing more technical guidance. HM Treasury and the Department for Business, Innovation, Science and Trade (BIST) will consider if further steps are needed to support digital ID for AML/CTF checks and respond to emerging risks. The FCA, HMRC and the Gambling Commission will also encourage responsible use of AI

to support AML/CTF compliance, including detecting suspicious transaction patterns, including (where applicable) through the FCA’s Mills Review49 published in July 2026.

###### International Standards

86. The Government will work at both the domestic and international level to support a more effective response to economic crime and reduce low-value activity by supervisors and regulated firms. While activity by both supervisors and firms is influenced by a range of factors, the standards that underpin the UK regime are set by FATF.

87. We will examine whether international standards constrain a more risk - based approach, and where changes could enable more targeted national regulation and more effective compliance by multinational firms. We will use the UK’s leadership roles, including the FATF presidency (2026–28) and G20 presidency (2027), to pursue this.

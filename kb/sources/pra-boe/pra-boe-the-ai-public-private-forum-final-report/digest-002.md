## Benefits for consumers, firms, and the financial system

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 30: for customers AI can enable more personalised financial products and services, including savings and investment advice, and a more seamless customer journey using Natural Language Processing (NLP) and voice, document, image and facial recognition. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI can enable more personalised financial products and services, including savings and investment advice"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 31: for firms AI can improve predictive power and profitability and operational efficiency (reducing processing time and costs), and a range of front and back office operations, from monitoring risks to regulatory reporting, can be automated, freeing subject matter experts for complex tasks. AI combined with real-time data may lead to more effective decisions. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "From monitoring risks to regulatory reporting, a range of front and back office operations can be automated."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 32: benefits can extend to the financial system and economy; AI can help analyse the scale and complexity of the financial system, including where complexity exceeds human intellect, and can help tackle synthetic identity fraud (identities created from a jigsaw of real data) by identifying cases hard for human analysts to identify. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI can also help tackle synthetic identity fraud, where synthetic identities are created from a jigsaw of real data"</sub>

## Barriers to adoption, challenges, and risks

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 33: there can be significant barriers to choosing and using AI, especially for smaller organisations. Some of the biggest involve data (access and data privacy); at the model level, explaining and documenting complex models' workings and outputs and ensuring governance of data and models; and regulatory uncertainty is another significant barrier. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "regulatory uncertainty is another significant barrier"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Paragraph 34: a further barrier is firms' ability to identify appropriate AI use-cases, especially when starting out; it may be more useful to look at small-scale use-cases where the firm already has enough data and business buy-in, which may be considered good practice for firms. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It may be more useful to look at small-scale use-cases where the firm already has enough data"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 35: AI use can pose risks to consumers, firms, the financial system and the wider economy; AI can amplify existing risks and introduce new challenges, so existing controls and frameworks may need to evolve. For example, bias embedded in historical data may cause algorithms to give more importance to certain features, which then make incorrect predictions. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "bias embedded in historical data may cause algorithms to give more importance to certain features"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 36: ever larger data sets that are harder to validate at a granular level could lead to unexpected outcomes such as biased and unfair decisions, and using multiple internal data sets previously held in silos, and third-party datasets, may require updated data management. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "ever larger data sets that are harder to validate at a granular level"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 36: some AI models are dynamic, continuously learning from live data so outputs can change; this is a particular issue when input data change (data drift) or the statistical properties of the data change (concept drift), which can affect all model types. Model performance can also deteriorate due to incorrect training data, so more continuous monitoring and validation (and potentially documentation) may be required than static validation and testing. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "when the underlying data inputs change (data drift) or statistical properties of the data change (concept drift)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 37: tailored products and services involve trade-offs, such as consumers being priced out or unable to get credit or insurance if deemed higher risk. Other consumer risks include competition and fiduciary concerns, such as unfavourable or unfair penalties or product conditions (for example level of collateral needed), and combining AI with personal data could risk breaching consumers' personal data rights. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "highly tailored products and services can lead to some consumers being priced out"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 38: for firms, inappropriate AI use could cause financial loss (for example from a poor credit algorithm), reputational risk, regulatory risk (fines or sanctions for breaching responsibilities to customers), operational risk (being unable to recover or debug) and loss of intellectual property. Certain adaptive models need large amounts of data and are open to 'adversarial attacks', exposing firms to greater cyber risk. AI can introduce less well understood non-financial risks, such as data privacy and protection, relevant because protected characteristics may be needed to measure the fairness of AI predictions. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "are open to ‘adversarial attacks’"</sub>

## Domestic and international context

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 39: AI will likely become integral to UK economic segments and society, as recognised in the UK National AI Strategy published by the OAI in September 2021, a ten-year plan to make the UK an 'AI superpower' building on initiatives such as the National Data Strategy. The CDEI has published research such as on bias in algorithmic decision-making, and the FMSB has published papers on themes and challenges in algorithmic trading. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "UK National AI Strategy, which was published by the OAI in September 2021"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 40: some UK regulators have addressed AI in their remits; the ICO issued guidance on explainability to help firms use AI in compliance with data protection laws. It outlines different levels of explainability depending on domain, use-case and impact on the individual, aiming to give reassurance about governance, provenance and use of personal data and the input-output link without delving into the black box as far as possible. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the ICO has issued guidance on explainability that can help firms use AI in a way that complies with data protection laws"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 41: many AI governance principles exist globally and there is broad consensus on their value, but firms find it difficult to apply them to specific use-cases and translate them into internal practice. Further regulatory clarification on applying high-level principles to use-cases would be welcomed, and financial services guidance could also include standards from other sectors (such as ISO/IEC AI standards or the IEEE SA guide for federated learning). (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Further regulatory clarification on how best to apply high-level principles to specific use-cases would be welcomed by firms"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 42: the European Commission proposal for draft AI regulation was published in April 2021; its documentation requirements and, in contrast to the wide definition of AI, its narrow definition of high-risk use-cases may be useful for other jurisdictions to consider. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the narrow definition of high-risk use-cases may be useful for other jurisdictions to consider"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: Paragraph 43: there may be a need to avoid regulatory fragmentation where possible, domestically, internationally and between sectors; harmonising regulation would help ensure accountability and manage risks without stifling innovation, especially as contradictions and vagueness make it harder to see differences between foreign and UK regulations and impede use of AI models developed outside the UK. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there may be a need to avoid regulatory fragmentation where possible"</sub>

## Data

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraphs 46-47: the second AIPPF meeting and workshops explored the role of data in AI and practical data challenges hindering adoption. A defining feature of AI is its ability to process large volumes of data and detect and exploit patterns; many data considerations are not AI-specific but are of paramount importance, reflected in data quality, strategy, management and governance. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "One of the defining features of AI is its ability to process large volumes of data and to detect and exploit patterns"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 48: AI practitioners have shifted from a model-centric to a data-centric approach. Preparing and constructing datasets is often time-consuming and expensive; data preparation tasks such as cleansing, removing duplication and fixing errors can take 45% of data scientists' time on average. Consistency in annotating datasets can have a greater impact on predictive performance than model tuning. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "can take 45% of data scientists’ time on average"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 49: AI applications often use relatively simple clustering, time-series prediction or regression models whose success is usually underpinned by high-quality data rather than complex algorithms; so high-quality data and fair and equal access to it may be a greater enabler of competition and innovation than the algorithms and models themselves. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI applications often use relatively simple clustering, time- series prediction or regression models"</sub>

## Key findings – Data

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Key finding: AI begins with data. AI can process large volumes of data and exploit hidden patterns; while modelling choices and model risk management are clear priorities, many AI benefits and risks can be traced back to the data rather than models, algorithms and systems. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "many of the benefits and risks from AI can be traced back to the data rather than the AI models, algorithms, and systems"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Key finding: AI often processes large volumes of unstructured or 'alternative' data (such as satellite images, biometrics, telematics). Alternative data use is one of the main ways AI could exacerbate data quality issues, partly because these data are often sourced from third-party providers, adding challenges of quality, provenance and sometimes legality. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "one of the main ways that AI could exacerbate data quality issues"</sub>

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 50: data quality flows across the AI lifecycle, from profiling data and ensuring metrics are met, through data preparation, labelling and quality improvement tasks such as cleansing and removing duplications, to feature engineering (designing or extracting characteristics or attributes from raw data used as model inputs). (p. 16)[^original]

## Data quality

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 52: a key factor behind many data quality challenges is the attempt to retrofit existing processes, controls and systems to AI. Pre-existing ways of managing and governing data quality may not always suit AI models, because existing control frameworks may not scale to the volume and variety of features in the data or to its range of applications. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "attempt to retrofit existing process, controls and systems to AI"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Paragraph 52: as AI systems grow in scale, there is a need for systematic data quality processes that are transparent, reproducible, auditable and able to be integrated with other processes. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is a need for systematic data quality processes"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 53: adapting existing data quality metrics and standards to AI may be hampered by a lack of industry-wide consensus on data standards and good practice, and rapid change might limit the value of standards-setting organisations if standards become dated. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "lack of industry‑wide consensus on data standards in general"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Paragraph 54: one approach could be for firms to develop their own internal standards and systems to assess data quality, and firms could also develop a template for data 'lineage' giving a clear history of where and how data was produced and how it travels through the organisation, which may help ensure data are catalogued and assist data quality impact analysis. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Firms could also develop a template for data ‘lineage’"</sub>

## Data strategy and economics

- **recommended / recommendation**: Paragraph 56: the value a firm puts on data can influence its business models, data-business strategy alignment and AI use. Firms should consider the pricing of data and expected payoffs from its use in any AI project; data source matters for consistency and transparency and in cost/benefit analysis. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Firms should consider the pricing of data and expected payoffs from its use in any AI project."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 57: data can provide competitive advantage; start-ups may be more agile with new technology but incumbents have greater volume and variety of data. Assessing the costs and expected benefits of individual data assets is not always straightforward, and is harder with third-party data, especially if data marketplaces are monopolistic or restrictive or access is limited, which could create challenges for data pricing policies. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "incumbents have a greater volume and variety of data on their side"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Paragraph 58: understanding, measuring and tracking data flows within, into and out of an organisation (including real-time and streamed data and third-party datasets) is an important part of data strategy; governing ownership, liability and control of data in complex distributed systems is challenging, and data audits and assessments of data usage and alignment with business objectives could form part of the solution. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Data audits and assessments of data usage and alignment with business objectives could form part of the solution."</sub>

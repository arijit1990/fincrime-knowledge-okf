## Foreword

- **descriptive / context**: The foreword states that AI is increasingly important for UK financial services, firms already use it widely, and Covid-19 accelerated adoption. AI's improved classification and predictive accuracy and ability to automate tasks can benefit households, firms and the economy, for example lower-cost, more-tailored products for consumers. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Covid-19 pandemic has accelerated the pace of adoption"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The foreword notes that the speed, scale and complexity of AI systems can amplify existing risks and pose new challenges: model complexity can give greater predictive accuracy but make outputs harder to explain, and make it harder to understand how multiple models interact as they become more interconnected within financial markets. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The speed, scale, and complexity of AI systems can amplify existing risks and pose new challenges."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Using a loan application as an example, AI may be used in marketing the loan, processing the application, guiding or making the decision, automating credit stewardship and modelling regulatory capital. Challenges include potential data bias that may exclude certain demographics, lack of transparency and explainability making it hard to see how inputs lead to outputs, and unclear accountability and responsibility for decisions based on outputs. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "potential bias in the data that may exclude certain demographics"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: As AI becomes more widely used in savings and investment management, for example institutional fund products, its market impact may grow. This could lead to 'herd' behaviour if trading systems use similar data and models, or through concentrations and disruptions in networks used to transfer data and models, posing risks to consumers, firms and the financial system. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This could lead to ‘herd’ behaviour if trading systems use similar data and models"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Bank of England and FCA established the AI Public-Private Forum (AIPPF) in October 2020 to further dialogue between the public sector, private sector and academia on AI, sharing information, deepening understanding and exploring how to support safe AI adoption in financial services. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "established the AI Public-Private Forum (AIPPF) in October 2020"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The AIPPF's topics fell into three areas: Data, Model risk and Governance. Earlier work showed AI risk drivers occur at each level within interconnected AI systems: risks from data used to train and run models, then risks from the models themselves, which feed into risks to the firm and governance needs, and finally risks to the financial system as a whole. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The issues and topics covered by the AIPPF fell into three areas: Data, Model risk, and Governance."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report summarises key issues discussed by AIPPF members, in particular challenges and risks of AI in financial services with examples of how to address and mitigate them, and highlights considerations for the regulatory framework. Because the area is evolving with many unanswered questions, what 'good' looks like can differ by situation. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "what “good” looks like can differ depending on the situation"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The AIPPF made clear that the private sector wants regulators to support safe AI adoption, building on what is already in place; different firms have different needs and any regulatory interventions should be proportionate. A key question is whether existing regulation such as the Senior Managers and Certification Regime can be applied to AI through extensions or whether a new approach is needed. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "any regulatory interventions should be proportionate"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Bank and FCA planned to publish a Discussion Paper on AI later in the year, building on the AIPPF work and broadening engagement. It would aim to clarify how the current regulatory framework applies to AI, ask how policy can best support safe adoption and let stakeholders share views; responses would help identify what is relevant to their remits and help formulate any potential policy. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "we will publish a Discussion Paper on AI later this year"</sub>

## Establishing the AI Public-Private Forum

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 2: the Bank, in its July 2019 response to the Future of Finance Review, announced that the Bank and FCA would establish the AIPPF. It sought to (2.1) share information and understand practical challenges, barriers to deployment and potential risks of using AI; (2.2) gather views on where principles, guidance or good practice examples could support safe adoption; and (2.3) consider whether ongoing industry input would be useful and in what form. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Gather views on potential areas where principles, guidance or good practice examples could be useful"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraphs 3-4: the Bank and FCA published the AIPPF Terms of Reference in January 2020 with a call for Expressions of Interest, received more than 100 applications, and selected 21 members after review and input from an independent senior adviser to the PRA. Members came from a range of sectors and acted in a personal capacity to further the interests of the market as a whole, not their employers. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Bank and FCA received more than 100 applications from a wide range of AI experts."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 1: AI is increasingly used in UK financial services, with Covid-19 having sped up adoption. It can bring a range of benefits to consumers, firms and the wider financial system, but there are also barriers to adoption and challenges, and AI use in financial services may create new risks or amplify existing ones. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly used in UK financial services and Covid-19 has accelerated the pace of adoption."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraphs 5-6: five observer organisations joined: the Centre for Data Ethics and Innovation (CDEI), the Fixed Income, Currencies and Commodities Markets Standards Board (FMSB), HM Treasury, the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) and the Office for Artificial Intelligence (OAI). The AIPPF was co-chaired by Dave Ramsden (Bank) and, at the FCA, initially Sheldon Mills then Jessica Rusu, with a Bank and FCA secretariat. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "five observer organisations were invited to join the AIPPF"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 7: the AIPPF launched on 12 October 2020 and ran for one year with four quarterly meetings and workshops structured around Data, Model risk and Governance, because AI risk drivers and firm challenges occur at each level of interconnected AI systems (Figure 1): issues at the data level can impact models, which then raise broader governance challenges. The report uses the same structure. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "issues at the data level can impact the models, which then raise broader governance challenges"</sub>

## Purpose of this report

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 8: the AIPPF committed in its Terms of Reference to publish a public document on its conclusion; this report meets that commitment and aims to advance understanding of AI use in UK financial services and promote debate on how best to support safe adoption. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the AIPPF committed to deliver a public document on its conclusion"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 9: the report is not intended as a primer or comprehensive overview of AI in financial services; it presents the culmination of a year-long dialogue between AIPPF members, observers and secretariat. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this report is not intended to be a primer or comprehensive overview"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 10: the report explores barriers, challenges and risks of AI use in financial services and ways to address and mitigate them; as the area is rapidly evolving there are no clear answers yet, so the report presents various approaches. AI use also concerns regulators beyond the Bank and FCA, for example the Digital Regulation Cooperation Forum, which brings UK regulators together on online regulatory matters such as AI. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Given that this is a rapidly evolving area, there are no clear answers yet."</sub>

## Executive summary

- **descriptive / context**: Executive summary paragraphs 1-3: AI is a rapidly evolving, powerful tool used in increasing ways by financial firms; it can benefit consumers, businesses and the economy but can amplify risks and create new challenges. AI models are increasingly sophisticated and their speed, scale, complexity and capacity for autonomous decision-making have sparked debate. AIPPF discussions began with data (the foundation), moved to models (where data are put to work) and ended with governance (the guardrails). (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Their speed, scale, and complexity, as well as their capacity for autonomous decision-making"</sub>

## Data

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Executive summary paragraph 4: AI begins with data. Recent growth in AI use is partly driven by vast growth in data available to train models. While modelling choices and model risk management are clear priorities, many benefits and risks trace back to the data rather than the AI systems or algorithms themselves. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "many of the benefits and risks can be traced back to the data"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Executive summary paragraph 5: data quality is as important as ever. At scale there is a premium on understanding data attributes, including provenance, completeness and how representative the data are; because data use can change, documentation, versioning and ongoing monitoring are increasingly important. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the importance of documentation, versioning, and ongoing monitoring is increasing"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Executive summary paragraph 6: AI can process large volumes of unstructured or 'alternative' data (for example satellite images, biometrics, telematics) that can generate unique insights, but this is also a main way AI could increase data quality issues, partly because such data are often sourced from third-party providers, adding challenges of quality, provenance and sometimes legality. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this use of alternative data is also one of the main ways that AI could increase data quality issues"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Executive summary paragraph 7: data are reshaping organisations; the changing role of data in the AI lifecycle raises questions about appropriate governance structures, and as firms develop data strategies for AI there is an increasing call for AI-specific data standards. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "increasing call for the development and use of AI-specific data standards"</sub>

## Model risk

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Executive summary paragraph 8: most risks of AI models in financial services are not new and can arise from non-AI models; what is new is the scale at which AI is used, the speed at which systems operate and the complexity of the underlying models, which can create new challenges and amplify existing ones. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Most of the risks related to the use of AI models in financial services are not new"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Executive summary paragraph 9: complexity is the key challenge for managing AI model risk, covering input complexity (many input layers and dimensions), complex relationships between variables, intricacies of models themselves (for example deep learning) and output types (actions, algorithms, unstructured such as images or text, and/or quantitative). It becomes more complicated when several AI models operate within a network. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Complexity is the key challenge for managing the risks arising from AI models."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Executive summary paragraph 10: being able to explain model outputs is vital; approaches to managing explainability should not focus only on model features and parameters but also on consumer engagement and clear communications. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Being able to explain model outputs is vital."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Executive summary paragraph 11: identifying and managing change in AI models, and monitoring and reporting their performance, are key parts of ensuring models behave as expected, including monitoring for changes in a model's functional form as well as its outputs and any need for retraining. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "monitoring for changes in a model’s functional form as well as its outputs and potential need for retraining"</sub>

## Governance

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Executive summary paragraph 12: a key characteristic of AI systems is their capacity for autonomous decision-making, which has profound implications for governing the technology and its outcomes, including ensuring effective accountability and responsibility. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A key characteristic of AI systems is their capacity for autonomous decision-making."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Executive summary paragraph 13: existing governance frameworks and structures are a good starting point for AI, partly because AI models interact with other risk and governance processes; the most relevant in financial services are data governance and model risk management (MRM) frameworks, and operational risk management. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Existing governance frameworks and structures provide a good starting point for AI models and systems"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Executive summary paragraph 14: AI governance should reflect the risk and materiality of the use-case (the specific application the AI system is designed for), even when existing frameworks are applied. Where possible firms should leverage and adapt existing governance structures, including data and MRM frameworks, to manage AI. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Governance of AI systems should reflect the risk and materiality of the use-case"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Executive summary paragraph 15: a centralised body within firms should set AI governance standards. Overall AI responsibility could be held by one or more senior managers, with business areas accountable for outputs, compliance and execution against the standards. Transparency and communication are key elements of AI governance. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A centralised body within firms should set the AI governance standards."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Executive summary paragraph 16: AI governance in firms is more effective if it includes diversity of skills and perspective and covers the full range of functions and business units; a cross-functional approach can help manage the complexity of AI systems and their data challenges. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Governance of AI in firms is more effective if it includes diversity of skills and perspective"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Executive summary paragraph 17: firms should ensure an appropriate level of understanding and awareness of AI's benefits and risks throughout the organisation, which can include more formal governance arrangements and a clear role for senior leadership. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Firms should ensure that there is an appropriate level of understanding and awareness of AI’s benefits and risks"</sub>

## Next steps

- **descriptive / context**: Executive summary paragraph 18: the discussion on safe AI adoption has only just begun; public-private dialogue through the AIPPF was informative and productive, with considerable appetite for it to continue in some form. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The discussion on the safe adoption of AI has only just begun."</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: Executive summary paragraph 19: an industry body for practitioners could build trust in AI; professionalising data science, including voluntary codes of conduct and an auditing regime, would help foster wider acceptance of and trust in AI systems. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Professionalising data science, including voluntary codes of conduct and establishing an auditing regime"</sub>

## Definition and characteristics of artificial intelligence

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 22: AI is difficult to define, but giving a sense of what the term means can demystify AI and separate hype from reality, helping (i) customers understand how and when AI affects them, (ii) firms use the technology responsibly and assess trade-offs, (iii) practical implementation of high-level principles, and (iv) more standardised approaches across industry, supporting wider and safer adoption. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "help customers understand how and when they are being affected by the use of AI"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Paragraph 23: in the broadest sense AI is the use of advanced statistical techniques with large computational and data needs; the term is often used for machine learning (ML), a sub-set of AI with three main categories: supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning. The draft European Commission AI regulation definition is broad and includes statistical models and techniques not always considered AI. The Bank and FCA previously defined ML as the theory and development of computer systems able to perform tasks requiring human intelligence. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI is the use of advanced statistical techniques with large computational and data needs"</sub>

## Box 1: Definition of AI used in this report

- **descriptive / definition**: Box 1: the report uses the ISO definition of AI, an interdisciplinary field usually regarded as a branch of computer science dealing with models and systems for performing functions generally associated with human intelligence, such as reasoning and learning. In the report the term AI also refers to ML. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In this report the term AI also refers to ML."</sub>

## Next steps

- **permissive / recommendation**: Executive summary paragraph 20: regulators can support AI innovation and adoption, and could begin by giving clarity on how existing regulation and policies apply to AI. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Regulators could start by providing clarity on how existing regulation and policies apply to AI."</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Executive summary paragraph 20: to support innovation, regulatory guidance on AI should concentrate on the outcomes expected and should not be overly prescriptive. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "guidance should focus on the outcomes expected and not be overly prescriptive"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Executive summary paragraph 20: such guidance could include illustrative case studies and could pick out high-risk use-cases on which to concentrate. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It could provide illustrative case studies and identify high-risk use-cases to focus on."</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Executive summary paragraph 21: the report says regulatory alignment will catalyse progress. Coordination between the Bank and FCA has been very helpful, and continued coordination with domestic regulators and government departments will be crucial; but regulators also need to coordinate across borders (transnational coordination) so that all parts of the global AI ecosystem are kept in check. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "transnational coordination between regulators is also needed to keep all parts of the global AI ecosystem in check"</sub>

## Definition and characteristics of artificial intelligence

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 24: it is important to consider characteristics of AI applications and how they differ from non-AI applications producing the same result, which may include AI complexity, its iterative approach, use of hyperparameters and use of unstructured datasets. The Bundesbank and BaFin set out ML characteristics defining a boundary of what is in or out of scope rather than a specific ML definition; with no single recognised definition of AI, this may help firms and regulators. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Rather than use a specific definition of ML, the paper sets out various ML characteristics"</sub>

## Artificial intelligence in financial services

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraphs 25-26: the report is not a comprehensive overview (Annex 1 gives use-cases). Data are at the core of financial services and the industry has long used mathematical and statistical modelling, so finance has seen relatively widespread AI adoption, with firms using AI from customer services and consumer credit to anti-money laundering (AML) and anti-fraud analytics and investment management. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "From customer services to consumer credit, anti-money laundering (AML) and anti-fraud analytics to investment management"</sub>

## Box 2: Hyperparameters

- **descriptive / definition**: Box 2: hyperparameters are parameters in AI that govern how a model is selected and trained; their values are fixed ahead of the training iterations, in contrast to parameter values, which are computed during the model fitting process. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "They are specified before training iterations, unlike parameter values"</sub>

## Algorithm auditing and certification

- **descriptive / context**: Auditing and certification of AI models could help address many governance challenges and wider transparency and explainability questions that could build broader trust. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Auditing and certification of AI models could help to address many of the governance challenges"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Open questions on AI auditing include who should set the framework, to achieve what outcomes, whether it should focus on the model, the data or both, and its role in regulation including sector-specific supervision; aligning AI auditing with other standards (sectoral guidance, data protection, equality law, employment law, competition policy, international alignment) is also a challenge. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "who should set the AI auditing framework? To achieve what outcomes?"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: AI certification raises similar questions: whom it targets and why, who undertakes it and the roles of public and private sectors, what standards and how they evolve, how to handle trade-offs such as transparency versus protecting intellectual property, and how to fit new audit and certification frameworks into the existing Enterprise-Wide Risk Management framework. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "How to navigate trade-offs, such as the need for transparency and prevention of the loss of intellectual property?"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: Paragraph 154 says there may also be a case for data auditing, covering provenance, ageing, how data are generated, what changes were made over the lifecycle and what the known limitations are. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There may also be a case for data auditing, including provenance, ageing"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 154 states that auditors still have a skills gap on AI models and data, compounded by AI auditing being in its infancy, so that in the immediate future auditing would need to be carried out by multidisciplinary teams. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There remains a skills gap for auditors when it comes to AI models and data."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 154 says there is a need to consider data auditing and model auditing holistically. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There is also a need to consider data and model auditing holistically."</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Paragraph 156 says that, to ensure an effective AI audit, a framework must be in place around which assurance products could be developed. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a framework must be in place around which assurance products could be developed"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 156 adds that there are certainly lessons to be learned from financial audit and control frameworks, including good practice for controls and for the use of data and AI. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There are certainly lessons to be learned from financial audit and control frameworks"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Developing an audit framework requires first establishing standards to audit against; AI audit currently has no industry standards on required metrics, or on transparency and explainability, and relevant risk frameworks are understood but not yet in place.   <sub>anchor: "AI audit currently has no industry standards on required metrics"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: There is an opportunity to simplify metrics: an accountable executive overseeing a suite of models finds a range of metrics hard to interpret, so the industry would find it useful to pick a small set of representative metrics to become commonplace; a two-tiered standard-setting approach could couple high-level principles from regulators or standard-setters with more detailed internal standards embedded in firm governance.   <sub>anchor: "it would be useful to pick a small set of representative metrics to become commonplace"</sub>

  <sub>anchor: "internal audit and 3LOD teams may not have the relevant AI skills"</sub>

## I – Use-cases

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 164 identifies three specific use-cases to consider: credit, savings and investment advice, and AML and fraud detection; other use-cases worth considering are customer on-boarding, money management and personalised financial offers, information extraction and document scanning, and marketing. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "(i) credit, (ii) savings and investment advice, and (iii) AML and fraud detection"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: AI can enable new use-cases; high-dimensional and quantum-like algorithms are starting to appear (not necessarily in financial services) and could be applied to complex money-laundering networks, especially where parts of the network are hidden. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These could be applied to complex money-laundering networks, especially where parts of the network are hidden."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: On credit, AI models can potentially improve predictive power and profitability; AI credit-card models can give an uplift in predictive power of up to 10% (measured by Gini impurity) compared to logistic regression models, giving the firm additional revenue and the consumer lower false positives/negatives. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "uplift in predictive power of up to 10% (as measured by Gini impurity)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: On savings and investment advice, AI models can potentially provide much more granular and personalised advice and help consumers understand risks and make more informed choices with appropriate guardrails. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI models can potentially provide much more granular and personalised advice"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: On AML and fraud detection, AI can help address synthetic identity fraud, in which synthetic identities are created from a jigsaw of real data. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "synthetic identities are created from a jigsaw of real data"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Table in paragraph 167, credit use-case: data are traditional credit applications complemented with alternative data (telecoms, social media, etc.) and behavioural data; the vast majority of models are linear regression, though with AI/ML they tend to be gradient boosted models (typically decision trees), and neural networks which may be used as inputs to linear regression models. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Traditional credit applications, complimented with alternative data (telecoms, social media, etc.)."</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Table in paragraph 167, savings and investment advice: uses lots of customer data, market signal data and alternative data, with natural language processing used for sentiment analysis. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Natural language processing is used for sentiment analysis."</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Table in paragraph 167, AML and fraud detection: very broad data sources from IP address to behavioural patterns; AML and sanctions use automated tree-based models such as random forest and graph-based AI; fraud detection uses neural networks and many unsupervised models to generate alerts. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AML and sanctions use automated tree-based models, such as random forest, and graph-based AI."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnotes 69 and 70: Gini impurity (not to be confused with the Gini coefficient in economics) measures a model's power to classify new instances; logistic regression is a statistical technique using a logistic function to model a binary dependent variable.   <sub>anchor: "Gini impurity (not to be confused with the Gini coefficient in economics)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In practice retail credit decisioning is not yet fully automated; AI-based models support decisions that are inputs into the overarching lending process, partly because of the treating consumers fairly principle and the human control and oversight currently required to meet it. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "retail credit decisioning is not yet fully automated"</sub>

  <sub>anchor: "The majority of challenges and risks apply to all three use-cases"</sub>

## Governance frameworks and structures

- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 117 says that where possible firms should use and adapt existing governance frameworks (data governance, MRM, operational risk management) to manage the novel challenges of AI, although some aspects of AI use may require new risk management and governance frameworks. ## Governance frameworks and structures

- **descriptive / limitation**: Balancing the scope of different lines of defence is hard, made more difficult by lack of model risk and validation skills in 1LOD teams and lack of data science and AI skills in 2LOD functions; automation changes 1LOD skill needs, so 1LOD teams may need software engineers as well as data scientists to integrate automated monitoring controls with the MRM framework. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "lack of relevant model risk and validation skills in the 1LOD teams"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: One way to address the skills gap is more training and closer 1LOD and 2LOD collaboration (especially data science teams with 2LOD MRM teams), but this requires significant investment, may realign staff incentive structures and may erode 2LOD independence. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "provide more training and promote closer collaboration between the 1LOD and 2LOD"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: An alternative is to integrate various 2LOD functions and rely less on MRM alone, since most 2LOD teams are small and MRM cannot be expert across all risks; AI risk management could be shared between MRM, data, cyber, IT, compliance and other 2LOD risk functions. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI risk management could be shared between MRM, data, cyber, IT, compliance"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: An enterprise-wide approach could help with AI's complexity and scale; a cross-functional or enterprise-wide 2LOD function could capture outputs of all models in all business areas, including third-party models, giving an aggregate view of model, operational, credit, market, traded and cyber risks. ## Box 10: SHAP & LIME

- **descriptive / definition**: SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) is based on the Shapley value, a game theory method for assigning payouts to players according to their contribution; it quantifies each feature's contribution to a model prediction by assigning an importance value, helping explain feature importance or the inner workings of the black box. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a game theory method for assigning payouts to players depending on their contribution to the total payout"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: LIME (Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations) uses local or surrogate models trained to approximate the black box model's predictions; instead of a global surrogate it trains local surrogate models to explain individual predictions. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "LIME focuses on training local surrogate models to explain individual predictions"</sub>

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Conflation of 'fairness' and 'bias' is a challenge; there are many definitions of fairness and which to use is often a business decision; businesses may be uncomfortable discussing fair outcomes and less clear on fair outcomes than fair processes. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There are many definitions of fairness; which one to use is often a business decision"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Change management matters for fairness: ethics conversations take time and need senior leadership buy-in, the right skillsets, a multidisciplinary and diverse approach and a culture allowing internal challenge; third-party vendors and customers may assess fairness differently, and what is considered fair can change over time. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "creating the right environment for conversations on ethics takes time and requires buy-in from senior leadership"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A further question is whether fairness and bias apply to corporate entities and not just individuals; within certain legislation small and micro-businesses have similar rights to natural persons, with possible implications for lending to small versus large businesses. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "small and micro-businesses have similar rights to natural persons"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 142 says problems of bias and fairness are not intrinsic to AI models but are potentially worsened by the ability of these models to extract (often hidden) patterns and relationships or to exploit existing biases. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Although problems around bias and fairness are not intrinsic to AI models"</sub>
- **permissive / typology or red flag**: Paragraph 142 gives as examples that biases may be embedded in aggregated data or processed through proxy features. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Biases may, for example, be embedded in aggregated data or processed through proxy features."</sub>
- **permissive / context**: Paragraph 142 notes that much work has already been done on treating customers fairly in financial services, and it may be possible to expand and build on that work to incorporate AI. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There has already been much work on treating customers fairly in financial services"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 147 says there also needs to be a distinction between different types of biases. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There also needs to be a distinction between different types of biases"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 147 says that although human biases are at the heart of many of these biases, it is important to understand how AI systems may exploit them. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is important to understand how AI systems may exploit them"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Paragraph 147 identifies two broad categories of bias: first, bias inherent in the data used to train AI models; second, bias that emerges from the models. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The first broad category is bias that is inherent in the data used to train AI models."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 147 notes that many biases are intended and central to business considerations, for example higher insurance premiums for higher risk businesses. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Many biases are intended and central to business considerations (e.g. higher insurance premiums for higher risk businesses)"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 147 says it is the unintended biases and unintended outcomes that should be of concern. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is the unintended biases and unintended outcomes that should be of concern."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 147 says it is important to have frameworks and decision-making processes that distinguish between 'good' and 'bad' bias. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "So it is important to have frameworks and decision-making processes that distinguish between"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Defining a list of features to exclude from datasets and models is not realistic; the burden remains of proving no proxy relationships between variables and known protected characteristics, raising the question whether responsibility to remove bias lies with firms, regulators (who would need to keep an updated exclusion list) or both. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Defining a list of features that should be excluded from datasets and models is not realistic."</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Regulators could assure themselves that supervised firms evaluate bias and fairness appropriately by defining criteria for measuring bias in models and setting criteria for evaluating bias and fairness of models throughout the development lifecycle from technical and business sides; there is also a social dimension on firms' responsibility to identify at-risk individuals or families, including those identified by AI models. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "defining a set of criteria for measuring bias in models"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: There is a risk of being bound to what is known today and ignoring future change in how data are classified and collected, e.g. changing ways people express gender identity, and areas where technology is less widespread so data sourced only from mobile devices would be unrepresentative. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There is a risk to being bound to what is known today"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: It is important who is 'in the room' when AI models or use-cases are created: a diverse team and culture that permits internal challenge is needed to critically review algorithms and data from an ethical standpoint. (p. 37)[^original]

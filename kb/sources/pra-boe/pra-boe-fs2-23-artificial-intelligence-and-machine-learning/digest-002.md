## 4: Regulation

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q11 (paragraph 4.14): some respondents called for clarity on requirements relating to accountability and data protection, and two respondents noted the importance of regulatory sandboxes in facilitating responsible innovation. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "importance of regulatory sandboxes in facilitating responsible innovation"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q12 (paragraph 4.15): most respondents said existing firm governance structures are already sufficient to cover AI or are being adapted by firms to be sufficient and to comply with existing regulatory requirements. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "existing firm governance structures are either already sufficient to cover AI"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q12 (paragraph 4.16): some respondents said firms should have a central or strategic AI function or committee to approve or oversee AI deployment for a coherent approach; others said local business areas should retain accountability for specific AI applications and outputs, and this was not seen as mutually exclusive. Some said board-level or senior management expertise or oversight of AI adoption is necessary; a few said governance should be proportionate (smaller firms may struggle to build the same structures as larger ones); a few said it is important to embed data science skills in audit and compliance functions. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "firms should implement a central or strategic AI function or committee"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q13 (paragraph 4.17): most respondents did not think a new Prescribed Responsibility (PR) for AI, allocated to a Senior Management Function (SMF), would help. Several said there are too many AI applications and that responsibilities are already reflected in existing PRs or could be in existing SMFs' statements of responsibilities. Many said firms need local owners to retain accountability, and some said a new PR is not technology neutral. Two respondents said it could overburden the Chief Operations Officer (SMF24), the most likely SMF to receive it. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could overburden the Chief Operations Officer (SMF24)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q13 (paragraph 4.18): other respondents disagreed and thought a new PR for AI allocated to an SMF would be helpful, creating an incentive for meaningful accountability for AI deployment and oversight; two responses suggested such an SMF could be a helpful single point of contact for regulators. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "create an incentive for meaningful accountability for AI deployment and oversight within firms"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q14 (paragraph 4.19): most respondents thought further guidance on interpreting the 'reasonable steps' element of the SM&CR in an AI context would be helpful, but only if practical or actionable; three respondents stressed engaging with industry on the substance of the guidance. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "only if it was practical or actionable guidance"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q14 (paragraph 4.20): some respondents disagreed, saying further guidance would not help; three noted additional guidance could create duplication or confusion with existing regulation and guidance. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "additional guidance could create duplication or confusion with respect to existing regulation and guidance"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q15 (paragraph 4.21): most respondents argued some areas of data regulation are insufficient to identify, manage, monitor and control AI model risks. Some pointed to insufficient regulation on data access, data protection and data privacy (for example to monitor bias); some thought regulation on data quality, data management and operations is insufficient. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "areas of data regulation that are not sufficient to identify, manage, monitor, and control the risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q15 (paragraph 4.22): many respondents said aligning the authorities' data definitions and taxonomies would be valuable, consistent with a general call for greater coordination among sectoral regulators. Several noted data regulation is a patchwork needing a more consolidated approach; two felt data regulation is already sufficient. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "value in alignment of the supervisory authorities’ data definitions / taxonomies"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q16 (paragraph 4.23): some respondents suggested clarifying expectations through additional guidance or best-practice guidance. They said existing regulation is sufficient for AI risks but clarification is needed on its application (for example accountability of different parties in outsourcing) and novel risks may need guidance later; best-practice guidance on responsible AI development and deployment would help firms. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "clarificatory guidance on the application of existing regulation is needed"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q16 (paragraph 4.24): several respondents suggested the authorities focus on consumer protection, promoting fairness, transparency and explainability so firms act with integrity and in consumers' best interests. They emphasised that managing data bias and effective oversight of development is key, especially for vulnerable consumers, and some suggested ways to identify and address bias, discrimination and exploitation, including monitoring and testing consumer outcomes. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "managing data bias and having effective oversight over the development process is key"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q16 (paragraph 4.25): many respondents emphasised cross-sectoral and cross-jurisdictional coordination because AI is a cross-cutting technology; some AI use cases are cross-sectoral and provisions beyond financial services, such as data protection and equality, are relevant. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "importance of cross-sectoral and cross-jurisdictional coordination as AI is a cross-cutting technology"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q16 (paragraph 4.26): respondents encouraged coherence and consistency across sectoral regulators, such as aligning key principles, metrics and interpretation of key concepts; some suggested working with other regulators to reduce overlaps and clarify the role of sectoral rules. Because many firms operate in multiple jurisdictions, an internationally coordinated and harmonised response is critical so that UK regulation does not disadvantage UK firms and markets while minimising fragmentation and operational complexity. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "ensure coherence and consistency in regulatory approaches across sectoral regulators"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q16 (paragraph 4.27): many respondents suggested the authorities collaborate or set up working groups with industry, academia and civil society, which would help address and monitor issues across the AI lifecycle; ongoing public-private engagement would help regulatory approaches adapt to technological change and public expectations. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "set up working groups with industry, academia, and civil society"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q17 (paragraph 4.28): respondents suggested international standards including BCBS 239 (Principles for effective risk data aggregation and risk reporting), BCBS 328 (Corporate governance principles for banks) and the US National Institute of Standards and Technology AI Risk Management Framework. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "BCBS 239 Principles for effective risk data aggregation and risk reporting"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q17 (paragraph 4.29): some respondents said emerging international standards would be important for a coherent global approach and interoperability; others cautioned that, given AI's rapid development, additional standards now could create uncertainty and stifle innovation. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "introducing additional standards at this stage could create uncertainty and in turn may stifle innovation"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q17 (paragraph 4.30): ISO and IEC jointly formed committee ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42 on artificial intelligence. Respondents cited its developing standards: ISO/IEC TS 4213 (Assessment of machine learning classification performance), ISO/IEC TR 24027:2021 (Bias in AI systems and AI aided decision making) and the ISO/IEC 24029 series (Assessment of robustness of neural networks). (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "ISO/IEC TS 4213 Assessment of machine learning classification performance"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q17 (paragraph 4.31): other technical standards mentioned for risk and security: ISO/IEC 27001 (information security management systems), the ISO/IEC 15504 series (process assessment in systems and software engineering), ISO 31000 (risk management), ISO/IEC 27032 (cybersecurity guidelines for Internet security) and ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148 (requirements engineering). The IEEE has also developed standards for AI model representation, compression, distribution and management. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "ISO/IEC 27001 Information security management systems"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q17 (paragraph 4.32): some respondents pointed to the AI Standards Hub, a partnership established jointly by the Alan Turing Institute, the British Standards Institution, the National Physical Laboratory and HM Government. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "partnership initiative established jointly between the Alan Turing Institute"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q18 (paragraph 4.33): a number of respondents argued a risk-based and principles-based approach to AI in financial services is key and would support international interoperability better than more prescriptive, potentially conflicting rules. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "would better support international interoperability than the adoption of more prescriptive"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q18 (paragraph 4.34): several respondents saw value in the draft EU AI Act's risk-based categorisation (unacceptable risk, high risk, low or minimal risk); one said it would give consistent classification across firms and focus risk management on higher-risk applications. Another said the draft focuses on a single criterion (impact on humans), which may not suit prudential regulators, giving algorithmic trading as an example that might not be high risk under the draft but could be from a prudential or financial stability view. Another said any framework must work with sector-specific regimes, citing MedTech Europe's example of duplication between the proposed EU AI Act and EU Medical Device and In Vitro Device Regulations. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "focuses on a single risk criterion (impact on humans)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q18 (paragraph 4.35): several respondents pointed to US risk-based guidance, and a number said the US Federal Reserve Supervisory Letter SR 11-7 on Model Risk Management is an effective framework for managing model and AI risk. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is an effective framework for managing model and AI risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q18 (paragraph 4.36): two respondents highlighted the NIST AI Risk Management Framework; one said it is helpful both for setting out key characteristics of trustworthy AI systems and for providing a playbook of actions to achieve the specified outcomes. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "providing a playbook of actions for achieving the specified outcomes"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q18 (paragraph 4.38): a number of respondents stressed harmonising regulatory approaches to AI across borders to mitigate the risk of conflicting requirements and significant compliance costs for cross-border firms and those expanding into new markets. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "harmonising regulatory approaches to AI across borders to mitigate the risk of conflicting requirements"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q19 (paragraph 4.39): many respondents said addressing regulatory uncertainty could help effective competition, since clear expectations give firms confidence to innovate, especially smaller firms that struggle disproportionately with uncertainty. Many said approaches should foster a level playing field and be flexible enough to adapt to technological change and manage public expectations; overly prescriptive or restrictive regulation would hinder AI development and likely become ineffective given the pace of change. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "overly prescriptive or restrictive regulations would, on the other hand, hinder the development of AI"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q19 (paragraph 4.40): several respondents emphasised the regulatory approach should be proportionate and not impose excessive costs, since high costs and burdens could be a barrier to entry and could widen the innovation gap between regulated and non-regulated financial services firms. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "High costs and regulatory burdens could be a barrier to entry"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q19 (paragraph 4.41): many respondents suggested the authorities support collaboration between firms, regulators, academia and technology practitioners to promote competition. Collaboration in developing and deploying AI, such as sharing knowledge and resources, could reduce costs, improve quality and make innovation more available to smaller firms; engaging a wide range of stakeholders would help keep proposals adaptable and proportionate. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "support collaboration between financial services firms, regulators, academia, and technology practitioners"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q19 (paragraph 4.42): some respondents suggested open banking could improve data access and so facilitate AI innovation and competition. Lack of access to high-quality data may be a barrier to entry for AI adoption, and open banking may level the playing field by giving firms larger and more diverse datasets. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Lack of access to high-quality data may be a barrier to entry for firms’ adoption of AI"</sub>

## 5: Questions

- **descriptive / context**: Chapter 5 lists all questions that appeared in DP5/22. Q1 asks whether a sectoral regulatory definition of AI in the authorities' rulebooks would help firms adopt AI safely and responsibly, and if so what it should be. Q2 asks whether equally effective approaches exist that do not rely on a definition and which suit UK financial services. Q3 asks which potential benefits and risks supervisory authorities should prioritise. Q4 asks how benefits and risks are likely to change as the technology evolves. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The questions listed below are all the questions that appeared in DP5/22."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: DP5/22 Q5 asks about novel AI-specific challenges in financial services not covered in the DP. Q6 asks how AI could affect groups sharing protected characteristics and how firms and/or the authorities can mitigate such impacts. Q7 asks which metrics are most relevant for assessing AI benefits and risks, including under an outcomes-focused approach. Q8 asks about other legal requirements or guidance relevant to AI. Q9 asks about regulatory barriers to safe and responsible AI adoption, particularly in rules and guidance for which the authorities have primary responsibility. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Are there any novel challenges specific to the use of AI within financial services that are not covered"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: DP5/22 Q10 asks how current regulation could be clarified with respect to AI. Q11 asks how current regulation could be simplified, strengthened and/or extended to encompass AI and address risks and harms. Q12 asks whether existing firm governance structures are sufficient for AI and, if not, how they could be changed. Q13 asks whether a new Prescribed Responsibility for AI allocated to an SMF would help effective governance. Q14 asks whether further guidance on the SM&CR 'reasonable steps' element in an AI context would help. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Q10: How could current regulation be clarified with respect to AI?"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: DP5/22 Q15 asks whether components of data regulation are insufficient to identify, manage, monitor and control AI model risks, and whether a unified approach to data governance and/or risk management or improved data definitions or taxonomies would add value. Q16 asks whether the authorities can do more to promote safe and beneficial AI innovation given the Chapter 3 risks. Q17 asks which existing industry standards are useful for developing, deploying or using AI and could support safe adoption. Q18 asks which approaches to AI regulation elsewhere would be worth replicating in the UK. Q19 asks about elements or approaches to apply or avoid to facilitate effective competition in UK financial services. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Are there any specific elements or approaches to apply or avoid to facilitate effective competition"</sub>

## 4: Regulation

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Feedback to the discussion paper (paragraph 4.37): several respondents pointed to the Monetary Authority of Singapore's (MAS) FEAT Principles (Fairness, Ethics, Accountability and Transparency) and the Veritas consortium programme with industry. One respondent said these initiatives provide a potential path forward in developing and operationalising principles for AI use in financial services; another argued the MAS approach balances governance requirements with practical considerations; one respondent noted MAS had published use-case studies on how organisations successfully integrated the FEAT Principles and the initial roadblocks, and highlighted MAS's open-source software toolkit, which could alleviate early technology challenges faced by many, particularly smaller, organisations using AI. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FEAT Principles (Fairness, Ethics, Accountability and Transparency) and Veritas consortium programme with industry"</sub>

## FS2/23 – Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning

- **descriptive / context**: The Bank of England (including the PRA) and the FCA, together the supervisory authorities, published discussion paper DP5/22 on Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning in October 2022 to deepen their understanding of, and dialogue on, how AI may affect their objectives for prudential and conduct supervision. The DP forms part of a wider AI work programme that includes the AI Public Private Forum (AIPPF), whose final report was published in February 2022. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "published a discussion paper (DP) 5/22 – Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning – in October 2022"</sub>

## Executive summary

- **descriptive / context**: Feedback statement FS2/23 summarises, anonymously, the responses to DP5/22 and identifies themes. It contains no policy proposals and does not signal how the authorities may clarify, design or implement current or future regulatory proposals on AI. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It does not include policy proposals, nor does it signal how the supervisory authorities are considering"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: DP5/22 received 54 responses from a wide range of stakeholders. Industry bodies were almost a quarter of respondents and banks a further fifth. There was no significant divergence of opinion between sectors. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "DP5/22 received 54 responses from a wide range of stakeholders."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Key point from respondents: a regulatory definition of AI would not be useful. Many pointed instead to principles-based or risk-based approaches focused on specific characteristics of AI or on the risks AI poses or amplifies. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A regulatory definition of AI would not be useful."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Key point from respondents: because AI capabilities change rapidly, regulators could respond with 'live' regulatory guidance, meaning periodically updated guidance and examples of best practice. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "periodically updated guidance and examples of best practice"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Key point from respondents: ongoing industry engagement is important, and initiatives such as the AIPPF have been useful and could serve as templates for continuing public-private engagement. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Initiatives such as the AIPPF have been useful and could serve as templates"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Key point from respondents: the regulatory landscape for AI is complex and fragmented, so more coordination and alignment between regulators, domestic and international, would be helpful. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "regulatory landscape is complex and fragmented with respect to AI"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Key point from respondents: most said data regulation in particular is fragmented, and that more regulatory alignment would help address data risks, especially those related to fairness, bias and management of protected characteristics. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Most respondents said that data regulation, in particular, is fragmented"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Key point from respondents: a key focus of regulation and supervision should be consumer outcomes, especially ensuring fairness and other ethical dimensions. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A key focus of regulation and supervision should be on consumer outcomes"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Key point from respondents: increasing use of third-party models and data is a concern and an area where more regulatory guidance would help; respondents also noted the relevance of DP3/22 on operational resilience and critical third parties to the UK financial sector. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Increasing use of third-party models and data is a concern"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Key point from respondents: because AI systems are complex and span many areas of a firm, a joined-up approach across business units and functions could help mitigate AI risks, in particular closer collaboration between data management and model risk management teams. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "closer collaboration between data management and model risk management teams would be beneficial"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Key point from respondents: the principles in CP6/22 (since published by the PRA as SS1/23 on model risk management principles for banks) were considered sufficient to cover AI model risk, although some areas could be strengthened or clarified for models with AI characteristics. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "were considered by respondents to be sufficient to cover AI model risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Key point from respondents: existing firm governance structures and regulatory frameworks such as the Senior Managers and Certification Regime (SM&CR) are sufficient to address AI risks. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "existing firm governance structures (and regulatory frameworks such as the Senior Managers and Certification Regime"</sub>

## 1: Introduction

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraphs 1.1-1.2: the feedback statement summarises responses to DP5/22, which set out the Bank and FCA views on AI and machine learning (ML) use in UK financial services. It contains no policy proposals and does not signal how future regulatory proposals might be clarified, designed or implemented. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This FS does not include policy proposals nor does it signal how the supervisory authorities"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 1.3: responses are presented anonymously and grouped into themes reflecting the Bank and FCA judgements. Not all respondents answered all questions, and the statement does not cover every comment or the full level of detail, though the Bank, PRA and FCA will use all responses in continuing to consider AI and ML in UK financial services. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Not all respondents answered all questions, and the grouping of responses into themes reflects"</sub>

## Background

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraphs 1.4-1.6: DP5/22 was published to further the authorities' understanding and deepen dialogue on AI, as part of a wider programme including the AI Public Private Forum. Comments were invited and the DP stated that a summary might be published. The Bank and the FCA received 54 responses from regulated firms, trade bodies and non-financial businesses. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Bank and the FCA received 54 responses from regulated firms, trade bodies, and non-financial businesses."</sub>

## Feedback statement structure

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 1.7: Chapters 2, 3 and 4 of the statement describe responses to the corresponding chapters of DP5/22, and Chapter 5 lists the questions posed in the DP. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Chapters 2, 3, and 4 of this statement describe the responses to the corresponding chapters in DP5/22."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 2.1: Chapter 2 of DP5/22 outlined the authorities' objectives and remits and surveyed approaches to distinguishing AI from non-AI. Some approaches give a legal definition of AI (for example the proposed EU AI Act) while others identify key characteristics (for example the UK AI White Paper). The DP asked whether a sector-specific regulatory definition is beneficial and whether effective approaches exist that do not rely on a definition. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Some approaches provide a legal definition of AI (eg the proposed EU AI Act )"</sub>

## 2: Supervisory authorities’ objectives and remits

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q1 (paragraph 2.2): most respondents thought a financial-services sector-specific regulatory definition of AI would not help safe and responsible AI adoption. Reasons given: (i) it could quickly become outdated given the pace of technology, (ii) it could be too broad (covering non-AI systems) or too narrow (missing use cases), (iii) it could create incentives for regulatory arbitrage, and (iv) it could conflict with the authorities' technology-neutral approach. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a definition could become quickly outdated due to the pace of technology development"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q1 (paragraph 2.3): respondents supporting a sectoral definition said it may help prevent misinterpretation or inconsistent implementation of requirements. A few favoured a broad but not sector-specific definition, noting a sectoral one would add complexity where jurisdictions define AI differently. Some said the benefit depends on the regulatory approach; if there are AI-specific rules, a sectoral definition would help firms understand what is in scope. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may help prevent misinterpretation and/or inconsistent implementation of regulatory requirements"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q2 (paragraph 2.4): most respondents suggested a technology-neutral, outcomes-based and principles-based approach would be effective. They noted many AI-related risks are not unique to AI and could be mitigated within existing legislative or regulatory frameworks, and that a technology-neutral approach could leverage existing regulation while balancing risk management and innovation proportionately. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "technology-neutral, outcomes-based, and principles-based approach would be effective"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q2 (paragraph 2.5): respondents said high-level principles give firms and regulators flexibility to adapt to technological developments and let firms tailor the identification, assessment and management of risks to the purpose, function and outcomes of each AI use case. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "high-level principles could give firms and regulators the flexibility to adapt to technological developments"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q2 (paragraph 2.6): respondents suggested the regulatory focus should be on outcomes affecting consumers and markets rather than specific technologies, in line with existing regulation under which firms should ensure good outcomes and effective oversight whether or not AI is used. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "regulatory focus should be on the outcomes affecting consumers and markets rather than on specific technologies"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q2 (paragraph 2.7): respondents emphasised that the approach to AI should be proportionate to the risks or materiality of each AI application. Some said the existing framework should be adequate, but recognised that specific risks or use cases may need further targeted guidance or intervention. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "approach to AI should be proportionate to the risks associated with, or materiality of each specific AI application"</sub>

## 3: Potential benefits and risks

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 3.1: Chapter 3 of DP5/22 summarised potential benefits and risks of AI against the authorities' objectives, described how AI risk drivers arise at different levels (data, models, governance and so on) and invited views on priorities and mitigation, in particular how AI could affect groups with protected characteristics. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the drivers of risks related to AI in financial services can occur at different levels within AI systems"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q3 (paragraph 3.2): a majority of respondents cited consumer protection as the priority. Benefits noted included better consumer outcomes, more personalised advice, lower costs and better prices; risks included bias, discrimination, lack of explainability and transparency, and exploitation of vulnerable consumers or those with protected characteristics. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a majority cited consumer protection as an area for the supervisory authorities to prioritise"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q3 (paragraph 3.3): there was no clear consensus on AI's competition effects. Some said high barriers to entry (data and technology infrastructure, expertise) could favour large incumbents; others said there are no significant barriers, including because open-source AI models are available. Other competition risks mentioned were tacit collusion, market manipulation and herding behaviour. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There was no clear consensus on the potential benefits or risks of AI for competition."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q3 (paragraph 3.4): on market integrity and financial stability, respondents said AI's speed and scale could raise new forms of systemic risk, such as interconnectivity between AI systems and AI-induced firm failures. Market risks listed: (i) new forms of market manipulation, (ii) deepfakes spreading misinformation that destabilises markets, (iii) third-party AI models producing convergent models including digital collusion or herding, and (iv) AI amplifying flash crashes or automated market disruptions. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the speed and scale of AI could increase the potential for (new forms of) systemic risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q3 (paragraph 3.5): on governance, respondents saw insufficient oversight as the most salient risk. Some said firms may lack the skills and experience to oversee technical risks (data and model risks) and non-technical risks (consumer and market outcomes), which is especially worrying with greater adoption of third-party AI software. Some stressed the importance of human-in-the-loop to mitigate overreliance on, or overconfidence in the accuracy of, AI. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the most salient risk for firms is insufficient oversight"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q3 (paragraph 3.6): on operational resilience and outsourcing, respondents suggested third-party AI providers should give evidence supporting responsible development, independent validation and ongoing governance of their products so firms can make their own risk assessments. They argued providers do not always supply enough information, that third-party exposure could increase systemic risk given the scope and ubiquity of third-party AI, and that not all firms have the expertise for adequate due diligence of third-party AI. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "third-party providers of AI solutions should provide evidence supporting the responsible development"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q4 (paragraph 3.7): several respondents expected AI adoption to grow in scale and almost half expected AI model performance and complexity in financial services to increase. Possible benefits include greater accuracy and effectiveness, wider scope and scale of applications and higher efficiency, which may improve consumer or market outcomes if AI is deployed safely and responsibly. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "almost half of respondents said there is likely to be an increase in the performance and complexity"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q4 (paragraph 3.8): some respondents expected greater use of third-party products, including AI-as-a-Service and off-the-shelf models, which could raise dependence on technology providers and associated risks, for example firms not fully understanding the model or its data and lacking sufficient controls over risks such as data bias. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "increased use of third-party products by regulated firms to source AI capabilities and infrastructure"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q4 (paragraph 3.9): many respondents said greater scale and model complexity could increase demands on governance, monitoring and oversight; some said robust governance and human oversight are required as firms rely more on AI for decisions or critical tasks, and some stressed the need for firm-wide AI skills. Respondents cautioned there could be a lack of experts who understand the latest technologies and their risks. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "greater scale and model complexity could lead to increased demand on governance, monitoring and oversight"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q5 (paragraphs 3.10-3.11): there was no clear consensus on novel AI-specific challenges, and some respondents saw none. Some pointed to challenges in managing open-source models and third-party AI services, including difficulty conducting due diligence and ensuring regulatory controls. They welcomed the PRA expectations in SS1/23 but sought further clarification; with off-the-shelf models, model users may not fully understand deployment risks and reliance on third parties may grow. Some said the authorities should ensure firms provide sufficient explainability and accountability when using third-party AI models or services. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "no clear consensus among respondents on the novel challenges specific to the use of AI in financial services"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q5 (paragraph 3.12): respondents expected AI use to grow as it integrates into institutions' infrastructure, with bad actors gaining easier access to cyber-attack tools and developing new techniques to exploit AI systems. Input attacks and poisoning attacks could disrupt an otherwise robust model, and because advanced models are opaque, firms may struggle to tell poor model performance from compromise by cyber-attack. If widespread this may create systemic risks to financial markets. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Through input attacks and poisoning attacks, respondents noted that attackers could disrupt an otherwise robust model."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Q5 (paragraph 3.13): respondents suggested bad actors may increasingly use AI for fraud and money laundering, for example generative AI being readily exploited to create deepfakes to commit fraud. This could make fraud more sophisticated, larger in scale and harder to detect, creating risks to consumers and, if sufficient in magnitude, to financial stability. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "increased access of AI tools by bad actors who wish to use AI for fraud and money laundering"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q5 (paragraph 3.14): some respondents said Generative AI (GenAI) adoption may grow rapidly in financial services while its risks are not fully understood, especially bias, accuracy, reliability and explainability. Because of 'hallucinations' in GenAI outputs, firms and consumers relying on GenAI for financial advice or information may face risks. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "risks associated with the use of GenAI are not fully understood, especially risks related to bias, accuracy, reliability, and explainability"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q6 (paragraph 3.15): most respondents said bias (intended or unintended), discrimination and financial exclusion may be particularly relevant for consumers with protected characteristics or characteristics of vulnerability, and that AI consumer harms mostly originate from data. Data bias and unavailability of sufficient key data drive harms, and most agreed data used to build an AI system should be representative, diverse and free from bias. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "data used to build an AI system should be representative, diverse, and free from bias"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q6 (paragraph 3.16): some respondents noted AI could help mitigate consumer harms, for example by helping firms identify patterns of unfair or discriminatory outcomes more effectively, creating products for consumers with characteristics of vulnerability and enhancing financial inclusion. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI could help mitigate consumer harms"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q6 (paragraph 3.17): most respondents said firms should focus on mitigating data bias, with strategies such as addressing data quality issues, documenting biases in data and capturing additional data that may highlight impact on particular groups with shared characteristics. Some suggested firms monitor and evaluate consumer outcomes when an AI model delivers regulated products or services, and stressed effective governance and oversight of data and AI to prevent bias and discrimination. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "most respondents suggested that firms should focus on mitigating data bias"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q6 (paragraph 3.18): on what the authorities could do, respondents suggested guidance clarifying regulatory expectations; some wanted guidance on interpreting and evaluating good consumer outcomes in the AI context under existing rules such as the FCA Consumer Duty, guidance on preventing, evaluating and mitigating bias with case studies, and guidance on use of personal data in AI in financial services with case studies. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supervisory authorities could release guidance to clarify regulatory expectations"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q7 (paragraph 3.19): respondents stressed that the most relevant metrics depend on the use case. For an AI model improving payment matching, example metrics are percentage increase in payments processed, percentage reduction in processing errors and improvement in customer satisfaction. For anti-money laundering applications, two key metrics are precision (proportion of highlighted records ultimately found suspicious) and recall (proportion of suspicious records the system highlights). One respondent said metrics depend on whether personal, commercial, market or other data is processed; one said firms should use multiple complementary metrics; a few said it is too early to say which are most relevant. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "precision (the proportion of highlighted records that are ultimately found to be suspicious)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q7 (paragraph 3.20): around half of respondents to the question said metrics focused on consumer outcomes would be most important. One respondent suggested indicators of better outcomes could include factors in the FCA Consumer Duty: more consumer-centric product or service design, evidence of engagement so customers can make timely and informed decisions, and evidence of consistently considering customer needs and behaviour at every lifecycle stage. Others suggested customer satisfaction and complaint metrics. Some said outcome-based metrics should not differ between AI and non-AI systems. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "metrics focused on consumer outcomes would be most important in assessing the benefits and risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q7 (paragraph 3.21): several respondents stressed monitoring fairness metrics designed to identify biased outcomes. Quantitative fairness metrics named were equalised odds, demographic parity, disparate impact, statistical parity difference, average odds difference and equalised opportunity difference. Some noted each metric has a different focus and relies on a different concept of fairness; one respondent argued regulators need to engage with industry to establish which metrics suit each context. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "equalised odds, demographic parity, disparate impact, statistical parity difference, average odds difference, and equalised opportunity difference"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q7 (paragraph 3.22): around half of respondents stressed data and model performance metrics during development and after deployment. Suggested areas: data quality, data representativeness, data drift, model accuracy, robustness, reproducibility, model/concept drift, and the time and cost to train and re-train. Several noted the need for explainability metrics such as SHAP and LIME. One respondent flagged measuring the system's autonomy from human control or oversight and how easily it could be turned off without disrupting business services. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) and Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations (LIME)"</sub>

## 4: Regulation

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 4.1: Chapter 4 of DP5/22 discussed the parts of the regulatory framework most relevant to AI and the authorities' approach to Chapter 3 risks, and invited feedback on whether clarifying existing requirements would help and how the authorities could best support safe and responsible AI adoption. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The DP invited feedback on whether clarification of existing regulatory requirements and expectations would be helpful"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q8 (paragraph 4.2): one respondent noted the suite of regulation governing AI in the financial sector is already extensive so care is needed to avoid unnecessary new requirements; another highlighted the importance of a consistent supervisory approach across authorities. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "care is needed to avoid creating unnecessary new requirements"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q8 (paragraph 4.3): on data protection, one response noted guidance indicating the UK GDPR 'right to erasure' extends to personal data used to train AI models, which could be hard in practice because developers have limited ability to separate and remove training data from a trained model. One respondent argued suppliers, developers and users may lack understanding of data protection law as applied to AI, potentially gaming or ignoring the rules. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "extends to personal data used to train AI models"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q8 (paragraph 4.4): respondents stressed the relevance of the operational resilience and outsourcing framework, including PRA SS1/21 (Operational resilience: Impact tolerances for important business services), SS2/21 (Outsourcing and third party risk management) and the FCA's PS21/3 (Building operational resilience), and noted DP3/22 on critical third parties. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "SS2/21 – Outsourcing and third party risk management"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q8 (paragraph 4.5): respondents argued an industry-wide data quality standard is necessary and a potential legal gap, because current legal requirements and regulatory expectations for regulated firms do not include a common data quality standard. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an industry-wide standard for data quality is necessary and a potential legal gap"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q8 (paragraph 4.6): other laws and guidance respondents considered relevant were discrimination laws (for example the Equality Act 2010), intellectual property law, contract law, laws on electronic communications and infrastructure (for example the Product Security and Telecommunications Infrastructure Act 2022) and forms of ethical guidance. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "discrimination laws (eg the Equality Act 2010 ), intellectual property law, contract law"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q9 (paragraph 4.8): many respondents thought there were no regulatory barriers to safe and reasonable adoption of AI in UK financial services. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Many respondents thought that there were no regulatory barriers"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q9 (paragraph 4.9): a number of respondents disagreed and cited barriers around data protection and privacy. One said the UK GDPR interaction with AI was hard to navigate, citing the provisions on rights related to automated decision making including profiling; another viewed the interaction as amounting to a prohibition on automated decision-making. A few said data localisation requirements were a concern, noting the EU's proposed Cybersecurity Certification Scheme on Cloud Services would include mandatory localisation in the EU. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "amounting to a prohibition on automated decision-making"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q10 (paragraph 4.10): several respondents sought clarification on what bias and fairness mean for AI models, including how firms should interpret the Equality Act 2010 and the FCA Consumer Duty; others wanted clarity on how data protection and privacy rights interact with AI techniques, and on explainability for AI applications. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "what bias and fairness could mean in the context of AI models"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q10 (paragraph 4.11): several respondents asked for clarity on governance, human oversight and where accountability for AI outcomes sits in a firm, some linking this to third-party vendor solutions. Others asked how firms should assess and rate AI model risk for model risk management. One respondent argued rating tiers should align with the definition of 'important business service' in the Operational Resilience Part of the PRA Rulebook and that firms should keep an inventory of high-risk AI use cases. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "firms should maintain an inventory of high-risk AI use cases"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q11 (paragraph 4.12): some respondents asked for more practical, actionable guidance and illustrative case studies; others called for direct guidance on risk-based approaches so that a firm's risk management measures (data, model risk management and governance) are proportionate to the risk of the AI use case. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "more practical and actionable guidance and illustrative case-studies"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Q11 (paragraph 4.13): a few respondents requested clarity on implementing bias and fairness requirements in practice; others on use of third-party vendors; some called for a consolidated stocktake of relevant legislation and regulation to understand how it encompasses AI. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a consolidated stocktake of relevant legislation and regulation"</sub>

## 3: Potential benefits and risks

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Feedback on cyber risk (paragraph 3.12): some respondents noted that AI use will increase as it becomes more integrated in financial institutions' infrastructure, so bad actors may gain easier access to cyber-attack tools; respondents also expected more sophisticated cyber-attacks, as attackers may develop new techniques for exploiting AI systems, increasing the risk of security incidents and data breaches. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "attackers may develop new techniques for exploiting AI systems, hence increasing the risk of security incidents and data breaches"</sub>

## Executive summary

- **descriptive / context**: The development and deployment of AI is likely to have a transformative impact across many UK sectors: it can save workers time and boost productivity, enhance firms' decision-making, make products and services better tailored to customers, and at the cutting edge catalyse other breakthroughs, with potential to increase long-term productive economic growth. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "likely to have a transformative impact across many sectors of the UK economy"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: AI is already helping financial institutions automate and optimise internal processes such as code generation and customer interactions; a likely area of development is advanced AI increasingly informing core financial decisions such as credit and insurance underwriting, potentially shifting the allocation of capital, but there is a high degree of uncertainty over how the technology and its use will evolve. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is a high degree of uncertainty over how the technology and its use will evolve"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: As a macroprudential policymaker the FPC defines a stable financial system as one with sufficient resilience to facilitate and supply vital services to households and businesses in a manner that absorbs rather than amplifies shocks; financial stability risks can arise even where individual firms' safety and soundness risks are well managed by microprudential authorities, for example from collective behaviour of firms. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "absorbs rather than amplifies shocks"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Core decision-making: AI can bring benefits such as greater choice and product availability but can introduce risks, especially in relation to models and data; firm-level risk management and microprudential regulation can help mitigate these by applying appropriate controls, including to agentic AI (systems that can take autonomous action to achieve specified goals using tools, learning from feedback and adapting to dynamic environments). (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "systems which can take autonomous action to achieve specified goals by utilising tools, learning from feedback"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Because of the high uncertainty over how AI will develop, the FPC is examining the macroprudential implications of AI becoming more widely used and changing in nature within the financial system, which lets it help the technology be adopted safely from a financial stability perspective and so support sustainable growth. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FPC is considering the potential macroprudential implications of more widespread, and changing, use of AI"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FPC's focus areas on AI are: greater use of AI in banks' and insurers' core financial decision-making; greater use of AI in financial markets; operational risks relating to AI service providers; and the changing external cyber threat environment. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In this context, the FPC is focused on the following areas:"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Systemic consequences could emerge if common weaknesses in widely used models cause many firms to misestimate certain risks and so misprice and misallocate credit, possibly causing loss of service provision for some households or businesses; reliance on AI models for key decisions could also lead to conduct-related risks, for example if decisions or processes were subject to legal challenge and financial redress. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "if common weaknesses in widely used models cause many firms to misestimate certain risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Financial markets: greater use of AI for trading and investment decisions could increase market efficiency but could lead participants inadvertently to act collectively in a way that reduces stability; more advanced AI-based trading strategies could lead firms to take increasingly correlated positions and act similarly during stress, amplifying shocks and affecting availability and cost of funding for the real economy. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "taking increasingly correlated positions and acting in a similar way during a stress"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Service providers: financial institutions generally rely on providers outside the financial sector for AI-related services, and reliance on a small number of providers for a given service could lead to systemic risks if they are disrupted, especially if it is not feasible to migrate rapidly to alternative providers. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Reliance on a small number of providers for a given service could lead to systemic risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: A more indirect way AI could affect the financial system is through wider economic adoption; if AI challenges established business models in certain sectors, this could affect some borrower firms' creditworthiness and increase credit risk for lenders exposed to them. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this could impact some borrower firms’ creditworthiness and thus increase credit risk"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The FPC intends to build out its monitoring approach to track AI-related financial stability risks; the approach will need to be flexible and forward-looking, and with support from the Bank, PRA and FCA the FPC plans to use a blend of quantitative and qualitative sources including the regular Bank and FCA Survey on AI in UK financial services, the AI Consortium, and targeted market and supervisory intelligence gathering. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The approach will need to be flexible and forward-looking given the uncertainties and potential pace of change in AI."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Cyber threat environment: AI might strengthen financial institutions' cyber defences but could also strengthen malicious actors' ability to mount successful cyberattacks on the financial system, and institutions' own AI use could open new vulnerabilities for those actors to exploit. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "financial institutions’ own use of AI could create new vulnerabilities that actors could exploit"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The FPC states that monitoring AI-related risks effectively is essential for understanding whether extra risk mitigations might be warranted to support safe innovation, what those might be, and when they could become appropriate. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The effective monitoring of AI-related risks is essential to understand whether additional risk mitigations might be warranted"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FPC says it will keep engaging actively with domestic and international initiatives to monitor and mitigate AI-related risks, not least because many such risks are likely to be cross-border. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FPC will also continue to engage actively with domestic and international initiatives to monitor and mitigate AI-related risks"</sub>

## 1: Context: the potential benefits of AI and its growing role in the financial system

- **descriptive / context**: Some forms of AI represent a discontinuity relative to previous modelling technologies: advanced AI models (including generative AI) can be dynamic, learning automatically from new input data so outputs evolve over time, can produce complex outputs and make decisions autonomously, and are trained on vast volumes of data on a different scale to previous tools. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "advanced AI models (including generative AI models) can be dynamic, learning automatically from new input data"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The distinct features of AI can also be additional sources of risk: complexity coupled with dynamic change creates challenges for predictability, explainability and transparency of model outputs; very large data volumes create challenges in ensuring data integrity; and potential market concentration in AI-related services, including vendor-provided models, is a further challenge. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "poses new challenges around the predictability, explainability and transparency of model outputs"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: As a general-purpose technology, AI can bring productivity gains across economic sectors by reducing resources spent on routine administrative tasks, enhancing institutional decision-making and tailoring products and services; the Government-commissioned AI Opportunities Action Plan says swift AI adoption could enhance competitiveness and unlock growth, and the UK is the third largest destination for AI investment globally. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the UK is well placed to capitalise on these opportunities"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FPC's work to protect financial system resilience includes emerging systemic risks whose timing and size are highly uncertain, and it is considering the implications of AI so as to contribute to adoption that safeguards financial stability and is conducive to sustainable growth. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This includes work on emerging systemic risks where there is considerable uncertainty over their potential timing and size"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FPC has an objective, subject to financial stability, to support the Government's economic policy, so when considering AI's financial stability implications it is also mindful of the significant economic opportunities presented by emerging technologies. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FPC is also mindful of the significant economic opportunities presented by them"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Many AI-based analytical techniques, such as those assisting statistical analysis, have been established for a decade or more, while advanced uses such as generative AI are now in production; so-called agentic AI systems, which can take autonomous action to achieve goals by using tools, learning from feedback and adapting, are not widespread at present, and some applications may not live up to their initial promise while unforeseen developments may have significant impacts. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "so-called agentic AI systems"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Financial institutions' decisions on pace, scale and nature of AI adoption depend on a combination of technical developments, commercial incentives and risk appetite; the regulatory environment is also relevant, as appropriate management of firm and system level risks can help firms innovate safely and unlock the technology's full benefits. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "technical developments, commercial incentives and risk appetite"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The 2024 AI Survey indicates the top near-term (next three years) AI use cases include optimising internal processes, enhancing customer support and combatting financial crime (Chart 1), operationally focused uses that are expected to be among those delivering the biggest benefits in three years; Chart 1 note defines AML/CFT as anti-money laundering and combating the financing of terrorism. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "optimising internal processes, enhancing customer support and combatting financial crime"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Generative AI such as large language models is often pre-trained and provided as cloud-based services by third-party providers, and is used to streamline internal functions such as code generation and information search and retrieval; AI-based analytics are also used to enhance customer interactions, e.g. helping payment firms predict a customer's preferred payment option. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These models are often pre-trained and provided as cloud-based services by third-party providers."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Greater AI use could help firms enhance their offering, for example by letting some lenders, including non-bank lenders, use a wider range of structured and unstructured data, which could in principle widen choice and access to finance for creditworthy companies including small and medium-sized enterprises. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could, in principle, widen choice and access to finance for creditworthy companies"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Supervisory intelligence suggests AI use in credit risk management is still in its infancy in aggregate, but some firms use AI-based techniques such as established gradient boosting decision tree models in prescreening, application scoring, pricing and provisioning across lending classes; among insurers, AI-based models are widely used to support pricing and underwriting, and the OECD highlights telematics (widely used in motor insurance) may increasingly feed AI models in other insurance products. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "some firms are using AI-based techniques (such as established gradient boosting decisions tree models)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Longer-term economic impacts of advanced AI are highly uncertain, but one study estimates that over the next 15 years generative AI could lift productivity by up to 30% in the banking and insurance sectors and among firms operating in capital markets. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "generative AI could bring productivity gains of up to 30% to the banking and insurance sectors"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The document notes that AI can also help public authorities achieve their objectives more efficiently and effectively, and that Box B covers how the Bank of England is adopting AI in its work, including in support of the FPC's financial stability objective. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI can also help public authorities to achieve their objectives more efficiently and effectively."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Among institutions undertaking algorithmic (rules-based) trading in highly liquid markets, established AI techniques such as decision trees are already used to refine predictive power of models feeding trading strategies; an IMF report says over half of all patents filed by high frequency or algorithmic trading firms now relate to AI; autonomous AI-based trading models do not yet appear widely in production but may plausibly be employed in future (Box A). (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "over half of all patents filed by high frequency or algorithmic trading firms now relate to AI"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The IMF report identified investment managers using generative AI to exploit alternative data sets such as social media content to uncover relationships between variables and generate new strategies; exploiting novel data sources could increase market efficiency with new information incorporated into pricing faster and more accurately. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to uncover previously unknown relationships between economic or financial variables"</sub>

## 2: The financial stability implications of AI

- **descriptive / context**: Section 2 explores how AI might interact with vulnerabilities at firm and system level and, especially without sufficient mitigations, lead to financial stability risks that could transmit to the real economy via systemically important institutions, systemically important markets or the operational delivery of vital services, across four areas: core decision-making in banks and insurers, financial markets, AI service providers, and the external cyber threat environment. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could transmit to the real economy via their effect on systemically important institutions"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Dynamism in complex AI models and a lack of predictability and explainability of outputs could make it harder for firms to manage related risks; around half of respondents to the 2024 AI Survey report only a 'partial understanding' of the AI technologies they use in the context of widespread vendor-provided models; AI output quality relies on input data quality, and very large-scale training data adds to challenges in ensuring data is relevant, of sufficient quality and free of bias. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "around half of the respondents to the 2024 AI Survey report having only a ‘partial understanding’ of the AI technologies they use"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Concentration in the generative AI market could increase over time due to the cost and complexity of models and vertical integration of parts of the 'AI stack', although factors such as widespread availability of open-source models could have the opposite effect. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "vertical integration of parts of the ‘AI stack’"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The section does not give a comprehensive overview of all AI-related financial stability risks, which would also cover effects on business models and market structures; for example AI could challenge business models and so affect borrower creditworthiness, and could increase the relative footprint of non-bank financial institutions (NBFIs) in certain markets through greater algorithmic trading; the high uncertainty makes longer-term impacts hard to assess. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI could also increase the relative footprint of non-bank financial institutions (NBFIs) in certain markets"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The document says that a scenario in which AI models are increasingly deployed autonomously, as opposed to being used largely as an assistive tool, could potentially pose significant additional risks to financial stability in future. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a scenario in which AI models are increasingly deployed in an autonomous manner"</sub>

## Risks can stem from vulnerabilities at both the firm and system level.

- **descriptive / context**: The FPC identifies and assesses risks by considering vulnerabilities at institution level (microfinancial) and system level (macrofinancial) and the transmission channels through which they affect financial stability; microfinancial vulnerabilities often relate to firms' safety and soundness, such as model risk, mitigated by microprudential regulation. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Microfinancial vulnerabilities often relate to risks that can impact individual firms’ safety and soundness, such as model risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Even where firm-level risks are well managed, macrofinancial vulnerabilities can mean collective firm behaviour in response to a shock affects financial stability, particularly where firms lack information or incentives to take account of system-level outcomes (are 'outcome agnostic'); such system risks are the focus of the FPC's macroprudential work. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "when they are outcome agnostic from a system perspective"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Figure 1 notes define 'microfinancial vulnerabilities' as entity-level vulnerabilities that determine financial and operational susceptibility to shocks, and 'macrofinancial vulnerabilities' as system features that, conditional on microfinancial vulnerabilities, can increase the impact of shocks across the system and real economy. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "‘Macrofinancial vulnerabilities’ are features of the system that help determine the impact of shocks on the system"</sub>

## While bringing various potential benefits to both firms and customers, AI can introduce new risks for individual firms, especially in relation to data and models.

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Banks and insurers are likely to increasingly integrate AI into core credit and insurance decisions, which could enhance offerings and improve accuracy of financial risk management, but it is important to be alert to risks in functions directly affecting a firm's financial position and customer outcomes; lack of explainability and potential autonomy of advanced models, if deployed without appropriate testing, governance and risk controls, could lead to a level of financial risk-taking that is not properly understood at the time. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "lead to a level of financial risk-taking that is not properly understood at the time"</sub>

## Microprudential regulation can help mitigate risks from AI...

- **descriptive / context**: Existing microprudential principles, regulation and guidance relevant to firm-level AI risks include model risk management, data and governance, and conduct measures, and the Senior Managers and Certification Regime (SM&CR) can ensure individual accountability for conduct and competence; responses to DP5/22 (summarised in FS2/23) highlighted aspects where existing regimes might need to evolve. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Senior Managers and Certification Regime (SM&CR) is a supervisory tool that can be used to ensure appropriate individual accountability"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The FPC says it will be important to ensure existing regulatory frameworks, alongside firm-level controls, sufficiently mitigate microfinancial AI risks, especially as AI models are increasingly used in agent functions, and it will continue to engage other authorities on relevant frameworks to inform its assessment of systemic AI risks. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It will be important to ensure that existing regulatory frameworks, alongside firm level controls, mitigate microfinancial risks"</sub>

## … but it is also important to consider system level implications.

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Common weaknesses in model and data risk management across firms would be a macrofinancial vulnerability: if many firms rely on the same open-source model components or data libraries, a significant unknown error or bias could cause many to misestimate risks and misprice and misallocate credit, generating losses for systemic firms, tighter credit supply or contagion through lost confidence; the report likens this to the 2008 Global Financial Crisis, where a debt bubble was partly fuelled by collective mispricing of risk. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a significant unknown error or bias could cause many firms to misestimate certain risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: High reliance on AI models for key risk management decisions could in principle affect other areas of firm resilience such as liquidity preparedness; where core decisions on availability and pricing of services rest on AI models, biased or wrongly calibrated data or models could directly affect consumer outcomes such as product access, giving rise to conduct risks (legal challenge and financial redress), amplified by practical issues over who is ultimately liable for AI model decisions. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "establishing who is ultimately liable for decisions made by AI models"</sub>

## Greater use of AI in financial markets

- **descriptive / context**: Market participants appear likely to integrate more advanced AI analysis into core trading and investment activities, though speed and scope are uncertain; institutions undertaking algorithmic trading already widely use established AI techniques such as decision trees to calibrate algorithms, and some investment managers use AI to help generate profitable insights. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "institutions undertaking algorithmic trading already widely use established AI techniques (such as decision trees)"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Greater AI use in core business processes could increase market efficiency (e.g. faster incorporation of new information) and returns for end-investors, but unknown data or model flaws might mean exposures are incorrectly measured, leaving insufficient resilience to market stress, and AI models may find it particularly challenging to respond to extreme events and radical uncertainty such as historically unprecedented shocks. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it may be particularly challenging for AI models to respond to extreme events and situations of radical uncertainty"</sub>

## AI-driven trading and investment strategies could increase the tendency for market participants to take correlated positions.

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: From a systemic risk perspective, AI-based participants could take increasingly correlated positions, driven by widespread use of a small number of open-source or vendor-provided models or data sets, or convergence on very similar model designs; herding and market concentration was the top risk cited in recent IMF outreach on wider generative AI adoption in capital markets. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Herding and market concentration was the top risk cited in recent IMF outreach"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: A future market with widespread autonomous AI trading might be more informationally efficient than one shaped by human traders but less resilient to shocks; increasingly correlated positioning could exacerbate fire-sales in a stress event (forced unwinding of leveraged positions), explored in the system-wide exploratory scenario, partly because institutions may not factor in the collective impact of their actions; systemic markets such as core bond markets are central to the flow of finance to the real economy. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "increasingly correlated positioning and strategies could exacerbate the impact of fire-sales in response to a stress event"</sub>

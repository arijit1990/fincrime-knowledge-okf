## Greater use of AI in financial markets

- **descriptive / context**: Greater AI use could in principle improve market resilience: AI could enhance risk management through better use of data, making fire-sale scenarios where leveraged firms are caught out by price moves less likely or less impactful, and, as noted by the FSB, investment managers' ability to offer increasingly customised options might, other things equal, reduce market correlations. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI could enhance risk management by enabling better use of available data"</sub>

## Advanced AI models could rationally exploit profit-making opportunities in a destabilising way or engage in other adverse behaviours.

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: If advanced AI trading models were deployed with more autonomy, they might identify and exploit weaknesses in other firms' trading strategies in a way that triggers or amplifies price movements, for example learning that stress events increase profit opportunities and so taking actions to increase the likelihood of such events. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "models might learn that stress events increase their opportunity to make profit"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Another risk is that such models could facilitate collusion or other market manipulation; given some models' ability to learn dynamically in multi-agent environments and limited explainability of outputs, such behaviours might emerge without the human manager's intention or awareness. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "such adverse behaviours might emerge without the human manager’s intention or awareness"</sub>

## Existing market monitoring and oversight measures are relevant to AI risks, and the FPC will continue to follow closely the implications of AI for systemic markets.

- **expected / expectation**: Because the rapid pace of AI change could lead to fast and significant shifts in risks to systemic markets, AI-related risks in this area merit an ongoing macroprudential focus and the FPC intends to monitor relevant developments closely (Section 3). (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI-related risks in this area merit an ongoing focus from a macroprudential perspective"</sub>

## Operational risks in relation to AI service providers

- **descriptive / context**: To capture AI productivity benefits financial institutions generally rely on service providers outside the financial sector; for many implementations they rely on vendor-provided models, particularly for very complex models such as recent large language models, and even for in-house models they may rely on cloud computing and external data aggregators for the large training data sets. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they may rely on cloud computing to develop and operate these models, and on external data aggregators"</sub>

## Growing concentration in the supply of AI-related services could increase risks to the financial system.

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: AI Survey evidence supports the view that third-party exposure will increase as model complexity rises and outsourcing costs fall; greater AI-driven interconnectedness could heighten existing vulnerabilities, and reliance on a small number of providers could create systemic risk if they are disrupted, especially if rapid migration to alternatives is not feasible. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "third-party exposure will continue to increase as the complexity of models increases and outsourcing costs decrease"</sub>

## This highlights the importance of building and maintaining operational resilience, an existing area of focus for the FPC.

- **descriptive / context**: In March 2024 the FPC set out its macroprudential approach to operational resilience, noting that firm-level operational resilience is the essential foundation for system-wide resilience; firms can mitigate operational risks through effective control frameworks and investment, and microprudential and macroprudential policies exist to manage certain existing risks, including Bank, PRA and FCA policies requiring regulated firms to deliver important business services within impact tolerances under severe but plausible disruption, and an FPC 'impact tolerance' for critical payments. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "firm level operational resilience provides the essential foundation for operational resilience across the system"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Public-private collaboration between the Bank, other authorities and financial and non-financial firms is supporting a 'shared responsibility model' for AI, whose output will be guidance on a structure for managing implementation risks, such as whether the third-party provider or client firm manages data in different AI deployments, which should help minimise divergent approaches causing firm or sector level operational impacts. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "development of a ‘shared responsibility model’ for AI"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Financial Services and Markets Act 2023 established a regime for critical third parties, in response to the FPC's 2021 recommendation on concentration risk; the Bank, PRA and FCA jointly published rules in November 2024 (supervisory statement 6/24); certain third parties providing data and AI models could emerge as future critical third parties, and AI services may differ from other third-party usage through the complexity of the most computationally powerful foundation models and challenges in identifying specialised and niche providers. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Certain third parties providing data and AI models could also emerge as potential future critical third parties"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Given rapid evolution in how financial institutions interact with the AI market and in its structure, the FPC will monitor developments in this space closely (Section 3). (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FPC will monitor developments in this space closely"</sub>

## AI could be a new tool for malicious actors that already pose risks to financial companies...

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Cyberattacks are a significant risk to many financial firms and remain near the top of perceived key sources of risk in the Bank's most recent Systemic Risk Survey; the FPC has noted higher geopolitical tensions heighten cyberattack risk, and in the 2024 AI Survey cybersecurity was near the top of perceived current AI-related risks, expected to grow over the next three years. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they remain near the top of the list of the perceived key sources of risk to financial system"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Threat actors' use of AI could increase their capability to carry out successful cyberattacks with greater sophistication and scale; institutions' own AI use could open new opportunities to exploit, e.g. vulnerabilities in third-party software or hardware; model development could be targeted via malicious manipulation of training data ('data poisoning'); and the potential combination of AI with future quantum computing will need monitoring. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "malicious manipulation of model training data (so-called data poisoning)"</sub>

## … exacerbating the risks they pose to the wider financial system.

- **descriptive / context**: The Bank says financial stability might ultimately be affected if, for example, systemic markets or the operational delivery of vital services were materially disrupted as a result of AI-related cyberattacks with systemic implications, such as large-scale attacks exploiting shared cyber vulnerabilities in common AI models deployed across systemic firms. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial stability might ultimately be affected, if, for example, systemic markets or the operational delivery of vital services"</sub>

## AI could be a new tool for malicious actors that already pose risks to financial companies...

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: AI might also increase capabilities of other malicious actors: those engaged in illicit financing (money laundering or terrorism financing) could use AI models to circumvent institutional controls; public or customer-facing AI models create risks such as 'prompt injection' (attackers manipulating models to extract confidential information); and generative AI, including 'deepfakes' and highly personalised text, could make fraud schemes against institutions or retail customers more effective and harder to detect. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could seek to use AI models to circumvent institutional controls"</sub>

## … exacerbating the risks they pose to the wider financial system.

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: AI-related cyberattacks could have systemic implications: widespread deployment of common AI models with shared cyber vulnerabilities across systemic firms would be a system-wide vulnerability that could increase the impact of large-scale attacks, spreading through operational contagion or loss of confidence; recent ransomware attacks at several financial firms and third-party providers did not affect financial stability but showed disruption at one firm can cause disruption at others. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the widespread deployment of common AI models with shared cyber vulnerabilities across systemic firms"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: A more general increase in cyberthreats could affect the real economy and so indirectly financial institutions; for example AI-based disinformation tools such as deepfakes could exacerbate geopolitical tensions and increase economic uncertainty. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI-based disinformation tools such as deepfakes could be used to exacerbate existing geopolitical tensions"</sub>

## However, AI could improve firms’ ability to combat threat actors.

- **descriptive / finding or result**: AI use by financial institutions could improve their ability to combat malicious actors, e.g. detecting cyberthreats by improving automated identification of malware or illicit finance activity; 2024 AI Survey respondents expected benefits of AI for cybersecurity and anti-money laundering to grow significantly over three years, so the impact is bi-directional, raising a prospect of a technological arms race and making the overall financial stability impact uncertain. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "raising the prospect of a technological arms race between financial companies and malicious actors"</sub>

## Public-private sector collaboration will be important for helping address AI-related cyberthreats.

- **descriptive / case example**: UK authorities and industry collaborate on cyber risk through the Cross Market Operational Resilience Group (CMORG); in 2024 CMORG established an AI Taskforce developing scenarios on how malicious actors could use generative AI to enhance attacks, including circumventing established security and authentication controls at scale, to inform proactive mitigation, sector-wide collaboration and firm and sector exercises. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could be used to circumvent established security and authentication controls at scale"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Tracking the evolution of AI-related cyber risks will be an important part of the FPC's approach to monitoring AI (Section 3). (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Tracking the evolution of AI-related cyber risks will be an important part of the FPC’s approach"</sub>

## The FPC has a range information sources to help it track AI-related developments in the financial system…

- **descriptive / method**: The FPC is using quantitative and qualitative sources to monitor AI implications for financial stability, drawing on Bank, PRA and FCA work and existing mechanisms such as supervisory intelligence: the regular Bank and FCA AI Survey (current and expected AI usage across sectors and views on benefits and risks); the AI Consortium (a platform for public-private engagement on capabilities, development, deployment and use of AI in UK financial services); market intelligence; supervisory intelligence (across PRA and FCA regulated firms and Bank regulated FMIs); and regulatory and commercial data sources. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Artificial Intelligence Consortium is being established to provide a platform for public-private engagement"</sub>

## …and the FPC will continue to adapt and add to these tools in a flexible way as the risk environment evolves.

- **expected / expectation**: Because rapid, unforeseen shifts in AI's financial stability implications are plausible, the FPC's monitoring approach will need to be flexible and forward-looking; AI is hard to monitor because firms use it as an input across many internal processes whose impacts may not be apparent in information the FPC typically examines such as market data or resilience metrics. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FPC’s approach to monitoring AI risks will need to be flexible and forward-looking"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: In the next iteration of the AI Survey the FPC intends to work with the Bank and FCA to increase responses from currently underrepresented sectors, and will consider additional sources of information or data to understand specific risks, for example exploring system-wide implications of AI developments through future system-wide exercises. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to increase responses from currently underrepresented sectors"</sub>

## …and to follow developments that could lead it to identify additional emerging risks.

- **expected / expectation**: The FPC will continue to monitor AI technologies and capabilities beyond the financial system, as these can directly affect potential sources of financial stability risk, engaging with AI expertise from other public bodies such as the AI Security Institute (including on cyber risks) and drawing on academic and industry-led research. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FPC will engage with AI-related expertise from across other public bodies, such as the AI Security Institute"</sub>

## Stakeholder engagement will help ensure the FPC’s monitoring approach is welltargeted and incorporates relevant external sources of information.

- **descriptive / context**: The Bank will gather feedback on its planned monitoring approach through stakeholder engagement channels including supervisory engagement, AI Survey responses and the AI Consortium. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Bank will gather feedback on its planned monitoring approach through various stakeholder engagement channels"</sub>

## The FPC supports international initiatives to monitor and mitigate AI risks.

- **descriptive / context**: Because the global financial system and the AI market are highly interconnected and globalised, global developments at the intersection of financial services and AI could affect UK financial stability; the Bank, PRA and FCA engage with international work including the FSB's work on AI and financial stability (notably its 2024 report), the G7 and G20, and IMF and IOSCO work on AI in capital markets. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "global developments at the intersection of financial services and AI"</sub>

## Monitoring will allow the FPC to understand if any systemic risks develop and to ensure that any risk mitigations are calibrated appropriately to support the safe adoption of AI.

- **expected / expectation**: In the near term the Bank will focus on working with industry, including through the AI Consortium, to understand changing AI deployment and identify and share good practice for managing AI risks, and it will be important to understand where AI adoption has led to incidents or 'near misses'. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to understand the changing ways in which AI is being deployed, and to identify and share good practice"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Bank will be mindful of the potential need for regulators to evolve existing guidance and regulation; even where microprudential measures suffice for individual firms, macrofinancial vulnerabilities could indicate further macroprudential measures are needed, e.g. if AI significantly increases correlations or procyclical decision-making in markets, existing FPC work on market-based finance such as NBFI leverage may need adjusting, and AI market structure changes leading to greater reliance on common models or providers may require different oversight or response. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "even where microprudential measures are sufficient to mitigate risks to the safety and soundness of individual firms"</sub>

## Box A: AI adoption in principal trading firms and systematic hedge funds

- **descriptive / context**: Box A, informed by engagement with market contacts, discusses AI use by principal trading firms (PTFs), including high frequency trading firms, and particular systematic hedge funds, covering a range of AI models including more advanced 'black box' models such as neural networks; per the IMF, a rise in AI use for trading and investment and greater autonomy of AI-based decisions is expected. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a rise in the use of AI for trading and investment and a higher degree of autonomy of AI-based decisions is expected"</sub>

## A range of quantitative techniques – including AI models – are being used in some markets.

- **descriptive / finding or result**: PTFs and hedge funds have used quantitative techniques for decades, evolving from statistical techniques such as generalised linear models to AI models such as tree-based models or neural networks, which tend to be developed in-house; these techniques suit traditional prediction problems with a clear goal (e.g. predicting a security's future price) and abundant high-quality historical data, as in equities, foreign exchange and exchange-traded derivatives markets, and many trading strategies rest on price prediction models with added risk management and time horizon constraints. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These models tend to be developed in-house."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Current experimentation suggests no single technique generally outperforms another, even versus legacy strategies, across a range of tasks; over the longer term there may be greater adoption of neural networks, and currently where deployed they are largely implemented as rules-based systems with human-in-the-loop. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is generally no single technique that outperforms another, even in comparison to legacy strategies"</sub>

## Autonomous trading strategies are a future possibility, but there are current challenges to their development and deployment…

- **descriptive / limitation**: Firms are experimenting with complex models such as neural networks to develop trading strategies that operate autonomously with minimal human involvement, but many scenarios and factors must be considered (whether an asset is over or undervalued, when and how much to buy or sell, adapting to other participants' real-time behaviour, risk tolerance and diversification, and legal or regulatory requirements), so such models require significantly higher volumes of high-quality training data than other models. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these models require significantly higher volumes of high-quality training data than other types of models"</sub>

## …including in relation to data…

- **descriptive / limitation**: In many markets high-quality data is scarce, which can lead neural networks to perform in unexpected ways, particularly when exposed to market conditions unlike their training data such as different volatility regimes or market paradigms. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This scarcity of data can lead neural networks to perform in unexpected ways"</sub>

## …and unpredictable behaviour.

- **descriptive / limitation**: Deploying such models live in an unconstrained way could result in positions exposing the firm to substantial losses; risk management is harder because of the lack of interpretability of neural networks, as actions may be unpredictable and reasons not understood by human risk managers, and sufficiently autonomous models could act detrimentally to market stability or integrity, e.g. by ignoring guardrails such as market abuse regulations, which human managers would also need to manage. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "by ignoring regulatory or legal guardrails such as market abuse regulations"</sub>

## Box B: AI in the Bank of England

- **descriptive / context**: Box B says the Bank is embracing AI to enhance internal operations; data are at the heart of the AI transformation and relevant, findable, accessible and interpretable data supported by high-quality metadata is a critical foundation for all models; the Bank published a refreshed data and analytics strategy and is setting up a new Enterprise Data Platform on the Cloud. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supported by high-quality meta-data is a critical foundation for all models"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Generative AI changes how data is used by increasing model size and complexity, including all data types in training and real-time applications, and letting all staff interact with AI via natural language; this places added requirements on the Bank's data platform, broadens data management and governance, and adds a staff training dimension. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the possibility for all staff to interact with AI solutions through natural language"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Many internal generative AI uses focus on staff personal productivity via off-the-shelf AI assistants; internal trials showed significant productivity benefits, particularly in document summarisation, meeting notes and code generation, and the tools are being rolled out across the Bank. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Internal trials of some of these tools have shown significant productivity benefits"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Bespoke internal generative AI work includes enabling the PRA to use cloud AI technology to gain supervisory insights from large volumes of unstructured text documents, and a generative AI solution in the Bank's Agency network to help Agents extract insights collected at company visits; for financial stability work, established AI methods remain important for regulatory or market data analysis and generative AI can support tasks such as synthetic data production and summarising external reports with large language models. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "work is underway to enable the PRA to use the latest cloud AI technology"</sub>

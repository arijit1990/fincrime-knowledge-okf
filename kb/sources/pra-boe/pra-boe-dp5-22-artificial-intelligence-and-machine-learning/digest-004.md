## AI and reasonable steps

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 4.61: one of the areas that could benefit most from further discussion is what may constitute reasonable steps in an AI context and how, if at all, these differ from the reasonable steps SMFs are generally required to take. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "what may constitute reasonable steps in an AI context"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 4.62: PRA SS28/15, SS35/15 and DEPP 6.2, the key sources on the 'reasonable steps' criterion, have detailed but not exhaustive expectations on what may constitute reasonable steps and how to document and evidence them; the guidance was issued when autonomous decision-making technology such as AI was less widespread and so does not explicitly refer to it. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the guidance does not explicitly refer to such technology"</sub>

## Human-in-the-loop

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 4.64: the level of human involvement in the decision loop is an important element of an AI system; humans typically interact at design and training stages and may operate the system and interpret outputs, and the human element can safeguard against harmful outcomes by providing contextual knowledge a model may lack and identifying where an automated decision may be problematic and need further review. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The human element can act as a valuable safeguard against harmful outcomes"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 4.66: human involvement in automated decisions has limits, as some reviewers may be subject to 'automation bias' (simply accepting automated recommendations) or unable to interpret complex outputs and falsely reject an accurate output. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Some reviewers may be subject to ‘automation bias’ where they simply accept automated recommendations"</sub>

## Safety and soundness: Operational resilience, outsourcing, and third-party risk management – PRA and FCA

- **descriptive / definition**: Paragraph 4.67: since 2018 the supervisory authorities have developed a coordinated framework to strengthen operational resilience of UK financial services; operational resilience is the ability of firms and the sector to prevent, adapt to, respond to, recover from, and learn from operational disruptions. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "prevent, adapt to, respond to, recover from, and learn from operational disruptions"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 4.68: operational resilience applies to AI use by firms and FMIs when it supports an important business service, meaning they should set an impact tolerance for disruption for each important business service involving AI and ensure they can remain within impact tolerances in the event of a severe (for FMIs, extreme) but plausible disruption. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "firms and FMIs should set an impact tolerance for disruption for each of those important business services that involve AI"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 4.69: operational resilience principles may be a useful basis for managing certain AI risks, e.g. effective business continuity and contingency plans for AI systems supporting an important business service; as the AIPPF noted, considering backup and remediation actions before a model goes into production can enable timely response if model performance or output deteriorates beyond the accepted risk threshold. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "backup, and remediation actions being considered before the model is put into production"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 4.71: DP3/22 'Operational resilience: Critical third parties to the UK financial sector' explored measures to oversee and strengthen resilience of services from critical third parties (CTPs) that could complement, not replace, firms' and FMIs' existing expectations and requirements; the authorities note that certain third parties providing data and AI models could emerge as future potential CTPs given increasing use of these data and models. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "certain third parties providing data and AI models could emerge as future potential CTPs"</sub>

## Human-in-the-loop

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 4.65: consumers and others affected by automated decisions may feel uncomfortable where important decisions are taken without a human-in-the-loop. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may feel uncomfortable where important decisions are made without a human-in-the-loop"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 4.65: in certain contexts human involvement can be a regulatory requirement; the UK GDPR is given as an instance, since it treats automated decisions differently from human decisions. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In certain contexts human involvement can be a regulatory requirement, for instance under the UK GDPR"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Paragraph 4.65: Article 22 of the UK GDPR limits fully automated decisions that have legal or similarly significant effects on individuals to a narrower set of lawful bases and requires certain safeguards to be in place. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Article 22 of the UK GDPR restricts fully automated decisions which have legal or similarly significant effects"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 4.65: the ICO has explained that human input must be meaningful, and that a decision is not outside Article 22 merely because a human 'rubber-stamped' it. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a decision does not fall outside Article 22 just because a human has ‘rubber-stamped’ it"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 4.65: the UK government plans to reform these laws, including changing the right to human intervention, through the Data Protection and Digital Information Bill, which at the time was at second reading in the House of Commons. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The UK government is planning to reform these laws, including amending the right to human intervention"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 4.66 notes that human involvement in an automated decision has limits: some reviewers may suffer 'automation bias' and simply accept automated recommendations, or may be unable to interpret the outputs of complex systems and wrongly reject an accurate output. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there are limits to the effectiveness of human involvement in an automated decision"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 4.66: the authorities think that firms deploying AI systems need to have oversight and governance arrangements strong enough to make effective use of humans in the decision-making loop, and to review how accurate those arrangements are. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "firms deploying AI systems need to have a sufficiently strong set of oversight and governance arrangements"</sub>

## Executive summary

- **descriptive / context**: AI and ML are described as rapidly developing technologies with potential to make financial services and markets more efficient, accessible and tailored to consumer needs, but also able to pose novel challenges and create or amplify regulatory risks; the Bank, PRA and FCA therefore have a close interest in safe and responsible AI adoption in UK financial services. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "have a close interest in the safe and responsible adoption of AI in UK financial services"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The DP is part of a wider programme of AI work, including the AI Public Private Forum (final report February 2022), and sits within the wider policy debate including the UK government's 'Establishing a pro-innovation approach to regulating AI' policy paper, Digital Regulation Cooperation Forum (DRCF) joint working, and international developments such as the proposed EU AI regulation. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "including the AI Public Private Forum , the final report of which was published in February 2022"</sub>

## Benefits and risks related to the use of AI in financial services

- **descriptive / context**: The benefits, risks and harms discussed in the DP are neither exhaustive nor applicable to every AI use case. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The benefits, risks, and harms discussed in this DP are neither exhaustive nor applicable to every AI use case."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The primary drivers of AI risk in financial services relate to three lifecycle stages: data, models and governance; data-level risks can feed into the model level and then raise broader challenges for the firm and its overall governance of AI systems, and issues at each stage can lead to outcomes relevant to the supervisory authorities' remits. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The primary drivers of AI risk in financial services relate to three key stages of the AI lifecycle"</sub>

## Consumers

- **descriptive / finding or result**: AI may benefit consumers through better matching to products and services, enhanced ability to identify and support consumers with characteristics of vulnerability, and increased financial access; if misused it may lead to harmful targeting of behavioural biases or vulnerability, discriminatory decisions, financial exclusion and reduced trust. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "harmful targeting of consumers’ behavioural biases or characteristics of vulnerability, discriminatory decisions, financial exclusion, and reduced trust"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: AI may benefit competition by helping consumers access, assess and act on information, but may harm competition where used to implement or facilitate harmful strategic behaviour such as collusion, or to create or exacerbate market features such as barriers to entry or leveraging a dominant position. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "risks to competition may also arise where AI is used to implement or facilitate further harmful strategic behaviour"</sub>

## Firms

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Potential benefits for firms include enhanced data and analytical insights, increased revenue, operational efficiency and productivity, enhanced risk management and controls, and better combatting of fraud and money laundering; AI use can also translate into prudential risks to firms' safety and soundness that differ depending on how it is used. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "better combatting of fraud and money laundering"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: AI may benefit the broader financial system through more responsive pricing and more accurate decision-making, increasing allocative efficiency, but may also create risks to system resilience and efficiency; for example models may become correlated in subtle ways, adding to herding or procyclical behaviour at times of market stress. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "models may become correlated in subtle ways and add to risks of herding"</sub>

## How existing legal requirements and guidance apply to the use of AI

- **descriptive / context**: The supervisory authorities say they may need to intervene further to manage and mitigate AI risks and harms to consumers, firms and the stability and integrity of the UK financial system; it is important that the regulatory environment is proportionate and does not act as a barrier to beneficial innovation, and a first step is clarifying how existing legal requirements and guidance apply to AI. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A first step towards this ambition is clarifying how existing legal requirements and guidance apply to the use of AI."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The authorities identified 'cross-cutting' legal requirements and guidance covering multiple areas of risk, relevant mainly to the three lifecycle stages: data-related ones target data quality, privacy, infrastructure and governance; model-related ones for managing capital risks may safeguard model development, validation and review; governance-related ones (notably the SM&CR) focus on proper procedures, clear accountability and effective risk management across the AI lifecycle. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Governance-related legal requirements and guidance (including, notably, the SM&CR"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: AI industry standards or codes of conduct may potentially complement the regulatory system by helping firms build user trust that systems meet widely accepted industry norms, which may extend beyond minimum regulatory compliance. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI industry standards or codes of conduct may potentially complement the regulatory system"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The supervisory authorities encourage all relevant stakeholders to respond, including dual- and solo-regulated firms, non-regulated financial services firms, professional services firms, law firms, technology third parties, trade associations, standard setters, academics and civil society. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The supervisory authorities encourage all relevant stakeholders to respond to this DP."</sub>

## Discussion questions for stakeholder input

- **descriptive / context**: The DP asks whether existing sectoral legal requirements and guidance are sufficient to address AI risks and harms, where gaps may exist, and how additional intervention may support safe and responsible AI adoption; questions are in Chapter 5 and fall into three categories. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "we are inviting responses to the questions listed in Chapter 5"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The three question categories are: (1) supervisory authorities' objectives and remits, on the best approach to defining or scoping AI characteristics for legal requirements and guidance; (2) benefits and risks of AI, on which areas of benefit, risk and harm the authorities should prioritise; and (3) regulation, on whether current requirements are sufficient and which areas (i) would benefit from clarification for AI, (ii) could be extended to encompass AI, and (iii) could be a regulatory barrier to safe and responsible AI adoption. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "exploring the best approach to defining and/or scoping the characteristics of AI for the purposes of legal requirements and guidance"</sub>

## Background

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 1.5: a 2019 joint survey by the supervisory authorities found financial services firms were increasingly using AI and needed effective and evolving risk management controls to use it safely and harness its benefits. (p. 1)[^original]

## Box 1: UK government – establishing a pro-innovation approach to regulating AI

- **descriptive / context**: The July 2022 policy paper highlighted cross-cutting challenges in the regulatory landscape including lack of clarity, overlaps, inconsistencies and gaps, many of which may apply to UK financial services regulation; the UK government aims to create a framework for regulating AI that is context-specific, pro-innovation and risk-based, coherent, proportionate, and adaptable. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "including a lack of clarity, overlaps, inconsistencies, and gaps in the current approach"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The UK government's proposed approach rests on six cross-sectoral principles: ensure AI is used safely; ensure AI is technically secure and functions as designed; make sure AI is appropriately transparent and explainable; embed considerations of fairness into AI; define legal persons' responsibility for AI governance; and clarify routes to redress or contestability. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "clarify routes to redress or contestability"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: As next steps the UK government stated it will consider the roles, powers, remits and capabilities of regulators, the need for coordination, and how this should be delivered across statutory and non-statutory regulators involved in AI regulation; Table A in Appendix 1 maps the principles against DP chapters and sections. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the roles, powers, remits and capabilities of regulators, the need for coordination"</sub>

## 2. Supervisory authorities’ objectives and remits

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 2.2: the Bank's mission is to maintain monetary and financial stability in the UK and, subject to price stability, support government economic policy; it also supervises certain Financial Market Infrastructure firms, including central securities depositories, central counterparties, recognised payment systems operators (RPSOs) and 'specified' service providers to RPSOs. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Bank’s mission is to maintain monetary and financial stability in the UK"</sub>

## Why the supervisory authorities have an interest in AI

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 2.6: AI may bring benefits such as more cost-effective, efficient, accessible, consumer-tailored services but can pose novel challenges or amplify risks, so the authorities have a close interest in safe and responsible adoption and may need to intervene further to manage and mitigate AI risks and harms. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may need to intervene further to manage and mitigate the potential risks and harms related to AI applications"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 2.7: the supervisory authorities generally take a technology neutral approach to regulation but are aware that risks may relate to the use of specific technologies. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "generally take a technology neutral approach to regulation"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 2.8: the PRA and FCA have competition-related statutory objectives and must have regard to proportionality when exercising general functions including rule-making; they would wish to avoid unnecessarily complex or costly rules that act as a barrier to entry, especially for non-systemic firms, or unintentionally favour larger firms, and a proportionate approach is critical to safe and responsible adoption of AI. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "unnecessarily complex or costly rules that can act as a barrier to entry"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 2.9: firms using AI to process personal data may have obligations under the UK GDPR and Data Protection Act 2018, enforced by the ICO. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "firms may have regulatory obligations under the UK General Data Protection Regulation (UK GDPR)"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 2.9: financial services firms need to ensure compliance with the Equality Act 2010, which the EHRC can enforce; where firms use AI in decision-making they will need to ensure it does not result in unlawful discrimination based on protected characteristics. (p. 1)[^original]

## Box 2: What do we mean by technology neutral?

- **descriptive / context**: The supervisory authorities recognise new technologies such as AI may benefit consumers through new products, enhance firms' safety and soundness through improved risk management informed by advanced analytics, and deliver a more efficient financial system. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may benefit consumers through new and better products and services"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Being technology-neutral means the supervisory authorities generally adopt an approach in which the core principles, rules and regulations do not usually mandate or prohibit specific technologies. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the core principles, rules, and regulations do not usually mandate or prohibit specific technologies"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The supervisory authorities nevertheless monitor and mitigate technology risks because they can adversely affect their objectives (consumer protection, competition, safety and soundness, market integrity, financial stability), and a certain technology may affect the risks of firms' activities. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the supervisory authorities monitor and mitigate technology risks as they can equally have adverse implications"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The supervisory authorities may issue technology-specific rules and guidance where appropriate, and where a technology raises novel challenges making it hard for firms to understand how existing rules apply, they may issue guidance or use other policy tools to clarify how existing rules and expectations apply; examples are FG16/5 on outsourcing to the 'cloud' and other third-party IT services and paragraph 2.4 of PRA SS2/21 'Outsourcing and third party risk management'. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may therefore issue technology-specific rules and guidance where appropriate"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Firms could, for example, also consider outcomes and risk management as covered in the FCA's 'Implementing Technology Change' paper. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Firms could, for example, also consider outcomes and risk management as covered"</sub>

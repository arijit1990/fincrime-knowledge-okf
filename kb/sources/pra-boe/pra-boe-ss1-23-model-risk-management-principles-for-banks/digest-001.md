## 1. Introduction

- **descriptive / definition**: SS1/23 paragraph 1.3: 'firms' means UK banks, building societies and PRA-designated investment firms with internal model approval. The statement aims to support firms in strengthening policies, procedures and practices to identify, manage and control risks from the use of all models, whether developed in-house or externally (including vendor models) and including models used for financial reporting. (p. 3)[^original]

## The use of models

- **descriptive / context**: SS1/23 paragraph 2.1: firms' use of models covers business decision making, risk management and reporting. 'Business decisions' means all decisions on general business and operational banking activities, strategic decisions, financial, risk, capital and liquidity measurement and reporting, and any other decisions relevant to safety and soundness. Increasing reliance on models and scenario analysis and increasingly sophisticated techniques highlight the need for sound model governance and effective MRM; inadequate or flawed design and implementation, and inappropriate use, could have adverse consequences. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Inadequate or flawed design and implementation, and inappropriate use of models could lead to adverse consequences."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 4: model use is defined as using a model's output as a basis for informing business decisions. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Model use is defined here as using a model’s output as a basis for informing business decisions."</sub>

## Quantitative methods and models

- **descriptive / context**: SS1/23 paragraph 2.2: a wide variety of quantitative calculation methods, systems, approaches, end-user computing (EUC) applications and calculators (collectively 'quantitative methods') are used in daily operations to support decisions. Good risk management practice involves testing that quantitative methods supporting business decisions are correctly implemented and used. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Good risk management practices involve quantitative methods that support business decisions being tested for correct implementation and use."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: SS1/23 paragraph 2.3: models are a subset of quantitative methods. Their outputs are estimates, forecasts, predictions or projections, which may be inputs to other methods or models. Model outputs are inherently uncertain because models are imperfect representations of real-world phenomena, simplifications of complex systems (often intentionally) and based on a limited set of observations. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Model outputs are inherently uncertain, because they are imperfect representations of real-world phenomena"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: SS1/23 paragraph 2.3: in addition to testing for correct implementation, good risk management practice for models involves (i) verifying applicability to the decisions they support and (ii) assessing the validity of underlying model assumptions in the business context of the decisions being assessed. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the validity of the underlying model assumptions in the business context of the decisions being assessed"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: SS1/23 paragraph 2.4: for the statement's expectations, a model is a quantitative method that applies statistical, economic, financial or mathematical theories, techniques and assumptions to process input data into output. Inputs can be quantitative, qualitative or expert judgement-based, and outputs quantitative or qualitative. A working definition brings consistency and clarity for firms implementing MRM frameworks. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a model is defined as a quantitative method that applies statistical, economic, financial, or mathematical theories"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: SS1/23 paragraph 2.5: advances in technology and data processing power increasingly let deterministic quantitative methods such as decision-based rules or algorithms become vastly more complex and statistically orientated, which would typically entail challenge to their applicability in supporting important business decisions. Understanding the potential impact of models and complex quantitative methods on business and safety and soundness is therefore equally important. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "deterministic quantitative methods such as decision-based rules or algorithms to become vastly more complex and statistically orientated"</sub>

## Model risk

- **descriptive / definition**: SS1/23 paragraph 2.6: model risk is the potential for adverse consequences from model errors or the inappropriate use of modelled outputs to inform business decisions. Consequences could include a deterioration in the prudential position, non-compliance with laws or regulations, or reputational damage, as well as financial loss and qualitative limitations such as restrictions on business activities. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Model risk is the potential for adverse consequences from model errors or the inappropriate use of modelled outputs"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: SS1/23 paragraph 2.7: model outputs may be affected by the choice and suitability of methodology, the quality and relevance of data inputs, the integrity of implementation and the ongoing scope of applicability. Continued suitability may also be affected by changes in the validity of assumptions supporting the use case (for example encoded macroeconomic assumptions, or assumed continued relationships between variables in historical data) or by inappropriate use. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the quality and relevance of the data inputs, and the integrity of implementation and ongoing scope of applicability of a model"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: SS1/23 paragraph 2.8: individual model risk rises with model complexity; the PRA anticipates higher model risk for more complex models that are hard to understand or explain in non-technical terms, or whose output is hard to foresee from the input. Greater uncertainty over inputs and construct (for example complex data structures, low quality or unstructured data) would also raise model risk, including where results and findings cannot easily be repeated or reproduced. Overall (aggregate) model risk rises with more inter-related models and more interconnected data structures and data sources. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Individual model risk increases with model complexity."</sub>

## Model risk management and the model lifecycle

- **descriptive / definition**: SS1/23 paragraph 2.10: the model lifecycle has three main processes: (i) the core modelling process (development, implementation and use); (ii) model validation, activities verifying models perform as expected, comprising (a) review of suitability and conceptual soundness (independent review), (b) verification of integrity of implementation (process verification) and (c) ongoing testing that the model continues to perform as intended (model performance monitoring); and (iii) model risk controls, being processes and procedures other than validation that help manage, control or mitigate model risk. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "ongoing testing to confirm that the model continues to perform as intended (model performance monitoring)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: SS1/23 paragraph 2.11: the sequence of modelling-validation-control activities describes the stages in a model's lifecycle. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The sequence of modelling-validation-control activities describes the various stages in a model’s lifecycle."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: SS1/23 paragraph 2.9: an effective MRM framework can reduce or mitigate model risk but cannot remove it entirely. Effective MRM begins with a comprehensive governance and oversight framework that is backed by effective model lifecycle management. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Model risk can be reduced or mitigated, but not entirely eliminated, through an effective MRM framework."</sub>

## Organisational structures, validation, and control functions

- **descriptive / context**: SS1/23 paragraph 2.12: roles and responsibilities for the three lifecycle processes are firm-specific and depend on business model and business-line structure. Usually the model owner is the individual accountable for a model's development, implementation and use and for ensuring performance is within expectation (and may also be developer or user). (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The model owner is the individual accountable for a model's development, implementation and use"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: SS1/23 paragraph 2.12: model users rely on model outputs as a basis for business decisions; they typically identify the economic or business rationale for a new model or for changing an existing one and may be involved in early development and ongoing monitoring. Model developers are the team or individuals responsible for designing, developing, evaluating (testing) and documenting models. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Model developer(s) is/are the team or individual(s) responsible for designing, developing, evaluating (testing), and documenting models."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: SS1/23 paragraph 2.13: large firms often set up a designated model risk (MRM) function within risk management or compliance, which may be separate from model validation and is usually responsible for creating and maintaining the MRM framework and risk controls. Without such a function, these responsibilities are usually assigned to individuals and/or model risk committees. The effectiveness of MRM control functions is affected by their stature and authority, for example to restrict use of models, recommend conditional approval or temporarily grant exceptions to validation or approval. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the effectiveness of MRM control functions is affected by their stature and authority"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: SS1/23 paragraph 2.14: the validation function's primary responsibility is usually an objective and unbiased opinion on a model's adequacy and soundness for a use case. Validators should therefore not be part of any model development activities, nor have a stake in whether a model is approved. Impartiality can be achieved in various ways, for example separate reporting lines and incentive structures from developers, or an independent party reviewing test results to confirm the objectivity of the independent review finding. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Validators should therefore not be part of any model development activities, nor have a stake in whether or not a model is approved."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: SS1/23 paragraph 2.15: regardless of model type, risk type or organisational structure, effective MRM is underpinned by strong governance and effective model lifecycle management, consisting of robust modelling, validation and risk control processes. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "effective MRM practices are underpinned by strong governance and effective model lifecycle management"</sub>

## The MRM principles

- **expected / expectation**: SS1/23 paragraph 3.1: the board of directors and senior management are ultimately responsible for establishing a sound MRM framework so that key business decisions relevant to safety and soundness are supported by sound and appropriate model output and consistent with the board's defined model risk appetite. Scope and depth may vary across firms, but core principles are fundamental and form the basis of the statement's expectations. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "ultimately responsible for establishing a sound MRM framework to ensure key business decisions relevant to a firm’s safety and soundness"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: SS1/23 Principle 1 (Model identification and model risk classification): firms should have an established definition of a model that sets the scope for MRM, together with a model inventory and a risk-based tiering approach for categorising models, to help identify and manage model risk. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Firms should have an established definition of a model that sets the scope for MRM, a model inventory"</sub>

## Principle 2 – Governance

- **expected / expectation**: Principle 2 (Governance): firms should have strong governance oversight with a board that promotes an MRM culture from the top by setting a clear model risk appetite. The board should approve the MRM policy and appoint an accountable individual to take responsibility for implementing a sound MRM framework that ensures effective MRM practices. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The board should approve the MRM policy and appoint an accountable individual"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The following high-level principle in the excerpt (heading not shown) states that firms should have a validation process providing ongoing, independent and effective challenge to model development and use. The individual or body responsible for approving a model should ensure validation recommendations for remediation or redevelopment are actioned so models are suitable for their intended purpose. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "ongoing, independent, and effective challenge to model development and use"</sub>

## Principle 5 – Model risk mitigants

- **expected / expectation**: Principle 5 (Model risk mitigants): firms should have established policies and procedures for the use of model risk mitigants when models are under-performing, and procedures for the independent review of post-model adjustments. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should have established policies and procedures for the use of model risk mitigants when models are under-performing"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: SS1/23 paragraph 3.2: the MRM principles are supported by sub-principles covering all elements of the model lifecycle. The PRA expects firms to meet the high-level principles as well as the individual sub-principles in the 'Model risk management principles for banks' section of the statement. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The PRA expects firms to meet the high-level model risk management principles"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: SS1/23 paragraph 3.3: the principles represent core risk management practices for all models and risk types, and their practical application should be commensurate with firm size, business activities and the complexity and extent of model use. For example, firms with fewer or less complex models should find maintaining a model inventory less burdensome, and tiering criteria can be materially simpler than for firms with more or more complex models. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should be commensurate with their size, business activities, and the complexity and extent of their model use"</sub>

## Proportionality

- **expected / expectation**: SS1/23 paragraph 3.4: the framework should also be applied proportionately within each firm. The rigour, intensity, prioritisation and frequency of model validation, risk controls, independent review, performance monitoring and re-validation are expected to be commensurate with the model tier assigned to a model. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "are expected to be commensurate with the associated model tier assigned to a model"</sub>

## Principle 2 – Governance

- **expected / expectation**: A further principle statement appearing under the Principle 2 heading in the excerpt (its own heading is not shown): firms should have a robust model development process with standards covering model design and implementation, model selection and model performance measurement; data, model construct, assumptions and model outcomes should be tested regularly so that model limitations and weaknesses are identified, monitored, recorded and remediated. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Firms should have a robust model development process with standards for model design and implementation"</sub>

## Proportionality

- **descriptive / context**: SS1/23 paragraph 3.5: the PRA's view is that active involvement of senior management and the board in firms' MRM governance processes is key to robust and effective MRM; strengthening the accountability of firms and individuals for managing model risk should increase senior management and board engagement and participation, which in turn will drive successful MRM implementation. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "active senior management and board involvement in firms’ MRM governance processes are key"</sub>

## SMF accountability for model risk management framework

- **expected / expectation**: SS1/23 paragraph 3.6: as part of Principle 2, the PRA expects firms to identify and allocate responsibility for the MRM framework to the relevant SMF(s) most appropriate to the organisational structure and risk profile. Firms should ensure the SMF(s)' Statement of Responsibilities is updated to reflect this. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Firms should ensure the responsibilities in the SMF(s)’ Statement of Responsibilities are updated to reflect this."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: SS1/23 paragraph 3.7: the expectations also apply to models used for financial reporting. The PRA considers MRM effectiveness for financial reporting relevant to the auditor's assessment of, and response to, the risk of material misstatement in the statutory audit, including its understanding of the firm's processes for monitoring the effectiveness of internal controls and its control activities. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The expectations in this SS are also relevant to models used for financial reporting purposes."</sub>

## Financial reporting and external auditors

- **expected / expectation**: SS1/23 paragraph 3.8: the PRA expects firms to ensure a report on the effectiveness of MRM for financial reporting is available to their audit committee regularly and at least annually, and available on a timely basis to inform the external auditor's assessment of, and response to, the risk of material misstatement in the statutory audit. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "available to their audit committee on a regular basis, and at least annually"</sub>

## 1. What is Sanctions Screening?

- **descriptive / context**: Screening lists are typically derived from regulatory sources and often supplied, updated and maintained through external vendors; FIs may also augment these with lists of sanctions relevant terms, names or phrases identified through their own operations, research or intelligence. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs may also augment these with lists of sanctions relevant terms, names or phrases"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Determining what constitutes a "true match" can be complex across variables such as alphabets, languages, cultures, spelling, abbreviations, acronyms and aliases; automated screening introduces further complexities such as "fuzzy matching" algorithms, workflows and match rules. (p. 1)[^original]

## 2. A Programmatic Approach to Sanctions Screening

- **expected / expectation**: Screening as a control is not sanctions specific and should be deployed as part of an integrated risk based FCC programme. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should be deployed as part of an integrated risk based FCC programme"</sub>

## 2.1 Sanctions Screening Programme

- **descriptive / expectation**: Policies and Procedures pillar: these define requirements for what must be screened, in what context and at which frequency, and how alerts should be adjudicated, paying particular attention to resolving alerts where information is unavailable, incomplete or potentially unreliable. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "defining requirements for what must be screened, in what context and at which frequency"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: Responsible Person pillar: ensuring appropriate skills and experience in understanding the nuances of often arcane sanctions requirements and how they might influence screening outcomes and decisions, as well as the technical capabilities of screening software. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "ensuring appropriate skills and experience in understanding the nuances of often arcane sanctions requirements"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Internal Controls pillar: implementing screening controls requires an understanding of the methodologies and technologies available and their operational consequences; there is no clearly defined approach to technology or configuration that is better or worse, each has strengths and limitations, and understanding those is critical. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There is no clearly defined approach to technology or configuration that is better or worse"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: FIs are expected to document how their screening systems are configured, to demonstrate the configuration is reasonably expected to detect and manage the specific sanctions risks to which the FI is exposed and to ensure transparency of any system limitations or risk based decisions that the screening controls are not designed to detect. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs are expected to document how their screening systems are configured"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Testing pillar: testing is conducted to validate that the screening system performs as expected and to assess its effectiveness in managing the specific risks in the FI's Risk Assessment; regular testing of the system should be supported by metrics, analysis and reporting. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Regular testing of the system should be supported by metrics, analysis and reporting"</sub>

## 2.2 Applying a Risk Based Approach

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The effectiveness of screening as a control varies between FIs, even where FIs use the same third-party screening solution, and screening is not necessarily appropriate for all products and services. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the effectiveness of screening as a control will vary between FIs, even where FIs are using"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Core principle 1: articulate the specific sanctions risk the FI is trying to prevent or detect within its products, services and operations. For example, a global FI may prohibit dealing with any party sanctioned by the U.S., U.N., E.U., its home country and core operating jurisdictions, while a smaller FI operating in one country may limit its policy to the sanctions laws of that sole jurisdiction. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Articulate the specific sanctions risk the FI is trying to prevent or detect within its products, services and operations"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Core principle 2: identify and evaluate inherent potential sanctions risk exposure from products, services and customer relationships. For example, screening may be more meaningful for cross-border payments between a wide range of parties than for payments within one jurisdiction where all account holders must comply with that jurisdiction's sanctions and KYC requirements, where KYC, on-boarding and regulatory requirements are known and consistent, lessening the incremental value of transaction screening. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "lessening the incremental value of transaction screening as a control"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Core principle 3: a well-documented understanding of the risks and how they are managed through set-up and calibration of the screening tool. For list based sanctions programmes the red flag is the presence of the sanctioned party's name, readily detectable through screening; for certain Sectoral Sanctions programmes only a defined subset of activities is prohibited, so screening payments for targeted parties will not detect the sectoral risk without further information about the underlying activity and may not be appropriate or effective. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "only a defined subset of activities is prohibited, and screening payments for targeted parties will"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Core principle 4: assess where, within the FI, information is available in a format conducive to screening (e.g. transactions solely containing ISINs). Where information is insufficient to assess an alert and distinguish a true from a false match, the FI may need to consider alternative controls or adopt new business processes. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Assess where, within the FI, the information is available in a format conducive to screening"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: If an FI decides not to screen a category of information because it, while in a format conducive to screening, is not sufficiently actionable to manage sanctions risk, the FI should implement alternative controls to identify and manage the sanctions risk. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FI should implement alternative controls to identify and manage the sanctions risk"</sub>

## 3. Screening Technology and Generating Productive Alerts

- **descriptive / context**: What is often thought of as simple name-matching can be a complex set of processes in which data from several often disparate systems and sanctions lists is compared using matching algorithms and risk based alert creation rules intended to ensure compliance with multiple regulatory regimes. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "What is often thought of as a simple name-matching process can be a complex set of processes"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A robust operating model employs expertise from IT, Operations and FCC working together to ensure appropriate alert generation and adjudication. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A robust operating model employs expertise from IT, Operations and FCC working together"</sub>

## 3.1 Principles for Generating Productive Alerts

- **expected / expectation**: Identifying and implementing risk based screening decisions, to maximise alert quality and minimise low quality or irrelevant alerts, should be undertaken prior to deploying a new screening system and thereafter on an on-going basis. (p. 1)[^original]

- **permissive / method**: Risk based decisions may include Suppression: use of suppression rules or "Good Guys" lists to manage common false positive alerts requiring unnecessary manual review. (p. 1)[^original]

## 4.1 What is Reference Data Screening?

- **descriptive / definition**: Reference data screening is the process of screening the information an FI collects and maintains on the parties it does business with, or on specific types of products and services it offers; though often called "name" or "customer" screening, it encompasses any data set within the FI's operations, separate from transactional records, that may present a relevant sanctions risk indicator and be conducive to periodic screening. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Reference data screening is the process of screening the information an FI collects and maintains"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: The most common types of reference data relevant for sanctions screening are: customers, including all parties whose identity is collected to meet KYC and CDD standards (such as beneficial owners and related or connected parties); employee data; third-party service providers (e.g. vendors, landlords of FI-occupied premises, tenants of FI-owned premises); ISINs or other sanctions-relevant identifying features of assets held in custody; and recipients of the FI's corporate donations or sponsorship. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Recipients of the FI’s corporate donations or sponsorship"</sub>

## 4.2 Determining Sanctions Relevant Attributes in Reference Data

- **descriptive / data or feature**: Names of individuals and entities with whom the FI has a relationship are relevant for screening against name based sanctions lists but not for geographically based sanctions programmes; the address data elements (most commonly cities and countries) are relevant for geographic programmes and also as identifiers in name based, list based programmes to differentiate a true name match from a false one. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they are not relevant for geographically based sanctions programmes"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: An FI should also define other data elements that may be relevant in some situations and not others; date of birth, for example, is a distinguishing factor to assess a true from a false match on an individual and might be used in combination with another attribute such as name. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An FI should also define other data elements that may be relevant for sanctions screening"</sub>

## 4.3 Manner, Timing and Frequency of Sanctions Screening

- **descriptive / context**: An FI's reference data is typically maintained in electronic files and is most effective when screened through an automated process repeated at defined intervals. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is most effective when screened through an automated process and repeated at defined intervals"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: An FI's policies and procedures should clearly define when reference data screening takes place. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An FI’s policies and procedures should clearly define when reference data screening takes place"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: As a general principle, screening should be done when establishing a new relationship, to ensure it is permissible, and then at regular intervals, either upon a trigger event or as customer and/or list information changes, to validate that relationships remain permissible. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "screening should be done when establishing a new relationship, to ensure the relationship is permissible"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: Where internal or external data sets change frequently, periodic screening may be as often as daily, but longer intervals between periodic rescreening may be acceptable where change is less frequent or the risk of potential sanctions exposure is low. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "periodic screening may be as often as daily, but longer intervals between periodic rescreening may be acceptable"</sub>

## 5. Transactions/Message Screening

- **descriptive / definition**: Transaction screening refers to screening a movement of value within the FI's records, including funds, goods or assets, between parties or accounts. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Transaction screening refers to the process of screening a movement of value within the FI’s records"</sub>

## 5.1 Transaction Screening, including Payments and Trade

- **expected / expectation**: To determine the scope of transaction screening, an FI should focus on those transactional records necessary to the movement of value between parties and at a point where detection of a sanctions risk is actionable to prevent a violation; consideration should be given to higher risk factors such as cross-border transactions, the currency used, and the routing of the transaction. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "at a point in the transaction where detection of a sanctions risk is actionable to prevent a violation"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Screening cross-border payments prior to completing the transaction, known as screening in real-time, is common practice. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Screening cross-border payments prior to completing the transaction is common practice and known as screening in real-time"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Real-time screening of domestic payments may be unnecessary for FIs subject to the same local regulatory requirements, including local sanctions and KYC requirements at client on-boarding, since screening each transaction is likely to be duplicative and less likely to identify new risk indicators; an FI also subject to a different jurisdiction and regulatory mandate would likely want to assess its requirements and decide to screen to address that risk. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "screening domestic payments in real-time may be unnecessary for FIs that are subject to the same local regulatory requirements"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: An FI may decide to screen a defined set of transactions where it assesses the sanctions risks within the local economy or financial system to be outside its own risk tolerance. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to be outside of its own risk tolerance"</sub>

## 5.2 Data Elements within Transactions

- **expected / expectation**: An FI should initially assess which transaction types are relevant for sanctions screening and then, as with reference data, identify which attributes within those records are relevant and the context in which they become relevant. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An FI should initially assess which transaction types are relevant for sanctions screening"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Names of parties involved in a transaction are relevant for list based sanctions programmes, whereas addresses are more relevant to geographical sanctions programmes and can help distinguish a true from a false match; other data elements such as bank identification codes may be relevant for both list and geographically based programmes. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Names of parties involved in the transaction are relevant for list based sanctions programmes"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Some data elements are more relevant in combination with other attributes; detecting sectoral sanctions risk typically requires detecting multiple factors, such as both targeted parties and prohibited activities, and many controls may not be capable of detecting both simultaneously and therefore may not be effective. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "detection of sectoral sanctions risk typically requires detection of multiple factors"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Certain data elements offer little or no risk mitigation through screening; amounts, dates and transaction reference numbers have no relevance from a screening perspective. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "amounts, dates and transaction reference numbers have no relevance from a screening perspective"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Common transactional attributes screened include: the parties to a transaction including remitter and beneficiary; agents, intermediaries and FIs; vessels, including IMO numbers, normally in Trade Finance transactions; bank names, BICs and other routing codes; free text fields such as payment reference information or the stated purpose in Field 70 of a SWIFT message; and ISINs or other risk relevant product identifiers, including those relating to Sectoral Sanctions Identifications within securities transactions. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Free text fields, such as payment reference information or the stated purpose of the payment"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Trade finance documentation attributes commonly screened include the importer and exporter, manufacturer, drawee, drawer, notify party, signatories; shipping companies and freight forwarders; facilitators such as insurance companies, agents and brokers; and FIs including issuing, advising, confirming, negotiating, claiming, collecting, reimbursing and guarantor banks.   <sub>anchor: "Trade finance documentation, including the: Importer and exporter, manufacturer, drawee, drawer, notify party, signatories"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Geography attributes commonly screened include addresses, countries, cities, towns, regions, ports and airports, such as within SWIFT Fields 50 and 59; place of taking in charge, receipt, dispatch, delivery and final destination; country of origin of goods/services, destination and transhipment; and airport of departure/destination. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Within SWIFT Fields 50 and 59 Place of taking in Charge"</sub>

## Introduction

- **expected / expectation**: Sanctions screening should sit within an effective Financial Crime Compliance (FCC) programme. It helps FIs identify sanctioned individuals and organisations and the illegal activity they could be exposed to, flags areas of possible sanctions concern, and supports risk decisions that are appropriately compliant. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Screening should be undertaken as part of an effective Financial Crime Compliance (FCC) programme"</sub>
- **expected / limitation**: Transaction screening and customer screening are meant together to give a robust set of controls for finding sanctions targets. It should be recognised that managing these controls has a number of limitations, so they should always be used as one part of a wider FCC programme. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there are a number of limitations in the way in which these controls are managed"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Like any financial crime risk, sanctions risk should first be identified and assessed by the FI, which should then put in place a screening programme that fits its nature, size and complexity. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an FI should first identify and assess the sanctions risks to which it is exposed"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The guidance does not suggest that every FI should apply every element at the same level. It seeks to show where screening can be an effective part of a wider sanctions compliance programme, where it is limited as a control, and where a risk based approach is needed despite sanctions compliance being a matter of strict liability. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "notwithstanding the strict liability nature of sanctions compliance"</sub>

## 1. What is Sanctions Screening?

- **descriptive / definition**: Sanctions screening is a control for detecting, preventing and disrupting financial crime, especially sanctions risk. It works by comparing one string of text with another to spot similarities that point to a possible match, checking data from the FI's operations, such as customer and transaction records, against lists of names and other indicators of sanctioned parties or locations. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is the comparison of one string of text against another to detect similarities which would suggest a possible match"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: An alert produced by screening does not on its own show that there is sanctions risk. It is only a first step towards detecting possible exposure, and additional information is needed to confirm or discount it by judging whether the textual similarities amount to a true sanctions match. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is not, by itself, an indication of sanctions risk"</sub>

## 2.1 Sanctions Screening Programme

- **expected / expectation**: The core pillars of an FCC programme, and its key enabling functions, should be applied to screening together with the FI's other financial crime risk prevention and control processes rather than on their own. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should be applied to screening, not in isolation, but in conjunction with other financial crime risk prevention and control processes"</sub>

## 2.2 Applying a Risk Based Approach

- **descriptive / limitation**: Screening cannot catch every sanctions risk, because a piece of text can be altered in so many ways and still mean the same thing. How well it works depends on the type, availability, completeness and quality of data and on the inherent sanctions risks of the FI, its products, customers and services. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Sanctions screening can never detect every possible sanctions risk"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Because screening needs a programmatic approach, each FI must evaluate its own risks to decide how, how far and in what circumstances it uses screening. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "each FI must assess its own risks in order to define the manner, extent and circumstances"</sub>

## 3. Screening Technology and Generating Productive Alerts

- **expected / expectation**: For larger or more complex FIs there is an expectation that the screening programme will need a technology application with core functions: applying risk based screening rules, producing good quality alerts for review, giving relevant metrics and reporting, ensuring data integrity, and enabling independent testing and validation. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is an expectation that the screening programme will require the use of a technology application"</sub>

## 3.4 Independent Testing /Validation

- **permissive / context**: Independent testing can be done by qualified teams with the right technology expertise in internal audit, by an independent group in the FI's compliance division, by a third-party vendor hired for the purpose, or by a mix of these. The screening application may also be put forward to be treated as a model and, if so treated, be subject to the related governance framework. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Independent testing may be carried out by qualified teams with appropriate technology expertise in internal audit"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: As a minimum, testing results should be reviewed by the FI team with primary responsibility for sanctions compliance, and that team should decide whether accepting the risk or remediating is the right response to any relevant findings. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "reviewed at a minimum by the team within the FI with primary responsibility for sanctions compliance"</sub>

## 3.6 Internal Technology Build or Vendor Selection

- **mandatory / requirement**: An FI has to either build its screening application in-house or buy it from a vendor. Since every FI's size, geographic presence and business and technology environment is different, the choice must come from an analysis of its identified sanctions risks and functional requirements. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this determination must be derived from an analysis of identified sanctions risks and functional requirements"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: On the functional side, consideration should be given to how much data will be screened, whether to support several local installations or a single central one, whether data integrity processes exist or are supported, and how well the application can fit into the FI's technology infrastructure. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "consideration should be given to the volume of data to be screened"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: After identifying its risk and functional requirements, an FI should strike a balance between the standard functions and configurability of a bought solution and the cost of building and maintaining a more bespoke application in-house. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an FI should achieve a balance between the standard vendor functionality and configurability of a purchased solution"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: It is critical to understand whether the FI or chosen vendor has, and will keep having, enough compliance and technology expertise and resources to support design, build and/or implementation, while staying well informed about new sanctions risks arising from changing regulatory frameworks or business expansion and strategy. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is critical to understand whether sufficient compliance and technology expertise and resources exist within the FI or chosen vendor"</sub>

## 4.2 Determining Sanctions Relevant Attributes in Reference Data

- **expected / expectation**: Not every data element in an FI's records matters for sanctions screening. In deciding what reference data to screen, the FI should pick out the data that bears on sanctions risk, work out how it is relevant, and make sure it suits effective screening. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an FI should identify and differentiate the data within its operations and records that are relevant to sanctions risks"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: For each data element, FIs should compare its incremental value in screening with the reliability of the data, and ask whether an alert on it would meaningfully help detect or prevent a sanctions risk that other controls, or screening other attributes, would not reasonably catch. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs should weigh up the relative incremental value of screening the data element against the reliability of the data"</sub>

## 4.3 Manner, Timing and Frequency of Sanctions Screening

- **permissive / recommendation**: Manual screening can be considered where risk is low enough and where reference data cannot be reliably obtained electronically or in the format automated screening needs. For example, an FI with only a few names to screen may decide not to invest in an automated system and instead key the names into an online screening filter. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The use of manual screening can be considered when the risk is sufficiently low"</sub>

## 6. List Management

- **expected / expectation**: Each of the list management considerations (list selection, sourcing, maintenance, data enhancement, whitelisting, geographic scope, exact versus fuzzy matching and frequency of screening) should be well documented and reviewed regularly, so that the FI's chosen approach stays consistent with its risk appetite. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "each should be well-documented and reviewed on a regular basis"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: FIs should consider what effect bringing in new lists and terms, which could produce large alert volumes or spikes, may have on operational risk. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs should consider the impact that the introduction of new lists and terms"</sub>
- **expected / method**: Whitelisting, as a list management consideration, means deciding how to manage rules that automatically remove potential hits arising from certain list terms meeting commonly seen data, for instance customer names already confirmed as false positives. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "determine the management of rules for automatically eliminating potential hits"</sub>
- **expected / method**: On 'exact matching' versus 'fuzzy logic', the FI decides which lists are applied in the screening filter on an exact match basis and which use fuzzy matching. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "determine which lists should be deployed within the screening filter on an exact match basis"</sub>

## 6.1 Regulatory Sanctions Lists

- **recommended / recommendation**: FIs should consider how to ensure the quality and timeliness of updates to the lists they screen against, including delays between regulatory list updates and updates supplied by the vendor, and enrichment of listed terms, such as foreign language name variations or BIC codes added for listed FIs. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs should consider the means to ensure the quality and timeliness of updates made to the lists they screen against"</sub>

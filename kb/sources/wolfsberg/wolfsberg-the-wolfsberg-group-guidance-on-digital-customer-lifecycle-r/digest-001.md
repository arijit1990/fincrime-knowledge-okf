## The Wolfsberg Group - Guidance on Digital Customer Lifecycle Risk Management

- **descriptive / context**: Customers and prospective customers increasingly expect to engage their FI and become customers via a digital device; the challenge for the FI is how to manage the financial crime risks of non-face-to-face digital engagement effectively. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "how to manage the financial crime risks associated with non-face-to-face digital engagement effectively"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Competent authorities increasingly recognise that, with appropriate controls in place, non-face-to-face customer engagement may be a standard, or even lower-risk, engagement channel. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "with the appropriate controls in place, non-face-to-face customer engagement may be a standard, or even lower-risk engagement channel"</sub>

## 2. Background

- **descriptive / context**: The FATF Guidance on Digital Identity recognised that FIs and government authorities are at an "inflection point" regarding innovation, and that with appropriate controls non-face-to-face engagement may be a standard or even lower-risk channel and can be a powerful enabler for financial inclusion. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs and government authorities are at an “inflection point” as regards innovation"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: An FI should not see digital customer lifecycle risk management as a competition between physically present and non-physically-present means of customer engagement, but as recognition that advances in technology have lessened the traditional importance of a "brick and mortar" office visit for verifying and authenticating a prospective customer's identity and underlying information. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an FI should not see digital customer lifecycle risk management as a competition between physically present and non-physically-present means"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Maintaining an accurate profile of a customer's risk for facilitating financial crime is less and less reliant on face-to-face interaction, and increasingly less dependent on customer contact in general. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "increasingly less dependent on customer contact in general"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The guiding principle for developing a digital customer lifecycle risk management programme is the same as for a traditional programme: building and maintaining, to a reasonable degree, an accurate profile of customer risk. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "building and maintaining, to a reasonable degree, an accurate profile of customer risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Accuracy here refers to the level of confidence that the information an FI collects is correct and up-to-date (akin to the FATF's "an appropriate level of trustworthiness"), and that the FI's evaluation of it lets the FI better predict and respond to the likelihood of a customer engaging in illicit activity. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the level of confidence that the information an FI collects is correct and up-to-date"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Historically a face-to-face meeting was an important reference point at account opening, a form of human authentication in which a trained employee would confirm documents as genuine, match the photo on a government ID to the individual present, and implicitly confirm proof of life (now called "liveness detection" for non-face-to-face channels). (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "match a photo of a face on a government ID to the individual present"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Advances in technology have increased the suite of reliable digital reference points available to gain the same level of comfort remotely, at a cost that makes their integration into customer engagement channels feasible across the customer lifecycle. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "increased the suite of reliable digital reference points now available to an FI"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Such technology, when designed and leveraged responsibly, is often available on many smartphone operating systems, opening the door to historically underbanked customer segments who buy mobile phones second- or third-hand; the advances have also increased transparency between an FI and the customer, enhancing the FI's ability to recognise when a risk profile has changed and update it in a timely and effective manner. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "opening the door to new, historically underbanked customer segments"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Data ethics principle of openness and transparency: FIs need to be open and transparent with customers, regulators and other stakeholders about how they are using their data. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs need to be open and transparent with customers, regulators, and other stakeholders about how they are using their data"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Data ethics principle of accountability and oversight: FIs should be accountable to customers, regulators and other stakeholders for their use of the new "digital reference points" and resulting decisions. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs should be accountable to customers, regulators, and other stakeholders for their use of these new"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Guidance covers three customer lifecycle elements: onboarding, customer risk assessment, and maintenance and update of customer information during on-going due diligence. It aims to draw out recommendations for an FI to consider, recognising that each FI's risk-appetite and resources are distinct. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "onboarding, customer risk assessment, and the maintenance and update of customer information during on-going due diligence"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The approaches and technologies described are intended to serve as guiding principles rather than future regulatory expectations, and many could equally complement face-to-face settings; for example, analysing technical device characteristics behind a mobile phone to build a more robust customer profile need not be limited to digital, non-physically-present onboarding. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "intended to serve as guiding principles rather than future regulatory expectations"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Even for customers originally onboarded by traditional methods, building out the customer's digital presence over the relationship (what the FATF calls the customer's "progressive identity") could aid greatly in detecting changes to the risk profile and subsequently refreshing information. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could aid greatly in the detection of changes to the customer risk profile and subsequent refresh of information"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: By increasing the application of digital reference points and understanding the distinct dimensions they add to the customer profile, an FI can demonstrate that the single reference of face-to-face engagement no longer provides a material advantage in knowing customers and the risk they present of facilitating, or engaging in, financial crime. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "no longer provides a material advantage in knowing customers and knowing the risk they present"</sub>

## 3. Onboarding: more effective identification, verification, and initial authentication, including for financial inclusion

- **descriptive / context**: Traditional, regulatory-required onboarding information continues to be collected when engaging a prospective customer digitally, often under a series of drop-down questions and answers; widening the scope of self-reported data points at identification better prepares the FI to leverage more advanced controls across the customer lifecycle. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "in widening the scope of self-reported data points at the identification stage, the FI is better prepared"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Because these elements are often dynamic and change over time as a progressive identity is formed, FIs aiming to leverage them will need to structure their systems to permit and facilitate regular updating and tracking of the variables behind the holistic CDD/KYC profile. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "will need to structure their systems in a way that both permits and facilitates the regular updating and tracking"</sub>

## The need for high quality digital identity data

- **descriptive / context**: Several private and public sector initiatives have substantially strengthened the ability to verify key customer profile elements by providing direct or indirect access to official government sources on natural persons and legal entities (e.g., date of birth, residency), and through globally recognised identification information such as increased adoption of the Legal Entity Identifier (LEI). (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "providing direct or indirect access to official government sources on natural persons and legal entities"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In some countries an FI no longer needs to rely exclusively on a government-issued photo ID handed to a bank teller, and can use an application programming interface (API) to call a government or third-party database (e.g., a corporate registry or voting registry) to verify customer-reported information; increasingly, similar databases can confirm an individual's professional activity. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "can now leverage an application programming interface 10 (API) to call a government or third-party database"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Services with adequate levels of reliability are now available to confirm the authenticity of a document, telephone number or email address, or to cross-reference the prospective customer's device location against the self-reported address/country of residency. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Services with adequate levels of reliability are now available to confirm the authenticity of a given document"</sub>

## The initial authentication event

- **descriptive / definition**: The initial authentication event is a critical element in non-face-to-face digital onboarding, in which the prospective customer demonstrates that they are the same person behind the provided customer information; significant enhancements have diminished the traditional importance of physically present onboarding channels. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "where the prospective customer demonstrates that they are in fact the same person behind the provided customer information"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: Other types of inherent factors, along with ownership factors and knowledge factors, when employed together can also provide the necessary level of comfort, recognising that the strength of the initial authentication event impacts confidence in the customer profile. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "when employed together can also provide the necessary level of comfort"</sub>

## 4. Dynamic customer risk assessment and the significance of information access

- **expected / expectation**: FIs should leverage this deeper understanding of the customer risk profile, whether the customer is a natural person or legal entity, to be more disciplined and targeted in applying resources to keep information accurate. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs should leverage this deeper understanding of the customer risk profile"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The authentication event at onboarding, paired with concepts like binding, establishes the initial level of confidence an FI can place on accepting that observed device usage represents the actual, presumably legitimate behaviour of the customer. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "establish the initial level of confidence an FI can place on accepting that the observed usage of the device is"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: When the initial authentication event is not as strong and thus more susceptible to impersonation/identity fraud (e.g., authenticating without facial recognition or without a liveness check like a video), the lower level of confidence can extend across the whole customer lifecycle unless remedied later. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "that lower level of confidence can extend across the duration of the customer lifecycle unless remedied at a later date"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Follow-up authentication events after onboarding may lose some of their power, as authentication events going forward will likely only be as strong as the initial binding. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "will likely only be as strong as that initial binding"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Particularly critical to maintaining an accurate customer risk assessment is the level of access the customer grants to their device data and the FI's own technical capabilities to leverage that data; for example, permission to use the IP address or GPS information behind the customer's device is a significant level of information access that can better inform the customer risk profile. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the level of access granted by the customer to their device data"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: A customer may legitimately, for privacy reasons, use a virtual private network (VPN) or block location permission on the FI's app; given the limits this places on information access, the FI's ability to identify significant deviations in geographical risk factors is diminished. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These are two completely legitimate decisions based on privacy concerns"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Information access is not an entry barrier but, given its impact on properly assessing customer risk, it will likely impact the level of necessary customer engagement. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Information access in this regard is not an entry barrier"</sub>

## 5. On-going due diligence: trigger-based refresh

- **descriptive / case example**: Example: the traditional country of residence risk factor could map to IP address, triggering an alert when the customer spends a significant amount of time outside their previously reported country of residence. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the traditional country of residence risk factor could map to IP address"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Established thresholds against a variable's behaviour would determine whether a deviation reflects a possible change in the overall risk factor and whether a specific action is necessary to gain further confidence that the risk profile has actually changed (the trigger). (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Established thresholds against the variable’s behaviour would determine whether or not a deviation reflects"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Initially the thresholds may be theoretical (or vendor-driven, based on vendors' experience with other FIs), but over time insights from the digital customer lifecycle risk management programme would help the FI to "follow their data" and decrease or increase the need to trigger a specific action. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Initially these thresholds may be theoretical (or vendor-driven"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: FIs should further consider that the specific action in responding to an anomaly be anchored in their degree of confidence that the underlying risk factor may have actually changed. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs should further consider that the specific action in responding to the anomaly be anchored in their degree of confidence"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Example: if behavioural monitoring indicates the device user has changed hands and now holds the device at a distinct tilt, or has started copying and pasting passwords or basic data into forms (collectively "behavioural biometrics"), suggesting the individual may no longer be the originally verified one, this may warrant a low-level authentication event such as answering a personal question or re-entering a pin; successful completion re-affirms confidence and helps the FI understand appropriate behavioural monitoring thresholds. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this may warrant a low-level authentication event, such as answering a personal question, or re-entering a pin"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Example: if a device's location data indicate the customer has been outside their stated country of residence for a significant period and an API call to a government database confirms the customer is no longer resident, the risk profile can be updated automatically and the FI can confirm the trigger threshold was properly set. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an API call to a government database confirms that the customer is no longer resident"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The chain of events and feedback (a risk factor deconstructed into observable activities, thresholds on those activities indicating possible change, and targeted action confirming a suspected change as actual) should be explicit, regularly subject to calibration, and assessed for trustworthiness according to criteria similar to those applied at onboarding and in assessing initial customer risk. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should be explicit, regularly subject to calibration, and assessed for their trustworthiness"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The significant execution risk that such a process generates must be recognised by the FI and mitigated appropriately, always with a clear understanding of the assumptions the FI makes in each decision it undertakes. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The significant execution risk that such a process generates must be recognised by the FI and mitigated appropriately"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: An authentication event to reconfirm that the individual is the same as at onboarding is only as believable as the initial authentication strength at onboarding. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is only as believable as the initial authentication strength at onboarding"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Monitoring device location to understand whether a customer's country of residence has changed is highly dependent on the level of access the customer grants to underlying device information. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is highly dependent on the level of access granted by the customer to underlying device information"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: When the decision logic behind an event chain maintains a high degree of confidence, "trigger refresh" could be largely invisible to the customer; as confidence decreases, additional measures, including customer contact, may be necessary. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could be largely invisible to the customer"</sub>

## 6. Conclusions

- **recommended / recommendation**: FIs should consider mapping the variables behind the holistic customer profile to internal or external data sources capable of alerting to a change or deviation, and structuring data and systems architecture to facilitate regular updating and tracking of these variables under a risk-based approach. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "structure data and the FI’s systems architecture in a way that facilitates the regular updating"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: FIs should consider recognising that reaching the requisite level of trustworthiness on the underlying customer profile is a risk-based decision, where certain local conditions, including support for competent authority initiatives on financial inclusion, may warrant distinct approaches to identification, verification and authentication. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "reaching the requisite level of trustworthiness on building the underlying customer profile is a risk-based decision"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: FIs should consider developing a robust assurance strategy focused on the key dependencies of their digital customer lifecycle risk management approach, assessing their reliability in line with existing frameworks and standards. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Develop a robust assurance strategy focused on the key dependencies"</sub>

## The Wolfsberg Group - Guidance on Digital Customer Lifecycle Risk Management

- **descriptive / context**: The Executive Summary places the digital guidance within the Wolfsberg Effectiveness Factors: setting up a reasonable, risk-based set of controls is one of the three factors, and within it sits the need to prioritise resources and strengthen controls. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Establishing a reasonable and risk-based set of controls is one of the three Wolfsberg Effectiveness Factors"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Executive Summary says that, when digital approaches to customer lifecycle risk management are defined and calibrated responsibly, an FI gets the chance to develop a dynamic view of customer risk, refresh customer information in a targeted way, and take on new customers, including the financially excluded, without face-to-face contact, while concentrating resources on genuine financial crime threats. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "provide the FI with an opportunity to build a dynamic understanding of customer risk"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: First consideration in the Executive Summary: an FI should construct a fuller customer profile using a broader notion of identity attributes that adds to what AML/CTF regulation requires, consistent with customer consent and applicable data protection rules. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Build a more holistic customer profile via a wider concept of identity attributes that complements the elements"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Second consideration in the Executive Summary: an FI should link each variable underlying the holistic profile to an internal or external data source that can flag a possible change or departure from the expected value of that data point or attribute. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Map the variables behind the holistic customer profile to internal or external data sources"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Third consideration in the Executive Summary: an FI should use its improved ability to detect profile changes and deviations to move from traditional periodic refresh cycles towards a more effective trigger-based approach. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "evolve from traditional periodic refresh cycles to a more effective trigger-based approach"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Fourth consideration in the Executive Summary: an FI should recognise that how robust the underlying customer profile must be, and what assurance level the underlying systems need, are both risk-based decisions; local conditions, including competent authority support for financial inclusion initiatives, may justify different approaches to identification, verification and authentication for some relationship types. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "both risk-based decisions, where, for example, certain local conditions"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Fifth consideration in the Executive Summary: an FI should work with governments on digital initiatives that aim to widen access to high quality identity data, promote interoperability and ease access to financial services. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Collaborate with governments on digital initiatives aimed at increasing access to high quality identity data"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Guidance sets out how digital ways of building and maintaining a holistic customer risk profile call into question the extra value that face-to-face engagement is assumed to give an FI in knowing its customers and judging the risk that they facilitate or engage in financial crime. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "digital approaches to building and maintaining a holistic customer risk profile challenge the"</sub>

## 1. Introduction

- **descriptive / context**: The Introduction says customer expectations of digital engagement have grown faster and wider since the coronavirus pandemic, but rest on a shared recognition that a customer who supplies due diligence information through secure digital means should be able to get a certain set of financial products. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "have accelerated and broadened with the coronavirus pandemic"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The Guidance examines whether non-face-to-face digital engagement could count as a standard, or even lower-risk, channel by developing three core AML/CTF controls further: broader identification and verification with greater weight on authentication; a dynamic, more holistic customer risk profile that is built and maintained; and targeted ongoing due diligence in which customer data is refreshed on a trigger rather than periodic basis, directing resources to priority risks in real time. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "by further developing three core AML/CTF controls"</sub>

## 2. Background

- **descriptive / context**: The Wolfsberg Statement on Effectiveness names, as a main element of an effective financial crime compliance programme, establishing a reasonable and risk-based set of controls to mitigate the risk of the FI being used for illicit activity; the Guidance extends this principle to digital, non-face-to-face management of the customer lifecycle at onboarding, in customer risk assessment and in refreshing customer data. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "specifically at onboarding, in assessing customer risk and in refreshing customer data"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Among the data ethics principles to be considered as technology advances, on design and technical expertise the Guidance says FIs should have a deep understanding of the technologies they rely on, including what their use implies, where they are limited and what follows from using them. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs should understand deeply their underlying technologies and the implications, limitations, and consequences of their use"</sub>

## 3. Onboarding: more effective identification, verification, and initial authentication, including for financial inclusion

- **recommended / recommendation**: FIs should consider adopting a wider concept of identity that adds expanded attributes, for example behavioural biometrics and identifiers such as email address, to commonly required elements such as date of birth, which will help the FI build a progressive identity of the customer over time. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs, in this sense, should consider embracing a wider concept of identity"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Expanded identity information such as an email address or telephone number is often already gathered even though AML/CTF rules do not require it. More sensitive device-linked data, for example location data or device identification characteristics, may need consent but does not unduly burden onboarding. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may require consent but does not overburden the onboarding process"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Further data points that are neither intrusive nor obvious also strengthen the prospective customer's profile and can help prevent fraud; examples are how smoothly the person moves through the onboarding questionnaire and, on mobile channels, how the person holds the device. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "how easily the individual navigates the onboarding questionnaire"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: According to the Guidance, the dialogue between the private and public sectors on tackling financial crime should cover broader strategies for access to high quality identity data, including government-supported digital identity and similar initiatives that promote interoperability and financial inclusion. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should include larger strategies on access to high quality identity data"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: FIs should recognise that the required level of trustworthiness in underlying customer data can be reached through several different verification paths, consistent with the FI's risk appetite, applicable regulation and local government financial inclusion strategies. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs should recognise that reaching the requisite level of trustworthiness on underlying customer data can follow multiple, diverse verification paths"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: Live video calls or video selfies combined with facial recognition can substitute computer vision for human sight and give a high level of confidence that the person using the device at onboarding is the person being onboarded. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "can replace human vision with computer vision and establish a high level of confidence"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: In real time the FI can run through identification and verification into authentication by checking the customer's information against the government photo ID scanned on the device, validating the document and device authenticity with third-party software that meets the FI's assurance standards, and confirming that the document photo matches the facial biometric presented through the device. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "validating the authenticity of the document and device via third-party software that meets the FI"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Authentication need not rely on biometric real-time video with facial recognition. Where older smartphones and operating systems are common, advanced inherent factors may be unavailable, which should not be a barrier to authentication; other inherent factors together with ownership and knowledge factors can give the needed comfort, though the strength of the initial authentication event affects confidence in the customer profile. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "which should not constitute a barrier to authentication"</sub>

## 4. Dynamic customer risk assessment and the significance of information access

- **descriptive / context**: A key opportunity of a digital customer lifecycle risk management programme is fuller use of a customer risk profile that has many dimensions and is updated continually, drawing for example on the expanded identification attributes. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a multi-dimensional, continually updating customer risk profile"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: This would let an FI move from periodic refresh cycles to a trigger-based approach, and possibly give traditional risk factors less weight if and when the FI's data show that behavioural factors are better at alerting it to financial crime risk. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "potentially focusing less on traditional risk factors when, and if, an FI"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Traditional customer risk assessment methods group risk factors into four categories: customer factors (professional activity, industry sector, legal entity structure); geographical factors (country of residency or incorporation, area of operations); product, services and transactional behaviour (inherent product risk, source or destination of funds); and delivery channel (present or non-present, or via an intermediary). (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Customer factors (e.g., professional activity, industry sector, legal entity structure)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In a digital approach, delivery channel is the organising principle for how the FI manages the customer across the relationship, not one factor among others. Two variables greatly raise or lower the FI's ability to mitigate financial crime risk in a non-face-to-face setting: the quality of the initial authentication event at onboarding with the device, and how much access to device information the FI has over time. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the ordering principle for how an FI manages the customer throughout their relationship with the FI"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: An FI should be encouraged to follow its own data by tracing identified stolen-identity cases or similar onboarding exploits back to the initial authentication event, which often shows clearly how much confidence that authentication approach actually supported. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an FI should be encouraged to follow its data by mapping identified cases of stolen identity or similar onboarding"</sub>

## 5. On-going due diligence: trigger-based refresh

- **recommended / method**: To set up a trigger-based approach for keeping customer data accurate, the FI should consider breaking each broad risk factor, such as geography or transactional behaviour, into a set of variables or data elements and linking each to internal or external data sources that can show a departure from what is expected for that data point, for instance as collected at onboarding. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FI should consider de-constructing each broad risk factor"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The Guidance cautions that confidence in changing a risk factor on the strength of an API call to a third-party database depends directly on the FI's confidence in that process and in the quality of the information in the database. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Confidence in the decision to modify a risk factor based on an API call to a third-party database"</sub>

## 6. Conclusions

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Conclusions state that new data points and innovative controls let an FI define alternative engagement paths beyond a branch visit; these non-face-to-face digital paths can ultimately give a matching level of confidence in the customer identified and verified at onboarding and throughout the relationship. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "can ultimately lead to a commensurate level of confidence in the identified and verified customer at onboarding"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: In moving from traditional to more innovative lifecycle mechanisms, FIs should consider building a fuller customer profile through a wider concept of identity, adding attributes (often used against fraud or cybercrime) to those AML/CTF regulation requires, always consistent with customer consent and data protection rules. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "with additional identity attributes (often used to prevent fraud or cybercrime)"</sub>

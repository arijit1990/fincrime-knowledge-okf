## 1. Addressing uncertainty about how to balance regulatory and supervisory obligations

- **descriptive / context**: Uncertainty among cross-border payments market participants about balancing obligations under different data frameworks was seen as a barrier to the Roadmap targets. The report says such challenges, including possibly conflicting requirements, are not unique to cross-border payments, and that other sectors (for example international civil aviation, and clinical trials and health research, combined with data privacy laws) have successfully reduced fragmentation. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "are not unique to cross-border payments"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Examples in the payments area include regional arrangements: the Joint Chiefs of Global Tax Enforcement (the J5, committed to combatting transnational tax crime through enforcement collaboration) and the payment systems SEPA (Single Euro Payments Area) and Buna. The report says identifying successful use cases may enable replication as global cross-border payments solutions. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Joint Chiefs of Global Tax Enforcement (the J5)9 as well as payments systems such as Single Euro Payments Area"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A comprehensive mapping exercise would be helpful to understand the extent of divergence across data framework approaches affecting speed and cost, would serve as a resource clarifying data requirements in different jurisdictions, and could be the basis for regulator discussions on consistency, recognising that some differences may be needed because of differing risks and contexts. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Undertaking a comprehensive mapping exercise would be helpful to understand the extent of divergence"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The mapping exercise could be augmented by gathering the guidance that private sector organisations give clients on the transactional data formats required by different jurisdictions. Such guidance helps payees provide the right input at payment initiation, which reduces payment returns or delays and so increases speed. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "gathering the guidance that private sector organisations provide to their clients"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Balancing the requirements of data privacy and protection authorities with AML/CFT rules is given as an example of work that could take place under Recommendation 2; the report says the private sector has repeatedly highlighted this issue and public consultation respondents confirmed it. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Balancing the requirements related to data privacy and protection authorities with AML/CFT rules"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Under Recommendation 2, Forum participants should take into consideration successful examples from other areas. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Forum participants should take into consideration successful examples from other areas."</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Under Recommendation 2, the Forum should also consider a process to ensure that new, potentially emerging divergences and inconsistencies are addressed as they arise. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Forum should also consider a process to ensure that new potentially emerging divergences"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A range of consultation respondents highlighted increasing cross-border payment fraud as a significant concern and recognised the importance of enhanced data flows for prevention and detection controls. Fraud prevention requirements and approaches may diverge significantly between jurisdictions, adversely affecting transaction security, at a time of heightened cross-border fraud boosted by technology and digitalisation (citing INTERPOL 2024). (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "increasing prevalence of fraud in cross-border payments as a significant concern"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report describes cross-border fraud as multifaceted and says work to implement the data frameworks recommendations may contribute to fraud prevention in many ways. Sharing best practices and exploring further harmonisation of fraud prevention requirements, in balance with the G20 Roadmap objectives, should improve effectiveness globally. The FSB and Forum will consider how to respond, including which international bodies are best placed to lead further work. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "sharing best practices and exploring further harmonisation of requirements and approaches to fraud prevention"</sub>

## 2. Promoting the alignment and interoperability of regulatory and data requirements related to cross-border payments

- **descriptive / finding or result**: These frictions create a greater need for manual intervention, which slows payments and raises costs; inconsistent implementation can raise the incidence of payment rejections where message data does not meet local standards; and these issues raise operational costs and divert resources from activities that could support more effective AML/CFT and sanctions compliance. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Different approaches to data requirements create a greater need for manual intervention"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 12 records that FATF is considering revisions to Recommendation 16 and published proposed revisions in February 2024 (public consultation on Recommendation 16 on Payment Transparency); this work is identified as priority action 6a in FSB (2023a). Respondents widely supported the four recommendations that follow (3 to 6) but noted their effectiveness will depend on consistent implementation of data standards across jurisdictions, particularly given variations in local data protection regulations and compliance requirements. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "that their effectiveness will depend on the consistent implementation of these data standards"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The most frequently mentioned friction was differing implementation by jurisdictions of FATF Recommendation 16 (Wire Transfers). Stakeholders also pointed to many kinds of jurisdiction-specific requirements on technical messaging-format standards and accompanying legal documentation, and some authorities described difficulty complying with sanctions obligations, including from differences in the data formats provided for sanctions screening. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "divergence in how FATF Recommendation 16 (Wire Transfers) has been implemented by jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The G20 cross-border payments programme identified fragmentation of payment messaging standards, including different standards or different implementations of the same standard, as a major friction behind high cost, slow speed and lack of transparency. Growing worldwide adoption of ISO 20022 is an opportunity for greater interoperability through richer, more structured data. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identified the fragmentation of payment messaging standards"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Limited, incomplete or inconsistent implementation of ISO 20022 in different jurisdictions risks undermining its benefits. Many inefficiencies stem from misaligned message flows and inconsistent data usage along the end-to-end payment chain, so how the standard is used in practice could vary considerably and frictions could persist even as ISO 20022 is adopted. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "limited, incomplete, or inconsistent implementation of the standard in different jurisdictions and regions risks undermining its benefits"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Roadmap directed the CPMI to develop, jointly with industry, harmonised ISO 20022 requirements for cross-border payments; these were published in October 2023 to foster consistent end-to-end use, supplement existing usage guidelines and market practices, and help the G20 meet its speed, cost and transparency targets. They are described as critical to implementing other recommendations in the report, particularly implementation of a revised FATF Recommendation 16 (Recommendation 4). (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These data requirements were published in October 2023"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Footnotes record that FSB outreach in 2023 noted frictions differ by payment rail (for example SWIFT, remittance providers, credit card networks) and by identifier used to settle a transaction (for example ABA routing number versus IBAN). A CPMI survey in late 2021 indicated 78% of respondents (out of 56 payment system operators in 38 jurisdictions) had implemented or had concrete plans to implement ISO 20022 by 2025. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "78% of the survey respondents (out of a total of 56 payment system operators in 38 jurisdictions)"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Recommendation 3 states that national authorities should encourage market participants, including central banks and payment system operators, to adopt the harmonised ISO 20022 data requirements for cross-border payments set by the Bank for International Settlements' Committee on Payments and Market Infrastructure (CPMI). (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should encourage the adoption by market participants, including central banks and payment system operators"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report judges that having national authorities encourage market participants (including payment system operators, payment service providers and end users) to adopt the harmonised data requirements by the end of 2027 appears to strike an appropriate balance. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "by the end of 2027 seems to strike a right balance"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Benefits of harmonised ISO 20022 depend on widespread, consistent use of the CPMI data requirements (for example because of network effects). The joint CPMI and Payments Market Practice Group (PMPG) task force has worked with the High-Value Payment System Plus (HVPS+) group and the Cross-border payments and reporting plus (CBPR+) group to align their usage guidelines with the CPMI data requirements, which consultation respondents strongly supported. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to align HVPS+ and CBPR+ usage guidelines with the CPMI data requirements"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Some consultation respondents supported mandating ISO 20022 adoption across Payment Market Infrastructures (PMIs) to avoid data loss from alteration or truncation as payments pass through PMIs with different data capacities, and said PMIs as gatekeepers must be held responsible for clear rules. Other respondents said harmonisation has costs and jurisdictions should simply be encouraged to find their own ways to support consistent implementation. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supported mandating the ISO 20022 adoption across Payment Market Infrastructures (PMIs)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A further suggestion was to clarify interpretation and usage of some data fields by liaising with FATF, the Wolfsberg Group and other financial crimes experts, to ensure consistency and common understanding between the AML/CFT and message standards communities; the report says this may be taken up by the Forum. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "liaise with FATF, the Wolfsberg Group and other financial crimes experts"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Recommendation 4 has a twofold aim: (i) reduce fragmentation in AML/CFT data requirements in payments by implementing the global standard in FATF Recommendation 16, which leverages ISO 20022; and (ii) encourage clear, accessible guidance on any additional data requirements beyond the Recommendation 16 minimum needed to address specific risks. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The aim of this Recommendation is twofold"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A 2021 FATF stocktake of divergence in implementing AML/CFT requirements found inconsistent implementation caused frictions for cross-border payments: rising costs were the main consequence, followed by reduced speed, reduced access and inconsistent levels of transparency. It also noted some differences in requirements may be necessary because of differing risks and contexts. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Rising costs appeared to be the main consequence, followed by reduced speed, reduced access"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Recommendation 4, aimed at avoiding inconsistent application of cross-border payment data requirements for AML/CFT compliance, states that national authorities should implement FATF Recommendation 16 and give clear, accessible guidance on any extra data needed to comply with local AML/CFT regulations. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "national authorities should implement FATF Recommendation 16 and provide clear and accessible guidance"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Recommendation 4 adds that national authorities should also use applicable global data standards, where available. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "They should also use applicable global data standards, where available."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The FSB stocktake and industry engagement found that differing implementation of FATF standards leads to disparities in the data required in payment messages by domestic AML/CFT frameworks, which may create ambiguity and complexity and can result in payment delays, extra costs from manual intervention, false positives in screening, and increased regulatory and legal risk. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "false positives in screening processes and increased regulatory and legal risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FATF is reviewing Recommendation 16 to reflect developments in payment systems architecture, including adoption of ISO 20022 and the need to encourage standardised entity identifiers such as the Legal Entity Identifier (LEI) in payments data, to improve consistency and usability of message data and enable more efficient and accurate AML/CFT controls. More consistent AML/CFT application would let financial institutions reduce resources spent on manual intervention, and FATF will consider data-related issues in its planned guidance once Recommendation 16 is revised. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "need to encourage the use of standardised entity identifiers, such as the Legal Entity Identifier (LEI)"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The report says domestic regulators may choose to introduce bespoke data requirements for risks specific to their jurisdiction; when additional bespoke requirements are needed, authorities should transparently set out the public policy rationale and should seek to make them easy to access and understand so all financial institutions can comply. To reduce fragmentation, consideration should be given to aligning data formats for minimum data requirements as much as possible and using applicable global data standards for any additional optional data. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "authorities should transparently set out the public policy rationale for them"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Consultation respondents noted the ongoing review of Recommendation 16 and a general theme was the need to resolve uncertainty about which data should travel along the payment chain, including whether and how information required by one national authority should or should not travel through the chain. Broad support was stated for consistent implementation across jurisdictions. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "including whether and how information that is required for one national authority should or should not travel"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In the FSB stocktake, engagement and consultation comments, challenges in complying with sanctions obligations were the most frequently cited issue affecting the cost and speed of cross-border payments, with technical challenges in complying with sanctions regimes highlighted as a key friction. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the challenges of complying with sanctions obligations is the most frequently cited issue"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Recommendation 5 states that sanctions authorities should take steps to standardise how sanctions lists are formatted, shared and updated. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Sanctions authorities should take steps to standardise the way sanctions lists are formatted, shared and updated"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Recommendation 5 also states that the use of sufficient and standardised identifiers should be encouraged, to help identification, reduce false positives and promote links between data sources. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The use of sufficient and standardised identifiers should be encouraged to better facilitate identification"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: With dozens of sanctions authorities globally, each with its own data formats, the report says standardising formats and making sanctions lists machine-readable is much needed. Listed designated parties may vary between jurisdictions' lists and the sharing format is not standardised, which raises compliance costs as participants apply non-standard technical solutions. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "dozens of different sanctions authorities globally, each having their own data formats"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Inconsistent and text-based presentation of sanctions lists can cause many false positives in sanctions screening, slowing processes because legitimate payments must be manually investigated and diverting resources from payments of genuine concern. Standardised presentation would let participants invest more in standardised screening solutions, and standard unambiguous identifiers could materially reduce false positive rates and streamline screening. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Inconsistent and text-based presentation of sanctions lists can also result in a significant occurrence of false positives"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report says the Forum could explore options to address inconsistencies in data elements relevant to sanctions screening, in close coordination with the FATF and national sanctions authorities, given the link to FATF Recommendation 16 and FATF recommendations on sanctions implementation. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Forum could explore options to address the inconsistencies in data elements relevant for sanctions screening"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Stakeholders also raised separate challenges from conflicting and inconsistent regulatory requirements and supervisory expectations on sanctions implementation across jurisdictions. The report says this issue falls outside its scope, though competent authorities may investigate options to address it. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This specific issue falls outside of the scope of this report"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: FSB engagement found that poor data quality, fragmented data sources and limited standardisation of data exchange complicate cross-border payment processing, raising cost, limiting speed and affecting transparency. Global standardised identifiers could help, with links to Recommendations 3, 4 and 5; the FSB had reviewed global digital identifiers for legal entities and natural persons as part of the Roadmap. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "poor data quality, fragmentation in data sources and limited standardisation of data exchange"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Standardised identifiers such as the Business Identifier Code (BIC) exist, but the LEI may offer advantages because it is not linked to a specific messaging network and is available to all corporate and legal entities; it can verify the identity of all legal entities in a payment chain. The G20 Roadmap included a priority action to explore enhanced use of the LEI in cross-border payments, reflecting the LEI's broad characteristics and G20 endorsement of its creation by the FSB. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The LEI may offer advantages as it is not linked to a specific messaging network"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Recommendation 6 states that national authorities should back wider use of standardised global identifiers such as the Legal Entity Identifier (LEI), including by taking steps to stress that using them in cross-border payments is best practice. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "National authorities should support the enhanced use of standardised global identifiers, such as the Legal Entity Identifier (LEI)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnotes state that identifier work for natural persons was concluded unlikely to contribute materially to 2027 targets, though greater LEI use could renew it. The LEI was endorsed by G20 Leaders at the June 2012 Los Cabos Summit, established by the FSB in 2012, and is overseen by the Regulatory Oversight Committee (ROC, over 65 regulators and public authorities plus 19 observers from more than 50 countries) with a data quality management programme. The Global LEI Foundation (GLEIF), established by the FSB in June 2014, supports LEI implementation under ROC oversight. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "more than 65 financial market regulators and other public authorities, as well as 19 observers from more than 50 countries"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: FSB work on improving LEI adoption found authorities and market participants recognise its potential benefits in strengthening data standardisation and supporting straight through processing (STP), customer due diligence (CDD) processes and sanctions screening, reinforcing the report's recommendations. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "assisting and supporting straight through processing (STP), customer due diligence (CDD) processes and sanctions screening"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: The October 2024 FSB LEI progress report recommends that FSB member jurisdictions, with the Regulatory Oversight Committee and GLEIF, explore where appropriate the scope to mandate LEI use for certain payment message types for routing message formats migrating to ISO 20022; keep exploring the LEI's role in KYC due diligence and other uses such as sanctions screening; and consider a staged introduction of LEI requirements in payment messages, assessing which categories of entities or payment value thresholds could be phased in. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "explore, where appropriate, the scope to mandate use of the LEI for certain payment message types"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: The same October 2024 progress report recommends that relevant standard-setting bodies and international organisations consider issuing guidance on the role the LEI and possibly the vLEI might play in assisting entities with due diligence for KYC and sanctions screening, and fraud prevention. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should consider issuing guidance on the role that the LEI and possibly the vLEI might play"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In October 2023 the CPMI recommended, as part of ISO 20022 implementation, identifying financial institutions in cross-border payments through globally recognised, publicly available identifiers, recognising benefits of BIC or LEI. The FATF is also considering, in its Recommendation 16 review, encouraging standardised global identifiers such as the LEI in the information accompanying all qualifying payments or value transfers. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identify financial institutions involved in cross-border payments via globally recognised and publicly available identifiers"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 25 describes the Regulatory Oversight Committee (ROC) as a group of more than 65 financial markets regulators and other public authorities plus 19 observers from more than 50 countries. It serves the broad public interest by improving the quality of data used in financial reporting, improving the ability to monitor financial risk, and lowering regulatory reporting costs by harmonising the standards across jurisdictions. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It promotes the broad public interest by improving the quality of data used in financial data reporting"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The FSB is aware that sanctions authorities use the LEI when possible to identify sanctioned entities. Most potential sanctions targets may not be eligible for an LEI because they are not legal entities, and many others would not register, but the LEI could still reduce false positives that currently reduce speed and raise costs. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the LEI could contribute to reducing the number of false positives"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Private sector participants include LEI use among payment best practices; SWIFT's Payments Market Practice Group (PMPG) September 2021 white paper described existing market practices on the LEI as an identifier in payments and its use case, with benefits including more transparent, efficient and secure payments. The report adds that the benefits of a global identifier with strong governance and data quality management have been recognised in other domains (for example statistics and trade), suggesting a jurisdiction-wide approach to supporting LEI use might be considered. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "benefits for participants include more transparent, efficient and secure payments"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Consultation respondents supported the rationale for Recommendation 6, that a standardised global identifier for legal entities could benefit payments across sanctions, AML and fraud prevention. Some added that LEI Level 2 data on 'who owns whom' could give useful beneficial ownership information when linked to high-quality beneficial ownership data via ongoing mapping exercises. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "LEI Level 2 data that answers the question of ‘who owns whom’"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Industry stakeholders asked for common principles across privacy frameworks to enable mutual recognition across jurisdictions, and some encouraged adequacy arrangements or other transfer mechanisms to bridge different privacy approaches between major markets. The GPA ran a 2023 census gathering information from 78 GPA members on privacy and data protection policies and delivery approaches. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "gathering information from 78 GPA members"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Recommendation 7 states that the OECD, building on its ongoing evidence-based, multi-stakeholder work on cross-border data flows and together with data privacy and protection authorities and relevant cross-border payments stakeholders, should explore options for faster, cheaper, more transparent and more accessible cross-border payment-related data flows while ensuring high levels of privacy protection. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should explore different options to enable faster, less costly, more transparent and more accessible cross-border payment-related data flows"</sub>

## Box 1: GPA Information on cross-border data transfer mechanisms

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The 2023 GPA Census found that 90% of surveyed jurisdictions have laws with provisions restricting cross-border transfers of personal information (up slightly from 83% in 2020), while only 17% of authorities require facilities in the jurisdiction for data processing (down from 27% in 2020). (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "most jurisdictions (90%) have laws that include provisions on restricting transfers of personal information across borders"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: On legislated mechanisms for cross-border transfers of personal data, authorities reported model clauses (56%), binding corporate rules (62%), commercial agreements (42%) and certification schemes (40%). (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "model clauses (56%) and binding corporate rules (62%)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The GPA Global Frameworks and Standards Working Group (GFSWG) and the OECD have done extensive work on cross-border transfer tools and commonalities; in 2023 the GFSWG completed a detailed comparison of standard contractual clauses across data protection frameworks to help understand transfer mechanisms and identify gaps. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "completed a detailed comparison of standard contractual clauses in different data protection and privacy frameworks"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The OECD noted that governments and authorities increasingly use a range of approaches to transfer data across borders while ensuring protection upon crossing, and that frameworks for trust and data flows build on convergence but businesses find them hard to fully operationalise globally; the OECD carried out work on the 'data free flow with trust' policy agenda. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "businesses identify challenges to fully operationalise them at the global level"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: This convergence work has not focused specifically on payments-related data flows, but a dedicated working group of the Data Free Flow with Trust (DFFT) Experts Community, launched at the OECD in April 2024, is now examining the issue. The DFFT agenda, promoted by the Japanese G20 (2019) and G7 (2023) presidencies, aims to promote free data flow while respecting privacy, security and intellectual property rights. The excerpt ends mid-sentence at 'inform the development of concrete'. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The DFFT Experts Community was launched at the OECD in April 2024"</sub>

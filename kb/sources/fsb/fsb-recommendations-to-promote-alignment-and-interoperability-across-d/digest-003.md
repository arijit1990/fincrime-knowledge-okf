## Box 1: GPA Information on cross-border data transfer mechanisms

- **descriptive / context**: The excerpt begins mid-sentence in the DFFT discussion: a DFFT working group is exploring payments-related cross-border data flows with more than 50 cross-border payments stakeholders, including national payments and privacy authorities, standard-setters such as the FATF, and industry experts. It will assess tools and legal bases for cross-border transfers of personal data suited to payments, which could eventually feed into OECD policy work. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A DFFT working group is exploring cross-border payments-related data flows with more than 50 cross-border payments stakeholders"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Recommendation 8, which builds on the work in Recommendation 7, states that relevant authorities should adopt and enforce consistent standards in domestic privacy and data protection regimes that apply to payment processing, and should identify appropriate cross-border data transfer mechanisms. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "relevant authorities should adopt and enforce consistent standards in domestic privacy and data protection regimes"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: The work done under Recommendation 7 could be used as a basis for identifying suitable mechanisms for payments-related data transfers. As an example, financial services firms sometimes rely on 'equivalence' or 'adequacy decisions' from jurisdictions that find another jurisdiction's data protection and privacy requirements equivalent or adequate, in order to transfer data across borders in the normal course of business. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The work carried out under Recommendation 7 could serve as the basis for identifying appropriate mechanisms"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Consultation respondents urged the Forum to coordinate closely with the OECD DFFT Experts Community to reduce duplication and asked how national DPAs are expected to engage; the report says this will be assessed with the Forum's launching organisations (FSB, FATF, GPA and OECD) and is relevant to national follow-up work. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "urged that the Forum coordinates closely with the OECD DFFT Experts Community"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Financial services firms sometimes rely on 'equivalence' or 'adequacy decisions' from jurisdictions that find another jurisdiction's data protection requirements equivalent or adequate, in order to transfer data across borders in the normal course of business. Footnote 34 adds that equivalency assessments are also used for supervision of payment system operators recognised by multiple jurisdictions, to decide whether informed reliance can be placed on the primary supervisor, and says this example is not related to data privacy rules. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "rely on “equivalence” or “adequacy decisions” from jurisdictions that find that another jurisdiction"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: To facilitate mutual recognition and interoperability (for example through effective equivalence or adequacy assessments), jurisdictions should adopt and enforce consistent standards in domestic privacy and data protection regimes applicable to payment processing. Authorities should administer transparent rulemaking, clearly articulate these policies in public communications including implementation guidance as appropriate, seek public input, and engage foreign counterparts to support understanding and regulatory cooperation, moving towards adequacy assessments or similar mechanisms over time. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "authorities should administer transparent rulemaking processes and clearly articulate these policies in public communications"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Consistent data protection and privacy standards will facilitate cross-jurisdictional benchmarking and assurance that data are adequately protected in the destination country, can foster mutual recognition arrangements and interoperability, and can limit the need to restrict personal data transfers to and from overseas jurisdictions. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "limit the need to restrict personal data transfers to and from overseas jurisdictions"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Jurisdictions should allow implementation of specific data transfer tools, such as standard contractual clauses, binding corporate rules, certifications or similar tools, based on an assessment that they give sufficient safeguards for personal data transfers in the payments context and meet payment industry requirements. These tools let parties identify responsibilities and ensure accountability through the transfer, and may give assurances the origin jurisdiction values even where the destination legal framework lacks protections. Footnote 35 links such tools to the Global Cross-Border Privacy Rules Forum (established 2022, building on the APEC CBPR framework). (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions should allow the implementation of specific data transfer tools, such as standard contractual clauses"</sub>

## 3. Mitigating restrictions on the flow of data related to payments across borders

- **descriptive / definition**: The FSB stocktake noted that Roadmap objectives can be undermined by policies requiring data to be stored or processed in-country: restrictions on transferring data across borders (data localisation) or requirements to keep a copy in the local jurisdiction (data mirroring). These range from requirements that locally generated data be stored and processed within the jurisdiction to export conditions that must be met before data move abroad; localisation forces firms to locate all or part of their data and operations within the imposing jurisdiction. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "requirements to maintain a copy of the data in the local jurisdiction (data mirroring)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Data localisation may be driven by differing public policy objectives: building local operational resilience, protecting against cyber threats, strengthening customer confidence in data storage security in the national payment system as some authorities see it, and ensuring law enforcement and supervisory access where authorities lack confidence they would have it. Many objectives extend beyond financial regulation and are often not set by financial regulators, but localisation can delay or even stop cross-border payments. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "ensure access to data for law enforcement and supervisory authorities"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: For participants operating in several jurisdictions, localisation limits internal sharing or aggregation of data. Inability or delay in aggregating locally-held data makes enterprise-wide risk assessment harder, including identifying cross-border fraud and complying with AML/CFT, sanctions and other obligations, and can raise risks of incomplete data causing transaction failures that disrupt STP. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "more difficult to assess and manage risk on an enterprise-wide basis, including effectively identifying cross-border fraud"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Localisation may require duplicated infrastructure and personnel in individual jurisdictions, adding fragmentation, complexity and opportunities for error, and creating new cyber vulnerabilities. Central monitoring for malicious activity using all relevant data is lost, as is the ability to move data to reserve capacity in another location (for example during a cyber attack), which may degrade data security and the global resilience of data systems. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Monitoring for malicious activity can no longer occur centrally"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Localisation may cause loss of economies of scale as more data centres are needed, raising firms' costs to manage risk and to store, secure and integrate data, which may affect consumer prices; the cost and complexity may compel some participants to leave certain markets or act as a barrier to entry for smaller players, reducing competition and possibly access to financial services. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "act as a barrier to entry for smaller players, reducing competition in crossborder payments"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Recommendation 9 states that national authorities should give cross-border payments market participants a clear and reasonable legal pathway to transmit across borders data related to payment processing, risk management, or fraud and financial crime prevention. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should provide a clear and reasonable legal pathway for cross-border payments market participants"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Under Recommendation 9, where applicable, national authorities should provide alternatives to requirements to use local computing facilities. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Where applicable, national authorities should provide alternatives to requirements to use local computing facilities."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The recommendations in this section aim to mitigate unintended consequences of restrictive data storage and transfer policies without compromising underlying objectives of some localisation policies, such as operational resilience and effective supervision and law enforcement. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "while avoiding compromising on the underlying objectives of some data localisation policies"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: National authorities should avoid restricting transfer of data out of their jurisdiction where the destination jurisdiction has broadly equivalent data protection and privacy standards, because lack of confidence in that equivalence can motivate localisation. Where localisation policies exist, authorities should give firms alternatives to facilitate effective payment processing, risk management, and fraud and financial crime prevention. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should avoid imposing restrictions on the transfer of data outside of their jurisdiction"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Legal pathways and alternatives for transferring and storing payments data without compromising storage security could mitigate localisation frictions, support data aggregation for risk management and compliance, and improve operational efficiency. Industry stakeholders cautioned that creating pathways, while helpful, could also lead to fragmentation, and the Forum could develop principles and best practices to help jurisdictions avoid this while keeping the objectives behind localisation. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the creation of pathways, while helpful in addressing the issues described, could also lead to fragmentation"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Consultation responses frequently cited rising cross-border payment fraud as a significant concern, with broad recognition of the need to enhance data flows for fraud prevention and detection controls, not only among payments participants but including social media and telecommunications firms, which are also stakeholders in anti-fraud efforts. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "including social media and telecommunications firms"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Jurisdictions often cite a valid need to keep access to private sector data for regulatory and law enforcement activities; financial regulators need payments data for regulation and supervision and may need personal data in financial crime or suspected fraud matters, and several authorities from different jurisdictions may be involved in cross-border payments supervision. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may also need to access personal data in matters related to financial crimes and/or suspicion of fraud"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Instead of requiring data to be held locally, authorities should establish requirements for participants to allow access to payments data for regulatory and supervisory activities, including legal arrangements facilitating cross-border access directly or via local authorities, for example under a memorandum of understanding. National authorities should give participants the opportunity to grant prompt access to payments data requested by financial regulators before being required to use local computing facilities. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "authorities should establish requirements for cross-border payments market participants to allow access to payments data"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: When considering domestic policies, authorities may look to build on existing and emerging governance models, including approaches to government access for law enforcement and national security purposes such as those in the OECD Declaration on Government Access to Personal Data Held by Private Sector Entities (December 2022). (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "authorities may look to build on existing and emerging governance models"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Recommendation 11: when designing data-related policies, relevant authorities should consider potential impacts on consumers and cross-border payments market participants, in particular how data restrictive policies, including data localisation and data mirroring requirements, could affect the cost, speed, transparency and accessibility of cross-border payments. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "relevant authorities should consider potential impacts on consumers and cross-border payments market participants"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Relevant authorities should analyse the impact of data restrictive policies on cross-border payments before imposing them; for example, requiring firms to build and integrate additional data centres into global infrastructure may act as a barrier to market entry, and additional requirements may affect the cost and availability of cross-border payments. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "relevant authorities should analyse the impact of those policies on cross-border payments before imposing them"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Recommendation 10 states that national authorities should set up clear and transparent mechanisms allowing cross-border payments market participants to share data with foreign regulatory and supervisory authorities, as appropriate. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should establish clear and transparent mechanisms to allow cross-border payments market participants to share data"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Under Recommendation 10, cross-border payments market participants should ensure that the relevant regulatory and supervisory authorities have full and timely access to data in line with their respective mandates. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should ensure relevant regulatory and supervisory authorities have full and timely access to data"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: The report says impact analysis could also take into account alternative, less restrictive ways of achieving the same outcome. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Impact analysis could also take into consideration alternative ways of providing the same outcome in a less restrictive manner"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Respondents to the public consultation suggested that authorities considering imposing or extending data barriers should identify the regulatory objective being pursued and consider whether other, less restrictive means could achieve it, including but not limited to privacy-enhancing technologies (PETs), regulatory reporting or data access mechanisms. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "consider whether other, less restrictive means – including but not limited to privacy-enhancing technologies (PETs)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Impact could be measured in various ways; the World Bank, OECD and others have conducted empirical and policy analyses authorities can leverage, and the Forum could in the longer term develop a framework to assist impact assessment. The report lists illustrative considerations (next four points). (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Forum could undertake the development of a framework to assist relevant authorities in assessing impacts"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Illustrative impact consideration 1: impact on operational resilience, including cyber resilience, because data siloes prevent institutions from building redundant systems and from aggregating data that may help detect threats. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These impacts stem from creating data siloes that prevent institutions from building redundant systems"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Illustrative impact consideration 2: impact on effectiveness in detecting and preventing fraud and financial crime, since data fragmentation hinders efforts to detect and monitor cross-border financial crime trends, risks and threats. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the fragmentation of data hinders efforts to detect and monitor cross-border financial crime trends"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Illustrative impact consideration 3: impact on overall availability of cross-border payments services, since added costs of some data protection and privacy requirements may deter foreign firms, particularly smaller ones, from entering the market or limit their services, which may disproportionately affect providers to low-income consumers. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may serve as a deterrent to foreign firms entering the market, particularly smaller firms"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Illustrative impact consideration 4: macroeconomic effects. Data restrictive policies can raise import prices for data-reliant downstream industries, reducing exports and trade. The World Bank notes removing data restrictions would bring, on average, about 5% on trade in services and about 4.5% productivity gains on average, with identifiable GDP effects. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "about 5% on trade in services44 and that the gains in productivity would be on average of about 4.5%"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Footnotes cite further estimates: ITIF reports that a 1-point increase in a nation's data restrictiveness cuts gross trade output 7%, slows productivity 2.9% and raises downstream prices 1.5% over five years; and an OECD finding based on an OECD-WTO business questionnaire that, depending on the measure, data localisation could raise data management costs by between 15 and 55%. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could raise data management costs by between 15 and 55%"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Factoring in such considerations will help authorities make informed decisions on causal connections and trade-offs between data protection and privacy objectives and other policy goals. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Factoring in such considerations will help authorities make informed decisions"</sub>

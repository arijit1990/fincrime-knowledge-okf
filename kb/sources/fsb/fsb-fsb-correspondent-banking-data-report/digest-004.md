## 2.2 Data on individual CBRs from the FSB-CBCG survey

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Graph 11 (share of respondents' correspondent banking arrangements terminated, by asset size, 2011 versus first 6 months of 2016): of 230 banks that participated as respondents, the 118 that answered saw on average, regardless of asset size, a higher proportion of their CBRs terminated by their service provider. It is particularly significant for medium-sized banks, whose termination rate more than doubled from below 10% in 2011 to more than 18% in 2016, exceeding small banks (16% in 2016) and large banks (12%). ## 3. Effects of the withdrawal from correspondent banking

- **descriptive / context**: Section 3 examines effects of the decline in correspondent banking on volume and value of payments, length of payment chains, concentration on the correspondent and respondent side, use of currencies, and potential consequences for banks' end-customers through prices or difficulties accessing services. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "length of the chains of payments, concentration on the correspondent and respondent side, use of currencies"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: As CPMI noted in July 2016, globally the decline in the number of active correspondents has not lowered payment message volume or the underlying value of messages processed through SWIFT; the number of payments rose by 36% between 2011 and 2016 (Graph 13). The following sections discuss whether this may at least in part signal longer payment chains and market concentration. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the number of payments has increased between 2011 and 2016 by 36% ( Graph 13 )"</sub>

## 3.2 Impact on the length of payment chains

- **descriptive / finding or result**: A reduction in correspondent relationships does not necessarily reduce payments sent and received: payments often continue through remaining relationships, and where the corridor between A and B becomes inactive payments are likely to flow through another country C that still has active corridors with A and B. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "payments will flow through another country C that still has active corridors with A and B"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Beyond other sources such as the World Bank report on withdrawal from correspondent banking, the FSB-CBCG survey found that 25% of banks changed their correspondent arrangements since 2011 so payments flow through different countries (Graph 16, 314 banks). This could imply banks where the share of direct cross-border payments fell turned to new relationships in different banks and countries, and suggests a lengthening of the chain of payment messages needed for a wire transfer. Bank asset size is not a determinant characteristic for changing arrangements, and increases in CBR restrictions were higher for respondents that changed their arrangements (footnote 31: ascertained from the 44 banks that answered both questions). (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "banks’ asset size is not a determinant characteristic to change correspondent banking arrangements"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Lengthening of payment chains is consistent with rising payment volume while relationship numbers fall (Graph 17), although more messages with limited increase in value may also reflect greater use of correspondent banking for smaller transactions. CPMI's July 2016 report noted rerouting through third countries could raise correspondent banking activity: in the serial method, when bank X pays bank Z through bank Y, X sends an MT 103 to Y which sends an MT 103 to Z, so two messages are sent instead of one if the banks lack accounts with each other and use a third bank as intermediary. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "two messages are sent instead of one if banks do not have accounts with each other"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Available statistics do not allow identification of messages that are part of the same chain of payments, so the lengthening of payment chains cannot be measured accurately. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Available statistics do not allow identification of the messages that are part of the same chain of payments"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The absence of a major rise in global payment value is not inconsistent with longer chains because small and medium banks are more affected by CBR loss and process individual payments of smaller value; the average transfer by the median small bank is 60 times less than for the median large bank (USD 42,400 against 2.4 million), so rerouting smaller banks' payments through longer chains raises global volume more than global value. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the average value of an individual transfer by the median small bank is 60 times less"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Longer payment chains may reduce the efficiency of payment services, with additional delays and cost for sending funds; the FSB-CBCG survey did not examine this aspect. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These longer chains of payments may result in a reduction in the efficiency of payment services"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Long payment chains reflect 'downstream clearing' (sometimes called 'nesting'), described as an essential feature of correspondent banking: as banks cannot have accounts with all other banks, settling a transaction may involve several intermediary banks, as the CPMI illustrated in its 2016 report. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "as banks cannot have accounts with all other banks, the settling of a transaction may involve several intermediary banks"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In its revised correspondent banking guidance published 7 June 2017, BCBS noted that downstream CBRs are an integral and generally legitimate part of correspondent banking; nesting may help regional banks give small local banks in the respondent's region access to the international financial system or facilitate transactions where no direct relationship exists. However, providing access to third-party foreign financial institutions that are not the correspondent's customer, and so not necessarily known, can obscure financial transparency and increase ML/TF risks. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "downstream CBRs are an integral and generally legitimate part of correspondent banking."</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: BCBS recommends practices to adequately assess and manage the risks of nested (downstream) relationships. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "BCBS recommends practices to adequately assess and manage these risks."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: As noted in the BCBS revised guidance, respondent banks are expected to disclose whether accounts include nested relationships, but this does not entail producing a list of the nested relationships; hence only a small number of surveyed banks could give exact figures when the FSB-CBCG asked correspondents for the total number of banks provided accounts by the respondent that indirectly use the surveyed bank's services (to the extent known). (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "respondent banks are expected to disclose whether accounts include nested relationships"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: For the 26 banks that gave a 2016 figure, downstream relationships or accounts represented 72% of the number of their reported direct correspondents, showing downstream clearing is common; the 18 banks that gave data for 2012-2016 show a slight increase in the proportion of known downstream relationships. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "represented 72% of the number of the direct correspondents reported by these same banks"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Table 8 (indirect/downstream relationships versus direct relationships, 18 banks 2012-2016, and 26 banks for the last row): 2012 indirect 278, direct 375, indirect/direct 74%, 18 banks; 2013 indirect 307 (+10%), direct 377 (+1%), 81%; 2014 indirect 298 (-3%), direct 380 (+1%), 78%; 2015 indirect 292 (-2%), direct 376 (-1%), 78%; 2016 indirect 284 (-3%), direct 357 (-5%), 80%, 18 banks; 2016 for 26 banks: indirect 4453, direct 6181, 72%. Source: FSB-CBCG Survey. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Table 8 – Evolution of the number of indirect relationships (downstream or nested relationships)"</sub>

## 3.3 Concentration in correspondent banking

- **descriptive / context**: Greater concentration of the correspondent banking market raises remaining participants' market share and could affect competition, raise costs and above all make networks more fragile since a participant's failure would have larger effects on the market and economy; alternatively, consolidation could strengthen CBRs over the medium term as larger volumes address some business-related termination drivers cited in the survey. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "especially lead to more fragile networks since failure of a participant could have larger effects"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Aggregated payment volume rose steadily between 2011 and 2016 while the number of active correspondents fell continuously, suggesting concentration in the correspondent banking market increased over six years (Graph 18). The Gini coefficient on the number of active correspondents per corridor is high, between 0.766 and 0.769; it rose over 2012-2015 and was more stable over 2015-2016. Footnote 33: a Gini coefficient of 1 would mean only one active bank per corridor. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Gini coefficient on the number of active correspondents per corridor flows at high levels, between 0.766 and 0.76933"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The average value of USD transactions processed by correspondents has probably increased from 2011 to 2016, since the fall in the number of USD correspondents (Graph 4) came with an increase in value of USD payments. USD and EUR account for approximately 80% of the total value of MT 103 and MT 202 messages (excluding MT 202 COV); EUR payments follow a continuous downward trend, USD payments significantly increased their share, and GBP holds a stable share (Graph 19). (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "USD and EUR transactions account for approximately 80% of the total value of MT 103 and MT 202 payment messages"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Graph 20 shows USD payments rising 38% from 2011 to 2016 and EUR payments falling 30%; GBP payments and all other currencies show no discernible trend, ending the period slightly up by 3% and 8% respectively. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "increasing 38% from 2011 to 2016; whereas EUR payments follow a downward trend, with a decline of 30%"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The increase in the average value of USD transfers is not itself sufficient to establish a change in concentration, since market concentration depends on how the value is spread among the thousands of banks active in USD correspondent banking. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is not itself sufficient to establish a change in concentration"</sub>

## Concentration on the correspondent side

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Graph 21 shows the percentage of value sent and received through correspondent banks listed as the 30 G-SIBs, the remaining 45 large internationally active banks, or any other banks, in each of the three main currencies in 2016 (USD answers from 217 banks, EUR 175, GBP 105). The majority of value in each currency passes through one of the 30 G-SIBs, but it remains to be checked whether the G-SIB share is lower in the survey sample than the global average, which would suggest affected banks more often use smaller correspondents. EUR payments pass through the 45 large banks or other banks in greater proportion than USD or GBP, which may reflect that SWIFT message data in Europe also include payment-system messages. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the majority of value in each of the three main currencies is sent or received"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 34: other large internationally active banks are banks other than G-SIBs in the sample of 75 large global banking groups used in the G-SIB assessment methodology (end-2014 exercise main sample). Footnote 35: for this question banks were asked to consider direct relationships only, meaning those where the respondent has an account with the correspondent in the listed category. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "banks were asked to consider direct relationships only"</sub>

## Concentration on the respondent side

- **descriptive / limitation**: The survey gives the proportion of respondent banks depending on two or fewer correspondent banks for more than 75% of the value of cross-border payments (all currencies), but changes in dependence over time cannot be identified since this part covered only June 2016. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is not possible to identify changes in dependence over time"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Per Graph 22 (311 banks), 45% of surveyed banks depend on two or fewer correspondents for more than 75% of payment value; of these 93% were small and medium banks and 7% large banks, whereas among banks not so dependent 64% were small and medium and 36% large. There is evidence that large banks are less dependent on few correspondents than medium and small banks, and surveyed Latin America and Caribbean jurisdictions reported that 56% of their respondent banks depend on two or fewer correspondents. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Of these banks, 93% were small and medium banks and just 7% were large banks."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Respondent banks that depended on fewer correspondents in 2016 were those whose restrictions increased between 2011 and 2016: banks not depending on two or fewer correspondents had some restriction on 11% of CBRs in 2011 rising marginally to 15% in 2016, while those depending on two or fewer saw restrictions rise from 5% of relationships in 2011 to 29% in 2016. Footnote 37: 44 respondents answered both required questions; footnote 36 refers to the small/medium/large asset-size classification in section 2. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "from 5% of their relationships in 2011 to 29% in 2016"</sub>

## Information from other sources

- **descriptive / finding or result**: In the World Bank 2015 survey, banking authorities, large international banks and local/regional banks reporting declines in correspondent products were asked which were affected. Regardless of bank size/type, the top six affected services include check clearing, clearing and settlement, trade finance, cash management, investment services and international wire transfers; local/regional banks rank trade finance third most affected, while large international banks rank international wire transfers fourth. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "among the top six affected services are check clearing, clearing and settlement services, trade finance"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Banking authorities perceive a much larger effect on international wire transfers than do banks (local/regional and large international), which see check clearing and clearing and settlement as the main affected products. Graphs 23 and 24 (affected products/services by respondent type) have no legible values in the excerpt. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "banking authorities perceive a much larger effect on international wire transfers than do banks"</sub>

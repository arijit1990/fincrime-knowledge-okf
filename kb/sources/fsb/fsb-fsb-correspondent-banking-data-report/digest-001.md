## FSB Correspondent Banking Data Report

- **descriptive / context**: The report is dated 4 July 2017. The FSB is established to coordinate internationally the work of national financial authorities and standard setting bodies; its activities, including decisions reached, are not binding and do not give rise to any legal rights or obligations under the FSB's Articles of Association. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "shall not be binding or give rise to any legal rights or obligations under the FSB’s Articles of Association"</sub>

## Executive summary

- **descriptive / definition**: The Committee on Payments and Market Infrastructures (CPMI) defines correspondent banking as an arrangement under which one bank (the correspondent) holds deposits owned by other banks (respondents) and provides payment and other services to them. The report notes several definitions exist. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an arrangement under which one bank (correspondent) holds deposits owned by other banks (respondents)"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The FSB Correspondent Banking Coordination Group (FSB-CBCG), whose mandate is filling gaps in knowledge on the decline in correspondent banking relationships (CBRs), its causes and effects, conducted through national authorities a survey of 345 banks in 48 jurisdictions, including large correspondent banks processing a substantial part of international customer payments and a sample of smaller banks, especially from affected jurisdictions. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a survey of 345 banks in 48 jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: SWIFT also provided, via the National Bank of Belgium (SWIFT's overseer) and Deutsche Bundesbank (Chair of the CPMI Working Group on Correspondent Banking), an end-2016 update of the aggregated, anonymised dataset given to CPMI for its July 2016 correspondent banking report; it covers 6 years (2011-2016) and contains sent and received volumes (number of messages), nominal payment values and number of active correspondents for each corridor (a unidirectional country pair). (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "contain sent and received volumes (which means the number of messages) and nominal values of payments"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 2: the count of active correspondents measures, corridor by corridor, the number of banks that sent or received messages through SWIFT; a bank active in several corridors is counted several times, so a fall in the count may reflect the same banks being active in fewer corridors, and correspondents active across several corridors may exchange messages without having an account relationship. Footnote 3: unidirectional means country pair A to B and B to A are differentiated. Footnote 4: an active corridor has at least one SWIFT transaction in either direction. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A bank active in several corridors is counted several times"</sub>

## Decrease in correspondent banking relationships continues

- **descriptive / finding or result**: According to SWIFT data, between beginning-2011 and end-2016 the number of active corridors fell by 6.3% and the number of active correspondents by 6% across all currencies; for the US dollar (USD) and euro (EUR) the number of active correspondents fell by around 15% over the same period. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the number of active corridors4 decreased by 6.3% and the number of active correspondents by 6% across all currencies"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: USD and EUR make up about a third of the number of correspondent accounts but the vast majority of the value of payments made through SWIFT messages (82% in December 2016), against 5% for the next most used currency, the British pound (GBP); the decline in active correspondents for GBP is in line with the overall -6% trend. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they represent the vast majority of the value of payments made through SWIFT messages (82% in December 2016)"</sub>

## Executive summary

- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 1 notes that definitions of correspondent banking differ: the ECB correspondent banking survey limits it to "payment services"; the Wolfsberg Group, an industry body, refers to a "current or other liability account, and related services"; and the FATF takes a broad view, describing it as one bank (the correspondent) providing banking services to another bank (the respondent). (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF defines correspondent banking broadly as the provision of banking services by one bank"</sub>

## Decrease in correspondent banking relationships continues

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Over 2011-2016 the number of active correspondents by SWIFT message traffic trended downward in most regions; Africa had a slight increase between 2011 and 2014 but after 2013-2014 all regions declined continuously. Most affected regions by percentage change: Eastern Europe (-16%), Europe excluding Eastern Europe (-15%), Oceania (-12%) and the Americas excluding North America (-8%). (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Eastern Europe (-16%), followed by Europe excluding Eastern Europe (-15%), Oceania (-12%), and the Americas excluding North America (-8%)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Both the FSB-CBCG survey and SWIFT data show the decline in CBRs continued in 2016: all regions except Southern Asia saw a reduction in the average number of active correspondents across currencies. The Caribbean and the small states of the Pacific (Melanesia, Micronesia, Polynesia) are the four sub-regions with the highest decline rates, close to or above 10% in 2016. Footnote 5 lists Southern Asia (UN Statistics Division) as Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Iran, Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan and Sri Lanka. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "all regions except Southern Asia5 have seen a reduction in the average number of active correspondents across all currencies"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Pacific regions already have the smallest number of active correspondents, so the decline matters more there than in Europe, which had a high rate of decline but still has the highest number of CBRs; Europe's lower count largely reflects fewer banks after post-crisis consolidation (for instance a 9.2% reduction in the number of EU banks between 2012 and 2015, per the ECB) and the use of European-wide payment systems. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a reduction by 9.2% of the number of banks in the European Union (EU) between 2012 and 2015"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Providers of correspondent banking services are concentrated in North America, Europe and East Asia & Pacific; other regions appear only marginal providers. Per the CBCG survey, provision of CBR by East Asia & Pacific banks keeps growing modestly but is mostly in the provider's domestic currency, and these providers did not significantly replace the reduction in USD and EUR accounts from North America and Europe. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Providers of correspondent banking services are concentrated in North America, Europe and East Asia & Pacific."</sub>

## Effects of the withdrawal: Lengthening of payment chains and increasing reliance on fewer correspondent banks

- **descriptive / method**: An important objective of the FSB-CBCG survey was to identify potential vulnerabilities, notably where countries or banks depend on a small number of correspondent banks for access to the international financial system. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries or banks depend on a small number of correspondent banks for their access to the international financial system"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: For the 48 other jurisdictions, which have CBRs with 3 surveyed banks or fewer, additional research would be necessary to determine the extent to which they may be served by correspondent banks not covered by the survey or have access to domestic or regional payment systems. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "additional research would be necessary to determine the extent to which these jurisdictions may be served by correspondent banks"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: From January 2011 to June 2016, the average number of surveyed banks serving a country fell by 9.6%, to 16 banks. Of the jurisdictions, 180 are served by at least 4 foreign correspondent banks that responded to the FSB-CBCG survey. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "has declined by 9.6% to 16 banks from January 2011 to June 2016"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The reduction in CBRs leaves banks and countries relying on fewer providers, generally Global Systemically Important Banks (G-SIBs). The number of terminated CBRs is higher than the net reduction in CBRs, possibly because banks replace part of the relationships lost; a high gross termination rate illustrates the potential risk of relying on too few CBRs when they are critical to a bank's operations. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the number of terminated CBRs is higher than the net reduction of the number of CBRs"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: As CPMI noted in July 2016, globally the decline in active correspondents has not lowered the number or underlying value of messages processed through SWIFT; the number of payments rose by 36% between 2011 and 2016. This is not always true at country level: between 2015 and 2016, 42 jurisdictions had both fewer active correspondents and lower volume of sent and received messages, and 30 of them also saw a reduction in average value sent and received. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the number of payments has increased between 2011 and 2016 by 36%"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The rise in message numbers may reflect lengthening payment chains rather than more actual customer wire transfers: as large clearing banks reduced correspondent networks, more intermediaries may be needed to connect two banks, requiring more messages. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the number of intermediaries needed to connect two banks may have increased, which would require a larger number of messages"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Available statistics do not allow identification of messages belonging to the same payment chain, so the lengthening of payment chains cannot be measured accurately; several indicators support the hypothesis. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the lengthening of payment chains cannot be measured accurately"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Indicators of lengthening chains: 25% of surveyed banks reported changing their correspondent arrangements since 2011 so payments flow through different countries; and the lack of a major rise in global payment value despite a significant rise in volume fits small and medium banks (more affected by CBR loss, typically processing smaller payments) being rerouted through longer chains. The average individual transfer by a median small bank is 60 times less than for a median large bank (USD 42,400 against USD 2.4 million). (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the average value of an individual transfer by a median small bank in the survey sample is 60 times less"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The FSB-CBCG survey did not examine in detail other consequences such as on trade finance or end-customers, which are covered by other surveys. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "did not examine in detail other consequences such as on trade finance or on end-customers"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: At bank level, the survey shows that 45% of surveyed banks relied, as of June 2016, on no more than two correspondents for over 75% of the value of wire transfers they sent or received. These banks were mostly small and medium-sized by assets and were more vulnerable to restrictions on CBRs. The share with the same reliance on two or fewer correspondents is above 50% in Latin America and the Caribbean, and this dependence appears to result at least in part from more severe CBR restrictions experienced by those banks. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "45% of surveyed banks reported relying on two or fewer correspondents for more than 75% of the value"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: For higher-risk products or customers, respondent banks reported terminating services to 'most' Money Transfer Operators (MTOs) at least 70% more often than other types of higher risk clients, in line with the World Bank 2015 report on de-risking in the remittance market. Footnote 6: the FATF instead uses the term Money or value transfer services (MVTS), defined in the FATF Glossary as financial services involving acceptance of cash, cheques, other monetary instruments or other stores of value and payment of a corresponding sum to a beneficiary by communication, message, transfer or a clearing network to which the MVTS provider belongs, including MTOs and Money Service Businesses (MSBs). (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "at least 70% more often than other types of higher risk clients"</sub>

## Drivers of withdrawal are intertwined and vary depending on size of economy

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Small economies are on average the most affected by the reduction in foreign correspondent banks: the 15 largest economies saw only a 3% reduction in surveyed banks offering correspondent services to banks there, against a 29% decline for the 55 economies with GDP below USD 10 billion, which were on average served by only 4 surveyed banks. This may suggest insufficient business volume deters correspondents given the fixed costs of opening and maintaining a relationship. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a decline of 29% for the 55 economies with a GDP of less than USD 10 billion"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: FATF compliance status, or lack of information on it, appears to affect the average 'exit rate' of correspondent banks (January 2011 to June 2016). Afghanistan and Cambodia, publicly identified by the FATF as of February 2014 as having made insufficient progress on AML/CFT deficiencies, had an average exit rate of 40% (footnote 7: -57% for Afghanistan and -23% for Cambodia), similar to the 43% for the 20 jurisdictions never assessed by FATF as of February 2017. Banks in the two jurisdictions facing a call for action (North Korea and Iran) lost 79% of their correspondent banks in the survey sample over the period. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The banks in the two jurisdictions (North Korea and Iran) facing a call for action lost 79%"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The 20 jurisdictions simply under FATF monitoring in 2011 were less affected than the world average (-12% against -18%) and those under monitoring in 2014 had the same exit rate as the world average, which could suggest that when deficiencies are addressed in a timely manner they have no lasting impact on banks' access to the international financial system. Footnote 8: timely resolution is assumed for countries that exited monitoring without entering the categories described in the preceding paragraph. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "were less affected than the world’s average (-12% against -18%)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Banks were asked to quantify exits attributable to different drivers; drivers are generally intertwined and a combination of causes may explain termination of the same CBR. Business reasons not directly related to the costs of the terminated CBR (changes to business model or strategy, termination of dormant relationships, industry consolidation) are the primary drivers cited by both correspondent and respondent banks. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "business reasons not directly related to the costs of the terminated CBR"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Lack of profitability, overall risk appetite and various drivers related to AML/CFT or sanctions compliance are mentioned equally frequently by correspondent banks. Respondents cite AML/CFT-related drivers less frequently than correspondents; small respondents tend to be more affected by terminations for profitability reasons, and dormant CBRs are less frequently cited in their case. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Respondents cite AML/CFT related drivers less frequently than correspondents."</sub>

## Responses to the withdrawal predominantly oriented to address AML/CFT concerns

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The survey shows that measures by authorities in response to the decline are predominantly oriented to AML/CFT concerns, mainly implementing or enhancing the AML/CFT legal framework, harmonizing domestic regulation with international standards and recommendations, and making information requirements more stringent. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "measures taken are predominantly oriented to address AML/CFT concerns"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The decline of CBRs is a global phenomenon of varying intensity; it increases concentration in the correspondent banking market, which may be a natural response to the profitability issue and may facilitate monitoring of CBRs, but could lead to structural instabilities in affected jurisdictions' payment traffic, which is why the FSB-CBCG is addressing the issue further. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this concentration could lead to structural instabilities in the payment traffic of affected jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FSB will publish in late 2017 a complement to the report deepening analysis of survey responses, especially from respondent banks' perspective, and updating the SWIFT data analysis (data as per end of June 2017); the FSB-CBCG will continue exploring ways to give policy makers and if possible the public relevant information from the datasets, subject to confidentiality constraints. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FSB will publish in late 2017 a complement to this report"</sub>

## 1. Introduction

- **descriptive / context**: A decline in CBRs concerns the international community because in affected jurisdictions it may impair the ability to send and receive international payments or push some payment flows into the unregulated sector, with potential consequences for international trade, growth, financial inclusion and the stability and integrity of the financial system. Data is essential to shape policy responses and prioritise technical assistance; the FSB-CBCG was mandated to identify and address knowledge gaps. Footnote 10: the FSB action plan to assess and address the decline in correspondent banking was published in November 2015. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "drive some payment flows into the unregulated sector"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The FSB-CBCG reviewed existing analyses, surveys and data, identified empirical questions as an organising principle, and designed a survey template implemented on a voluntary basis by domestic authorities and banks; SWIFT's aggregated anonymised dataset complements it, and the two sources are complemented by other data as necessary. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "implemented by domestic authorities and banks in their jurisdictions on a voluntary basis"</sub>

## 1.1 FSB-CBCG survey methodology

- **descriptive / data or feature**: The survey involved 50 jurisdictions in total; two provided only general information or part 1 responses and are not covered. Forty-eight provided responses to the other parts, two of which gave aggregate responses used where data characteristics allowed. (Footnote 13: the BiH Federation and Republica Srpska reported separately and both count in the 48.) (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The survey conducted by the FSB-CBCG involved in total 50 jurisdictions."</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Sample objective 1: include countries with large providers of correspondent banking services and jurisdictions that reported or were likely to experience a decline, based on the World Bank 2015 survey Withdrawal from Correspondent Banking: Where, Why, and What to Do About it, IMF surveillance and Table 1 of the July 2016 CPMI report. For scale, 57 banks in the sample sent over 17 million MT 103 messages in June 2016, which is 40% of SWIFT messages of that category that month. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "57 banks in the sample taken together sent over 17 million of MT 103 messages in June 2016"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Sample objective 2: geographic diversity; Europe tends to be overrepresented, consistent with the large correspondent banking activity in Europe shown by SWIFT data. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Europe tends to be overrepresented in the sample as shown in Table 1"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: The sample includes 13 large economies (>USD 1,000 billion, current 2015 GDP), 19 medium ones and 16 small economies; the small-economy threshold and the sentence that follows are garbled in the excerpt, which then continues with 'Guatemala and Poland' next to a footnote 11 marker (apparently the two jurisdictions not covered). (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The sample includes 13 large economies (>USD 1,000 billion, current 2015 Gross Domestic Product - GDP), 19 medium ones"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Table 1 lists countries of participating banks by World Bank region: East Asia and Pacific (7 of 38): Australia, Hong Kong SAR, Indonesia, Japan, Philippines, Samoa, Singapore. Europe and Central Asia (19 of 58): Albania, Bosnia and Herzegovina (both BiH Federation and Republica Srpska), Finland, France, Germany, Iceland, Ireland, Italy, Latvia, Lithuania, Netherlands, Portugal, Russian Federation, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, Turkey, United Kingdom, Uzbekistan. Latin America and the Caribbean (8 of 42): Argentina, the Bahamas, Barbados, Brazil, British Virgin Islands, Colombia, Mexico, Peru. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Australia, Hong Kong SAR, Indonesia, Japan, Philippines, Samoa, Singapore"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The FSB-CBCG survey had three parts: (1) a short section for authorities on the size of their banking sector and their regulatory environment; (2) questions for banks about their arrangements as clients and as providers of correspondent banking services, including restrictions or terminations; and (3) a quantitative section in which banks supplied monthly values and volumes of cross-border payments sent and received. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a quantitative section for banks to provide monthly values and volumes of cross-border payments sent and received"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Table 1 continued: Middle East and North Africa (5 of 21): Jordan, Kuwait, Morocco, Qatar, West Bank & Gaza. North America (3 of 3): Bermuda, Canada, United States. South Asia (2 of 8): India, Pakistan. Sub-Saharan Africa (3 of 48): Ghana, Mauritius, South Africa. Footnote 12: regions follow the World Bank analytical grouping; some banks' responses may include branches and subsidiaries of foreign banks. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Jordan, Kuwait, Morocco, Qatar, West Bank & Gaza"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: For the bank sample within each country, authorities were invited to include all G-SIBs headquartered in their jurisdictions and other major providers of correspondent banking services, and, for emerging markets and developing economies, a representative sample of banks (where possible at least six). (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "authorities were invited to include all G-SIBs that are headquartered in their jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In at least 30 of the 48 jurisdictions with bank-level responses, reporting banks represent more than 50% of domestic banking system assets (Graph 1). (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In at least 30 out of the 48 jurisdictions that provided bank level responses"</sub>

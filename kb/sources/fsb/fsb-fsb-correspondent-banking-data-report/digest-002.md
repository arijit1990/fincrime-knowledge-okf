## Banking sector coverage1

- **descriptive / data or feature**: Graph 1 (banking sector coverage) shows participation as a percentage of banking sector assets and the number of reported banks; the chart values are not legible in the excerpt. Percentage of banking sector assets was not provided for Ireland and the British Virgin Islands; for example, in Albania twelve banks participated and their assets cover 100% of the Albanian banking sector. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Percentage of banking sector assets was not provided for Ireland and British Virgin Islands."</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: For 46 of the 48 surveyed jurisdictions the FSB-CBCG collected, through local competent authorities, anonymised individual responses from 334 banks: 139 reported only as respondent banks, 22 only as correspondent banks, 129 as both, and 44 could not be identified as either because the information was insufficient to determine their role. Footnote 14: only responses received before May 3rd, 2016 were used, excluding Swiss and US banks; footnote 15: role is based on the overview-of-accounts question, and banks that did not answer it may still have responded to other questions in some role. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "139 reported only as respondent banks, 22 reported only as correspondent banks, 129 banks reported as both"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The US, highly significant on the correspondent side, gave aggregate and limited responses rather than individual anonymised ones, due to legal impediments; these lacked the granularity to compare with other countries, could not be included in much of the analysis, and affected the sample's representativeness. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "has provided aggregate and limited responses rather than individual anonymised responses"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Switzerland, also significant on the correspondent side, provided aggregate responses with distributional information on eight surveyed banks; because aggregation prevented associating individual bank characteristics with the information, its responses were only incorporated where data characteristics allowed. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "provided aggregate responses along with distributional information regarding eight surveyed banks"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The number of bank responses varied by question, so the report notes the number of usable responses throughout. In part III of the survey (monthly transaction data by corridor and currency), only 138 banks gave data covering at least 5 years at the requested granularity, 82 gave the requested granularity but for a shorter period, 69 gave more aggregated data that reduced the scope of analysis, and 44 did not respond to that part. For these reasons transactions data is not analysed in detail, but the FSB-CBCG will continue working on it, particularly for jurisdictions that reported high-quality information with good coverage, for instance to inform the development of ongoing monitoring mechanisms of correspondent banking. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "only 138 banks were able to provide data covering at least 5 years with the requested granularity"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Surveyed banks were classified into three groups by reported asset size (Table 2) using a statistical algorithm: 96 small, 153 medium and 71 large, with the remaining banks unclassified because they did not report assets. Classification is global rather than jurisdictional because banks large globally may be less likely to suffer relationship terminations even if relatively small locally, due to fixed costs and absolute profit levels. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "96 banks were classified as small, 153 as medium, and 71 as large"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Footnote 16: the algorithm is a k-means clustering over the natural logarithm of banks' reported financial assets, optimising group boundaries to minimise the within-group sum of squares so banks in each group are statistically nearest in asset size. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "follows a k-means clustering algorithm over the natural logarithm of the reported financial assets of banks"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Table 2 gives distributional statistics (number of currencies, number of corridors, volume and value) by asset-size group; the table's numbers are not present in the excerpt. As a reading example, one quarter of the 35 small banks (asset size below 1.7 billion) process wire transfers in 3 currencies or fewer and 3 corridors or fewer, with a yearly average of fewer than 1,800 transfers received, for a total value below USD 280 million. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "process wire transfers in only 3 currencies or less, in 3 corridors or less"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Table 2 note a: corridor and currency figures are based on 2016 transactional data; value and volume figures are totals from June 2015 to June 2016; information was assessed from 123 banks (35 small, 72 medium, 16 large). Footnote 17: the subset includes only banks that provided sufficient information. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Information assessed from 123 banks that provided the necessary information: 35 small banks, 72 medium banks and 16 large banks."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The median number of currencies, and of corridors, transferred (sent or received) rises with bank size: large banks transfer funds in more currencies and corridors than small and medium banks. Median volume operated by small banks is approximately 40% of medium banks' volume and only 3 to 4% of large banks'; small and medium banks are closer to each other than to large banks. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "small banks represent approximately 40% of the medium banks’ volume"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: By value transferred the gaps between groups are wider: small banks represent 5% of the value sent and 1% of the value received by medium banks, and 0.06% of value sent and 0.13% of value received compared with large banks. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to 0.06% for value sent, and 0.13% for value received"</sub>

## 1.2 SWIFT Data18

- **descriptive / data or feature**: SWIFT provided an anonymised, aggregated dataset similar to the one CPMI analysed in July 2016, with one additional year (now 2011 to 2016). Footnote 18: SWIFT data is published with permission of S.W.I.F.T. SCRL; SWIFT message flows are not complete market or industry statistics, SWIFT disclaims liability for decisions based on its statistics, the analysis was prepared by National Bank of Belgium staff (which does not guarantee accuracy or necessarily share the findings), and significant input came from Deutsche Bundesbank and the Bank of Mexico (which chairs the CBCG Workstream on Data Collection and Analysis). (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "covering an additional year of data (i.e. now covering 2011 to 2016)"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: The SWIFT dataset includes message types MT 103 (single customer credit transfers, by which an institution instructs another to transfer funds for a single customer's benefit) and MT 202 (general financial institution transfers, used to request movement of funds between institutions not related to an underlying customer credit transfer), plus subtypes. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "message types MT 103 (single customer credit transfers"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: The dataset holds sent and received volumes (numbers of messages) and nominal values per corridor (a unidirectional country pair), the number of active correspondents in each corridor, and the currencies, volumes and nominal values per message type for each corridor. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "as well as data on the number of active correspondents in each corridor"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The SWIFT data gives a worldwide view complementing the more detailed but narrower FSB-CBCG survey: it covers more than 200 countries and territories, and since SWIFT is the most commonly used messaging standard for cross-border payments it captures a meaningful amount of correspondent banking activity and gives an accurate picture of actual payment traffic between jurisdictions. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the SWIFT data set comprises more than 200 countries and territories"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: SWIFT limitation 1: institutions have multiple means to exchange information (for example transfers between offices of the same bank group may use bank-specific messaging systems), so SWIFT statistics do not represent complete market or industry statistics. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "SWIFT statistics on financial flows do not represent complete market or industry statistics"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: SWIFT limitation 2: for confidentiality, data for corridors with fewer than four transactions or four correspondents were excluded; this mainly affects the cumulative value of transactions, not whether a corridor is active, so an active corridor is one with at least one transaction in either direction (for the relevant month, currency or message type). Footnote 19: MT 202 COV messages (cover method) were excluded to avoid double counting with MT 103, which goes directly to the final recipient's institution under the cover method. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "underlying data for corridors with fewer than four transactions or four correspondents were not included"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: SWIFT limitation 3: nominal values were converted to US dollars using daily exchange rates, so changes in value may partly reflect exchange-rate changes. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "changes in the value may partially reflect changes in the exchange rate"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: SWIFT limitation 4: the data do not differentiate payments cleared via correspondent banking arrangements from those sent via transnational financial market infrastructures such as TARGET2 in Europe. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "do not differentiate payments cleared via correspondent banking arrangements"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: SWIFT limitation 5: a payment message generally implies an account relationship between sender and receiver under the serial method, but not under the cover method, where a bank exchanges MT 103 with banks with which it has no account relationship; a count of 'correspondents' from messages may therefore exceed one based on accounts. Use of the cover method, evidenced by MT 202 COV volumes (Graph 2), appears stable over the period at least globally. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In the cover method, a bank exchanges MT 103 with banks with which it has no account relationships"</sub>

## Monthly transaction volume by message type

- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 21, quoting the CPMI July 2016 report: in the serial method payment information and settlement instruction travel together in the MT 103 and a direct account relationship exists between each connected pair of banks in the chain; in the cover method settlement is decoupled from payment information, the MT 103 going directly via SWIFT from originating to receiving bank while the settlement instruction (cover payment) goes through intermediary banks via direct correspondent banking relationships. Graph 2 (monthly transaction volume by message type, in millions) has no legible values in the excerpt. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the cover method decouples the settlement from the payment information"</sub>

## 2. Scale of the withdrawal from correspondent banking

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Section 2 assesses global and regional evolution using SWIFT and survey data; in line with previous surveys a significant reduction in the number of CBRs is observed for certain regions, though the extent varies significantly within regions. SWIFT monthly data (Graph 3) show the decline in active corridors and active correspondents continued in 2016, an active corridor being a country pair that processed at least one transaction. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a significant reduction in the number of CBRs can be observed for certain regions"</sub>

## 2.1 SWIFT data

- **descriptive / definition**: The count of active correspondents measures, corridor by corridor, banks that sent or received messages, so correspondents active in several corridors are counted several times; this explains the roughly 470,000 active correspondents at end-2016 in Graph 3 against approximately 11,000 banks connected to SWIFT. Footnote 22: the data are at BIC8 level (branch/subsidiary level depending on legal set-up) and the graph also multiplies activity across currencies and message types. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "correspondents active in more than one corridor are counted several times"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: From 2011 to 2016 there is a clear downward trend in monthly active corridors and active correspondents, more acute from 2014: active corridors fell 6.3% (from 13,072 to 12,242) and active correspondents 6% (from 510,619 to 479,947). The slight upward movement or stabilisation at end-2016 may not be significant as December usually sees increased activity. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a reduction of 6.3% of active corridors (from 13,072 to 12,242)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Active correspondents declined for the three most used international payment currencies (USD, EUR, GBP; Graph 4): GBP's decline is similar to the overall -6% over 2011-2016, while USD and EUR both fell around -15%. Footnote 23: these results used an additional high-level dataset that avoids double accounting of correspondents. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the decline is more acute for USD and EUR, both around -15%"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The USD and EUR declines do not further affect the all-currency evolution because the two currencies represent only about a third of the number of correspondent accounts; but they represent the vast majority of the value of international wire transfers (82% as of December 2016, Table 3), so a reduction in these relationships may be more acutely felt. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these two currencies only represent about a third of the number of correspondent accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Table 3 (share of the three main currencies in value of SWIFT transfers, MT 103 and MT 202 excluding MT 202 COV, December 2016, and in number of accounts reported by correspondents in the FSB-CBCG survey, June 2016), as legible from a garbled header: USD 53.49% of value, 7,059 accounts, 14% of accounts; EUR 28.67% of value, 8,702 accounts, 17%; GBP 4.52% of value, 2,421 accounts, 5%; All 100% of value, 51,553 accounts, 100%. Two vostro-currency columns are empty in the excerpt. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Table 3 Share of the three main currencies in the value of transfers in SWIFT messages"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: By region (Graph 5), the number of active correspondents trended down for all regions over 2011-2016; some regions rose slightly early on, especially Africa between 2011 and 2014, but after 2013-2014 all regions declined continuously. Most affected: Eastern Europe (-16%), Europe excluding Eastern Europe (-15%), Oceania (-12%), Americas excluding North America (-8%). Graph 4 and 5 footnote: correspondents are counted multiple times across corridors but not across message types and months. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "After 2013–2014, all regions experienced a continuous decline."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The number of active corridors fell in all regions over 2011-2016 (Graph 6), at varying rates. Measured as change in the average number of counterparty countries (average corridors per jurisdiction in a region), Oceania declined most (15%), then Africa (9%) and the Americas excluding North America (7%), broadly consistent with survey results on correspondents completely exiting whole jurisdictions. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Oceania was the region that experienced the greatest decline in the number of corridors per jurisdiction"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Graphs 7 and 8 show changes for 5 continents and 22 UN Statistics Division regions for all currencies (USD, EUR, GBP versions in annex 1). Unlike Graph 3a of the July 2016 CPMI report they use yearly rather than monthly data and remove double counting of correspondents across months, message types and currencies, giving a more accurate picture over six years. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The data set used removes double counting of correspondents over months, message types and currencies"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Graph 7 (active correspondents based on sent messages) counts banks in a region sending messages abroad (including within the region) totalled across corridors, so a bank active in two corridors counts twice; this is the perspective of respondent banks sending payment instructions, correspondents forwarding them, or, under the cover method, the originating bank informing the receiving bank. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "count the number of banks located in the stated region that send messages abroad"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Graph 8 (active correspondents based on received messages) counts banks in a region receiving messages from abroad (including within the region) totalled across corridors, again counting a bank active in two corridors twice; this is the perspective of correspondents receiving instructions from respondents, respondents being forwarded instructions, or, under the cover method, the receiving bank being informed a customer is receiving a payment. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "count the number of banks located in the stated region that receive messages from abroad"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: A reduced count of active correspondents can mean fewer banks in the region send or receive messages, for instance from a reduction in the number of banks (mergers, loss of banking licence) or a bank using a messaging system other than SWIFT (for instance an intragroup system). (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A reduction in the number of banks in the region (mergers between banks, loss of banking licence, …)"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: A reduced count can also mean banks in the region deal with fewer countries (active in fewer corridors), for example when a bank with correspondent X in country A and Y and Z in country B loses X, or when a bank moves from the cover method (sending MT 103s directly to final recipients' banks, presumably in many countries) to the serial method (sending only to banks where it has an account, likely in fewer countries). (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "If a bank that was using the cover payment method moves to the serial payment method"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: For all currencies (Graph 7), all 22 regions except Southern Asia saw a reduction in the average number of active correspondents in 2016, and a majority (15) saw an acceleration of the decline. The decline for USD local respondents affects all regions in 2016; for EUR and GBP most regions also decline, the exceptions being, for EUR, Central America, Southern Asia and Micronesia, and, for GBP, very modest increases in Southern Africa, Northern America, Central Asia, Eastern Asia and Western Asia. Footnote 24 lists Southern Asia (UN) as Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Iran, Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan and Sri Lanka. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A majority (15) have seen an acceleration of the decline in 2016."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Graph 8 (received messages) shows all regions declining in 2016, generally at a similar or faster pace than 2015, except South America, South Eastern Asia, Southern Europe and Melanesia where decline slowed (though remaining high in Melanesia). For USD there are fewer foreign correspondents in all regions in 2016 than 2015, with deceleration in Northern and Southern Africa, South America, Central and Western Asia and a similar pace for most European regions, South Eastern Asia and Australia and New Zealand. For EUR the number fell for all regions except Micronesia, where the small number of correspondents may explain large variations; for GBP about half the regions see a small increase or stable situation. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "For all regions, there are also fewer foreign correspondents"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Across all currencies, the six regions with a slower pace of decline in active correspondents are primarily Southern Africa, Central and South America and Central Asia, and to a lesser extent Northern and Southern Europe. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The 6 regions that have seen a slower pace of decline across all currencies"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report calls the situation all the more concerning because Melanesia, Micronesia and Polynesia (not the Caribbean) are already the regions with the smallest number of active correspondents; it notes the IMF working paper 'Challenges in Correspondent Banking in the Small States of the Pacific' also highlights that many countries there have few commercial banks present, and that the FSB-CBCG survey confirms this. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Melanesia, Micronesia and Polynesia are already the regions with the smallest number of active correspondents"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The report notes that technical factors, such as banking consolidation or a move to payment systems, may have affected the SWIFT statistics on the decline in active correspondents. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "some technical factors, such as banking consolidation or a move to payment systems, may however have impacted SWIFT statistics"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Besides the Caribbean and small Pacific states, the regions with highest decline rates by sent messages (Graph 7) are Northern Africa and European regions, but Europe has the highest average number of correspondents and its reduction largely reflects post-crisis banking consolidation: Graph 9 shows EU credit institutions fell from 3610 to 3278 between 2012 and 2015, a 9.2% reduction. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the number of credit institutions in the EU went down from 3610 to 3278"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: CPMI noted in July 2016 that Europe's decline could also be explained by some large banks starting to move from correspondent banking to payment systems for low-volume/high-value payments following introduction of the Single European Payment Area (SEPA). Footnote 25: Accuity notes the EU's correspondent banking relationships dropped significantly from 126,502 in 2011 to 91,262 in 2012, primarily due to SEPA completion (intra-EU payments no longer cross-border), and continued falling to 70292 in 2016. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "some large banks starting to move away from correspondent banking to payment systems for low-volume/high-value payments"</sub>

## 2.2 Data on individual CBRs from the FSB-CBCG survey

- **descriptive / data or feature**: Annex 2 gives per country the number of surveyed correspondents that stated having exited the country since 2011, the number applying other restrictions (such as dealing only with central banks, major local banks or subsidiaries of foreign banks), and the number still serving it. '% exit' is exits divided by the estimated number of correspondents serving the country in 2011 (approximated by CBRs as of June 2016 plus full exits January 2011 to June 2016); Annex 2 also gives total accounts that correspondents hold with banks in the country and details for the three main currencies. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the proportion of exits (“% exit”) is the number of exits divided by the estimated number of correspondents"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Data from some 150 banks providing over 50,000 correspondent accounts were used to compute remaining relationships; although a significant proportion of the market, the numbers should be taken with caution since banks outside the sample may still serve a country. US and Swiss data were aggregated so their presence in a jurisdiction was counted as one though there could be several, and respondent banks in some territories (especially non-independent ones) may not be headquartered there, underestimating relationships with that territory. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the number should be taken with caution: other banks outside the sample may still serve that country"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: On average the 228 countries and territories in SWIFT data are served by 16 surveyed banks, down 9.6% from 2011; across a broader universe of 252 countries and non-independent territories the number fell 18% to 14 banks. Half of jurisdictions have an account relationship with at least 10 banks, 180 jurisdictions are served by at least 4 banks, and the 48 served by 3 or fewer surveyed banks are listed in Table 4 (whose rows are not in the excerpt, only its title). (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The 48 jurisdictions served by 3 surveyed banks or less are listed in Table 4"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A higher number of accounts reflects a correspondent with several respondents in the country, accounts in several currencies, or both; the FSB-CBCG will conduct additional research on whether low-coverage jurisdictions are served by correspondents outside the survey or have access to domestic or regional payment systems. Table 4 is titled as jurisdictions served in June 2016 by fewer than 4 surveyed banks, ranked by increasing number of relationships and accounts. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A higher number of accounts reflects that the correspondent has several respondents in the country"</sub>

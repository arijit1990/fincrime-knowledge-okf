## 3.5 Consequences for specific types of banks’ end-customers

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The withdrawal of CBRs has affected particular service providers that serve more vulnerable socioeconomic sectors, such as Money Transfer Operators (MTOs) and Non-Government Organizations (NGOs). The FSB-CBCG survey collected information on how respondents terminated services to some end-customers (customers of financial institutions, per footnote 38), particularly NGOs, MTOs, Payment Service Providers (PSPs), Politically Exposed Persons (PEPs) and other Financial Institutions (FIs). (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The withdrawal of CBRs has affected some particular service providers that cater to specific socioeconomic sectors"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Graph 25 measures termination intensity of end-customers: respondent banks said whether they terminated relationships with 'none', 'some' or 'most' of each type of end-customer for each reason, and the graph shows the frequency of banks answering 'some' and 'most'. For example, 37 banks terminated services with 'some' NGO clients while only five did so with 'most' of their NGO clients (118 banks answered; chart values otherwise not legible). (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "37 banks reported having terminated services with “some” of their clients who are non-governmental or humanitarian organizations (NGOs)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Banks reported terminating services to 'most' MTOs at least 70% more often than other types of clients, in line with the World Bank 2015 report on de-risking in the remittance market. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "terminating services to “most” MTOs at least 70% more often than other types of clients"</sub>

## Information from other sources

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The World Bank 2015 survey on de-risking in the remittance market found MTOs particularly affected: 28% of respondent MTOs said both the MTO principal and its agents can no longer access banking services, and 17% said the principal may still hold bank accounts but its agents are not allowed to. Of the 28% without access, 74% stay in the market using alternative channels to clear and settle internationally, while the remaining 26% of MTO principal respondents reported being unable to operate regularly through bank channels. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "28% of the total respondent MTOs declared that both the MTO principal and its agents can no longer access banking services"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The World Bank 2015 survey on withdrawal from correspondent banking indicates that over 69% of banking authorities report money transfer operators and other remittance companies among the most affected by the decline, followed by small and medium domestic banks (44%) and small and medium exporters (26%). Some local/regional banks reported money exchange businesses, tour operators, the food and drink industry, exporters, importers and PEPs as significantly affected. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "over 69% of banking authorities report that money transfer operators and other remittance companies are among the most affected"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In the same World Bank survey 18% of banking authorities considered a higher-risk customer base a cause of the decline; other client segments they saw as significantly affected due to higher risk were retail customers (students, foreign workers, etc.), international business companies, e-gaming/casino businesses and foreign exchange (FX) services. Footnote 39: methods MTOs reported using are operating through other MTOs, via cash management companies and physically transporting cash, and using personal bank accounts. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "18% of the banking authorities considered that higher-risk customer base is a cause of decline"</sub>

## 4. Drivers of the withdrawal from correspondent banking

- **descriptive / method**: The FSB-CBCG examined which jurisdictions were most affected by the decline using criteria (GDP, AML/CFT framework) and which causes drive CBR terminations, using the same questions as the World Bank survey plus a request for banks to quantify the number of exits attributable to each cause. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "with in addition a request for banks to quantify the number of exits that could be attributed to the different causes"</sub>

## 4.1 Analysis of economic and risk factors

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Per Table 9, small economies are on average the most affected by withdrawal of correspondent banks from whole jurisdictions in the survey: the 15 largest economies see only a 3% reduction in foreign banks offering correspondent services against a 29% decline for the 55 economies with GDP below 10 billion, which were on average served by only 4 banks in the survey sample. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The 15 largest economies only see a minor reduction of 3% in the number of foreign banks"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: There are no significant differences when measuring the evolution of CBRs on SWIFT data, probably because SWIFT counts active relationships by the number of corridors in which banks are active (traffic sent or received directly to or from a country); by terminating services to some countries, correspondents of large economies also reduce the corridors in which large countries are active. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There are no significant differences when measuring the evolution of CBRs based on SWIFT data."</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Table 9 (average evolution of correspondents and remaining correspondents by jurisdiction GDP, current 2015, billion USD): columns are average evolution of active CBRs by SWIFT traffic 2012-2016, average evolution in number of correspondents serving the jurisdiction 2011-2016 (survey), average number of correspondents serving it in June 2016 (survey) and number of jurisdictions. Row GDP>1000: -7.7%, -3.1%, 53.9, 15 jurisdictions. A further row with a garbled label ending 'available GDP data' shows -7.4%, -15.4%, 14.3, 185 jurisdictions; the other GDP-band rows are missing from the excerpt. Sources: SWIFT Watch, FSB-CBCG Survey, UN and World Bank GDP data. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Table 9 – Average evolution of the number of correspondents (SWIFT and FSB-CBCG survey)"</sub>

## 4. Drivers of the withdrawal from correspondent banking

- **descriptive / finding or result**: In the World Bank's 2015 survey, authorities and local banks mainly named risk appetite and profitability as the key drivers of the decline in CBRs. Almost all large global banks surveyed instead named concerns about ML/TF risks in the jurisdictions of the foreign correspondent banks (95% of large banks) and the imposition of international sanctions on those jurisdictions (90%) as the most important drivers. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "(95% of large banks surveyed) and “imposition of international sanctions on the jurisdiction(s) of the foreign correspondent bank(s)” (90%)"</sub>

## 4.1 Analysis of economic and risk factors

- **descriptive / finding or result**: FATF compliance status, or absence of information on it, appears to affect the evolution of correspondents serving a jurisdiction between 2011 and June 2016 (the 'exit rate'): Afghanistan and Cambodia, publicly identified by the FATF as of February 2014 as having made insufficient progress on AML/CFT deficiencies, had an exit rate of 40%, similar to the 43% average for the 20 jurisdictions never assessed as of February 2017; the two jurisdictions facing a call for action lost 79% of their correspondent banks. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The two jurisdictions facing a call for action lost 79% of their correspondent banks."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The 20 jurisdictions simply under monitoring in 2011 were less affected than the world average (-12% against -18%) and those under monitoring in 2014 have the same exit rate as the world average, which may be a sign that when deficiencies are addressed in a timely manner they have no lasting impact on a jurisdiction's access to the international financial system through correspondent banking. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "those under monitoring in 2014 have the same exit rate as the world’s average"</sub>

## 4.2 Drivers of termination reported by banks in the FSB-CBCG survey

- **descriptive / method**: The survey asked respondent and correspondent banks alike to report potential drivers for terminating CBRs. Reasons closely follow the World Bank 2015 survey drivers for comparability, with added categories, especially 'dormant relationships', which proved relevant (17% of terminations per correspondent banks); and where the World Bank asked banks to list main drivers for all terminations, the FSB-CBCG asked for the number of terminations in which each driver played a significant role. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "especially “dormant relationships”, which proved to be a relevant driver, involved in 17% of terminations"</sub>

## 4.2 Drivers of termination reported by banks in the FSB-CBCG survey

- **descriptive / data or feature**: Table 11 reports reasons from 43 correspondent banks that counted terminations by specific reason, total terminations and total relationships; the 43 banks had some 17687 CBRs in June 2015 with 1220 net terminations between June 2015 and June 2016 (net termination rate 6.9%), and include major correspondents in Canada, France, Germany, the United Kingdom and other countries; US and Swiss banks are not covered. Footnote 40: drivers marked with an asterisk were added by participants under 'other drivers'. Footnote 42: net terminations are terminated minus opened relationships. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These 43 banks together had some 17687 CBRs41 in June 2015 with 1220 net terminations42"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Calculation of the correspondents' percentage: each driver's terminations for a bank were divided by that bank's total terminations, then weighted by the bank's CBRs in June 2015 relative to the 43-bank total; a driver's score is the sum of these weighted scores. Footnote 41: the count is of customer banks, not accounts, approximated by Vostro accounts (in the bank's currency, or USD, EUR or GBP if higher) when not provided; weighting is by relationships not terminations because some banks counted the SWIFT RMA (a communication capability, not necessarily an account relationship) as terminations. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The score for each driver is the sum for the 43 banks of the weighted scores of that driver."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Classifying drivers is difficult as there may be several reasons behind a termination; banks could give several reasons for the same termination, so the total exceeds 100%. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Banks could give several reasons for the same termination, hence the total exceeds 100%."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Business strategy reasons (excluding profitability, AML/CFT-related reasons or costs) are the most frequently cited driver, involved in over 40% of reported terminations, and include changes to business model, termination of dormant relationships and industry consolidation. Two banks explained a large number of terminations as 'simplification exits' without further detail. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Business strategy reasons (excluding profitability, AML/CFT-related reasons or costs) are the most frequently cited driver"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Reasons overlap and are interrelated: for example a change in business model may stem from a change in risk appetite, and ML/TF concerns could lead to costly preventive processes and controls that reduce profitability. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "concerns about money laundering/terrorist financing (ML/TF) risks could potentially lead to costly preventive processes and controls"</sub>

## Information from other sources

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The 2014 KPMG global AML survey notes that 78% of respondents reported higher total AML investment over 2011 to 2014, exceeding 50% for 22% of respondents, mainly in transaction monitoring systems, Know Your Customer updates and maintenance, and recruitment; but it is not specifically about correspondent banking and the FSB-CBCG did not assess its methodology or findings. Because fees tend to be volume-based, some relationships may bring insufficient volume to meet fixed maintenance costs combined with other costs (cost of capital, liquidity, etc.). (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "78% of survey respondents reported increases in their total investment in AML activity during the three year period from 2011 to 2014"</sub>

## 4.2 Drivers of termination reported by banks in the FSB-CBCG survey

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Three further types of driver are each cited in about a fifth of terminations: lack of profitability (which may itself be seen as business-related), overall risk appetite, and assorted drivers tied to AML/CFT or sanctions regimes. Within the last group, the most commonly cited reasons are the cost of due diligence and the respondent's lack of compliance with AML/CFT or sanctions rules (or concerns, or insufficient information, about such compliance). (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The three other types are each mentioned in a fifth of terminations:"</sub>

## Information from other sources

- **descriptive / data or feature**: A February 2016 study for the UK Financial Conduct Authority (footnote 43: John Howell and Co. Ltd.) illustrated correspondent due diligence costs: on-boarding a respondent through dedicated teams costs roughly GBP 2,300 for lower risk up to 3,300 for higher risk, and annual upkeep (monitoring and non-monitoring) costs GBP 1,400 to 2,000 depending on risk level. The FSB-CBCG did not survey this aspect, nor assess claims from other sources that these costs may have grown in recent years, perhaps partly to remedy insufficient investment. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "total on-boarding costs of a respondent by dedicated teams sums to around GBP 2,300 for lower risk"</sub>

## 4.2 Drivers of termination reported by banks in the FSB-CBCG survey

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Table 11 also shows respondent banks' answers (not weighted, since terminations vary less between respondents), counted as the number of respondent banks that experienced a termination for a listed reason, i.e. frequency of banks rather than of terminations, so not directly comparable with correspondents. Business strategy reasons are again most frequent (36.4% for changes to business model, industry consolidation and dormant relationships combined); lack of profitability of certain services or products was cited by 17.6% of respondents and overall risk appetite by 12.6%; AML/CFT-related reasons are less frequently mentioned by respondents surveyed, who are not necessarily those with whom surveyed correspondents terminated. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "business strategy reasons are also the most frequently mentioned (36.4% for the total of changes to business model"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The gap in AML/CFT reasons may partly reflect information asymmetry: correspondents know their reasons whereas respondents only report the driver given to them; correspondents may also be prevented by law or regulation from divulging suspicions reported to authorities (prohibition of 'tipping off'). (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "correspondent banks may be prevented by law or regulation to divulge suspicions that were reported to authorities"</sub>

## Information from other sources

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report observes that some stated reasons, such as "concerns about money laundering and terrorism", are about fear of criminals misusing financial institutions' systems rather than the actual cost of complying with AML/CFT policies. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "focus specifically on the fear of criminals misusing financial institutions’ systems"</sub>

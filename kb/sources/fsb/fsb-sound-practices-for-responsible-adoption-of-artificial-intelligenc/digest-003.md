## 2. Organisation-wide AI governance

- **descriptive / context**: AI governance concerns how institutions choose, monitor and periodically review their approach to AI adoption and organisational governance. The board and senior management set clear direction and senior-level oversight so AI adoption aligns with business model, risk appetite and strategy (Sound practice 1), establish clear roles and responsibilities for accountability and oversight (Sound practices 2 and 3), and institutions adapt governance and operating models and resource the organisation as AI usage evolves (Sound practice 4). (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "setting a clear direction for AI adoption and in providing senior-level oversight"</sub>

## 2.1. Strategic direction and oversight

- **expected / expectation**: Under Sound Practice 1, the board and senior management consider AI adoption as part of business strategy, weighing positive impacts on the business model, customers, efficiency, growth and legal and regulatory compliance against AI-related risks; the board promotes clear accountability for senior management to implement the institution's approach to AI adoption. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "consider AI adoption as part of the financial institution’s business strategy"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The board and senior management define and communicate across the organisation the outcomes expected from AI adoption, including desired outcomes from appropriate risk management, and are engaged end-to-end from strategy through implementation so that resources (for example technology investment, strategic partnerships) align with the intended outcome. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "define and communicate across the organisation the outcomes expected from AI adoption"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Sound Practice 1 (Strategic direction and oversight) describes the board and senior management as aligning how the institution adopts and governs AI with its business model, risk appetite and strategy. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The board and senior management align AI adoption and governance with the financial institution’s business model risk appetite and strategy"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A footnote explains that the report assumes a management structure of a board of directors and senior management, that legislation and regulation on their functions differ across jurisdictions (in some the board mainly or solely supervises the executive body and is called a supervisory board with no executive functions, in others it has broader competence and sets the general management framework), and that the two terms are used to label distinct decision-making functions within a financial institution. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The terms “board of directors” and “senior management” are used in this report to label distinct decision-making functions"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The board and senior management regularly re-assess whether the institution's AI approach remains fit for purpose. The board curates and receives management information (MI) allowing it to assess whether the approach (including prior decisions not to adopt AI for certain use cases) remains aligned with strategy and whether senior management's implementation remains effective. Footnote 34 says MI include business plans, risk reports (including high risk uses), performance reports, failures and mitigation actions and audit reports, and may include AI adoption metrics such as time savings, staff utilisation, customer satisfaction and acquisition measures. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "regularly re-assess whether the financial institution’s approach to AI adoption remains fit for purpose"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The board and senior management establish clear boundaries for AI adoption by considering AI risks when setting and reviewing risk appetite and tolerance. This could include articulating prohibited AI use cases, for example fully automated decision-making in critical business areas. In setting boundaries they consider the type of AI and potential use cases: level of autonomy, materiality and risk, potential customer impact, legal and regulatory requirements, and dependencies on data, technology and third parties. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "articulating prohibited AI use cases, for example, fully automated decision-making in critical business areas"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 35 defines risk appetite as the aggregate level and types of risk an institution is willing to assume within its risk capacity to achieve strategic objectives and its business plan (citing FSB 2013), and risk tolerance as the boundaries of specific risks the institution can take or withstand. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "aggregate level and types of risk a financial institution is willing to assume within its risk capacity"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The board and senior management also align AI adoption with the institution's conduct standards, considering impact on customers and markets, which informs decisions on explainability, human oversight and redress for specific use cases. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "align AI adoption with the financial institution’s standards for conduct"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Risk appetite and tolerance guide decisions on AI use selection and prioritisation, risk monitoring, management and mitigation (including human oversight and escalation protocols), which matters more as AI expands to critical operations and core business lines; addressing AI explicitly in risk appetite bridges board-level oversight and management action. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "guide decisions on AI use selection and prioritisation, risk monitoring, management, and mitigation"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The board and senior management appropriately resource the institution to support AI adoption now and on an ongoing basis: appropriate competencies and skills at board, senior management and staff levels (including control functions) for their roles, and sufficiently mature data and technology capabilities. They reassess resources, skills and capabilities periodically as AI becomes more embedded in critical operations and core business lines and as technology, legal or regulatory requirements evolve. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "appropriately resource the financial institution to support AI adoption currently and on an ongoing basis"</sub>

## Case study: Aligning skills and capabilities to AI strategy

- **descriptive / case example**: Some large internationally active groups, under board steer, treat AI as a strategic pillar and redesign processes end to end; boards and senior managers took master classes developed with academic institutions and consultants, and staff attended AI bootcamps to become 'hybrid' professionals alongside organisation-wide AI literacy programmes. They set concrete targets for adoption covering hard benefits (productivity, cost) and softer ones (staff capacity), but acknowledge that finding suitable metrics, particularly for value realisation, remains challenging. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "finding suitable metrics remains challenging, particularly around value realisation"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Other institutions layer AI onto existing processes without broader transformation; their boards and senior management focus on organisation-wide AI literacy and clear boundaries on AI use, such as restricting staff to specified tools accessed only through prescribed platforms. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "restricting staff to specified tools accessed only through prescribed platforms"</sub>

## 2.2. Governance and accountability

- **expected / expectation**: Sound Practice 2 (Governance and accountability): financial institutions define clear roles and responsibilities and maintain an appropriate governance framework to enable responsible AI adoption. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial institutions define clear roles and responsibilities and maintain an appropriate governance framework"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Institutions define clear roles and responsibilities for AI adoption, building on existing structures such as the three lines of defence or creating new structures if appropriate. Under that model, first-line functions may assess business outcomes, controls, materiality and risks of use cases; second-line functions may keep an organisation-wide view of AI risks and may provide independent assessment across compliance, data quality, explainability, conduct risk and model performance; third-line functions may deliver independent assurance proportionate to materiality and risk. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "building on existing structures such as the three lines of defence36 or creating new structures if appropriate"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The report states it is essential that audit, evaluation, risk management and testing stay independent of, and functionally separate from, development teams so as to preserve objectivity, and that the people carrying out these independent functions are empowered and sufficiently skilled to give effective challenge. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "independent and functionally separate from development teams to maintain objectivity"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 36 states that the three lines of defence commonly consist of business unit management, an independent corporate operational risk management function, and independent assurance, and that how formally they are implemented varies with the organisation's nature, size, complexity and risk profile. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The three lines of defence commonly consists of business unit management, an independent corporate operational risk management function"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Operating models consider the nature and scale of AI adoption and may be centralised (consistent oversight and standards across use cases), decentralised (business unit flexibility and domain expertise) or hybrid; institutions may adapt existing operating models. The operating model is reviewed periodically for effectiveness in supporting strategy, organisation-wide risk assessment and management, and alignment with risk appetite and tolerance as AI adoption evolves. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The operating model may be centralised, decentralised, or a hybrid of both"</sub>

## Establish cross-functional coordination mechanisms

- **expected / expectation**: Institutions implement cross-functional coordination mechanisms drawing on business owners, risk managers, technology experts, compliance officers, legal specialists, developers and other stakeholders, to share knowledge, understand the full range of AI benefits and risks, optimise implementation plans and delineate responsibilities. Mechanisms may include regular fora, standardised communication channels, consistent methodologies for assessing, monitoring and reporting AI risks, and feedback loops adapting governance and risk frameworks to evolving risks. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "implement cross-functional coordination mechanisms to leverage on the diverse perspective from business owners"</sub>

## 2.3. AI risk management framework and effective documentation

- **expected / expectation**: Institutions incorporate AI risk considerations into existing risk management frameworks, policies and procedures as appropriate, so AI risks can be identified, assessed, accepted (or approved), documented, monitored and managed. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identified, assessed, accepted (or approved), documented, monitored, and managed"</sub>

## Establish cross-functional coordination mechanisms

- **expected / expectation**: Sound Practice 3 (Incorporation of AI risks into risk management framework) describes financial institutions as having risk management frameworks that effectively cover AI risks, including processes for identifying and documenting AI and for assessing materiality and risk. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "adopt risk management frameworks that effectively address AI risks"</sub>

## 2.3. AI risk management framework and effective documentation

- **permissive / expectation**: Where existing policies, procedures and frameworks do not sufficiently address AI risks, such as lack of explainability, autonomy, or inadequate or inappropriate human oversight, institutions may enhance them or establish new ones as appropriate. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "financial institutions may enhance these policies, procedures, or frameworks, or establish new ones as appropriate"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Institutions implement guardrails and controls proportionate to materiality and risk (for example streamlined processes for low materiality, low risk use cases and rigorous processes for high ones). Guardrails can vary by lifecycle stage and use case and may include staged approvals, comprising supervised experimentation in controlled environments and pilot phases before deployment. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "streamlined processes for low materiality and low risk use cases, and rigorous processes for high materiality"</sub>

## Maintain accurate documentation of AI use cases

- **expected / expectation**: To keep visibility over AI risks, institutions track and document AI use throughout the lifecycle, which may leverage existing processes such as use case and model inventory or third-party or systems registry, and may hold more detailed information for higher materiality and higher risk use cases. Footnote 37 says proactive record-keeping from inception promotes effective risk management. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "track and document their AI use throughout the lifecycle"</sub>
- **permissive / data or feature**: Lifecycle documentation can include: a use case description covering (i) underlying AI model(s) or system(s) and rationale for selection, (ii) approved, intended or prohibited applications, and (iii) whether developed in-house, using open-source tools or acquired from a third-party AI provider; materiality and risk of the use case and mitigants; business lines, regions, products, customers and market participants impacted; key assumptions and limitations; data sources used during inference and, if developed in-house, training data. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "its underlying AI model(s) or system(s), and the rationale for its selection"</sub>
- **permissive / data or feature**: Documentation can also include: allocation of accountability (key roles and responsibilities); dependencies on other AI use cases, models, systems or third-party providers; lifecycle stages and status; findings from validation, testing, ongoing monitoring and performance assessment (footnote 38 examples: indicators of proper functioning, dates last updated, outstanding remediation actions); and relevant information from third-party AI providers (footnote 39 examples: acceptable use policy, model or system cards, certifications, standards and other assurance, or customisation or fine-tuning by the institution). (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "findings from validation, testing, ongoing monitoring, and performance assessment"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Where appropriate, institutions deploy enhanced documentation and logging for GenAI and agentic AI use cases. For GenAI this may include prompt versioning (systematic tracking, management and auditing of prompt iterations) and version control enabling rollback to previous configurations, model parameters to monitor performance changes, and chain-of-thought logging to help explain outputs. Where such controls are managed by third-party vendors and rollback or configuration access is limited, institutions establish contractual arrangements for vendors to provide timely information and compensating controls. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "deploy enhanced documentation and logging for GenAI and agentic AI use cases"</sub>

## Case study: Documentation of AI use cases

- **descriptive / case example**: Some large internationally active banks keep a centralised organisation-wide AI inventory on dedicated software, with records across the lifecycle; attributes may include purpose, scope of use, risk materiality rating, dependencies, responsible personnel and approvals, and systems may support automated tracking of approvals and issues, identification of model interdependencies and lifecycle checkpoints. Some banks with more limited AI adoption were observed to rely on spreadsheets. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "centralised, organisation-wide AI inventory supported by dedicated software systems"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: For agentic AI, banks added inventory attributes such as agentic-specific identifiers, tools the agent can access, its components and risk guardrails. Where banks use reusable agents across use cases, some track risk information at agent level (permitted uses and known risks); in more mature organisations this takes the form of agent 'certification' with certified status and permitted scope recorded. Attributes are expected to develop as governance for agentic AI and industry norms and regulatory expectations mature. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "agent ‘certification’, where an agent is reviewed and approved for use within defined boundaries"</sub>

## 2.4. Organisational adaptability

- **expected / expectation**: Sound Practice 4 (Organisational adaptability): financial institutions learn, adapt and adjust their oversight, governance, risk management practices and capabilities as AI evolves. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "learn, adapt and adjust their oversight, governance, risk management practices, and capabilities as AI evolves"</sub>

## Sustain AI expertise and risk-aware culture

- **expected / expectation**: Institutions develop and maintain AI expertise and embed a risk-aware culture encouraging challenge, escalation and continuous learning; adaptability requires adequate capability across the institution, from foundational literacy for all staff to specialised expertise for implementers and risk managers and informed board and senior management oversight. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "embed a risk - aware culture that encourages challenge, escalation, and continuous learning"</sub>

## 2.4. Organisational adaptability

- **permissive / recommendation**: Among AI-specific capability-building efforts that may be used, the report lists embedding a strong risk-aware culture that encourages spotting emerging risks, early escalation, questioning of model outputs, and consistent application of governance across business lines. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "embedding a strong risk - aware culture that encourages detection of emerging risks, early escalation"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Capability-building may also include feeding insights from incidents, data/model drift events, near-misses, testing outcomes and validation findings, plus relevant information from third-party AI providers, into staff training and behavioural expectations so that capabilities match operational experience. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "integrating insights from incidents, data/model drift events, near - misses, testing outcomes, and validation findings"</sub>

## Sustain AI expertise and risk-aware culture

- **permissive / recommendation**: AI-specific capability-building may include: foundational AI training for all staff to recognise and respond to inappropriate use, automation errors, automation bias or over-reliance and unusual model behaviour; role-specific expertise for developers, validators, risk managers, auditors and compliance teams on AI-specific risks (for example data or model drift, instability, unusual behaviour, explainability gaps, data vulnerabilities); and targeted oversight training for the board and senior management. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "providing foundational AI training for all staff so that they can recognise and respond to inappropriate use"</sub>

## Maintain external awareness

- **expected / expectation**: Institutions proactively monitor developments outside the organisation for timely awareness of new technology, risks and supervisory expectations, since external developments can surface early risk signals internal processes may not detect. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "proactively monitor developments outside the organisation for timely awareness of new technology, risks"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: External awareness efforts can include monitoring emerging AI capabilities, model architectures and risk typologies; regular engagement with peers, industry groups, academics, technology providers and authorities; participating in shared-learning fora, collaborative testing and public-private consortia; and establishing routines to synthesise external intelligence and flag areas needing governance review. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "establishing routines to synthesise external intelligence and flag potential areas requiring governance review"</sub>

## Case study: Learning from the AI ecosystem through public-private sector collaboration

- **descriptive / case example**: Several authorities set up public-private collaboration platforms, including industry consortia and regulatory sandboxes, bringing together authorities, institutions and technology partners. The Monetary Authority of Singapore convened Project MindForge in 2023, which produced a whitepaper on GenAI risks and opportunities for banks in November 2023 and an AI Risk Management Toolkit in March 2026. The Bank of England and FCA established the AI Consortium in May 2025 to gather stakeholder input on AI capabilities, development, deployment and use in UK financial services. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) convened Project MindForge in 2023, bringing together financial institutions"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The Bank of England and the Financial Conduct Authority set up the AI Consortium in May 2025. Its stated aims are to identify how AI is used in financial services, discuss emerging benefits and risks, and inform their approach to safe AI adoption. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The AI Consortium aims to identify how AI is being used in financial services"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The Hong Kong Monetary Authority's GenAI Sandbox, launched in 2024, gave participating banks supervisory guidance, technical support and GPU computing access, with symposia and roundtables for knowledge sharing; it has since expanded to banking, securities and capital markets, asset and wealth management, insurance, mandatory provident fund and stored valued facilities. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "provided participating banks with supervisory guidance, technical support, and access to GPU computing resources"</sub>

## Evolve with insight

- **expected / expectation**: Institutions update governance, controls and oversight mechanisms as needed based on lessons from internal and external insights such as AI incidents, testing outcomes and emerging risks, including periodically reviewing governance, lifecycle controls and oversight roles, and regularly reassessing internal assumptions to avoid outdated mental models or blind spots. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "update governance, controls, and oversight mechanisms as needed, based on lessons learned"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Updates to governance, controls and oversight can include performing root-cause analysis and post-incident reviews of AI-related incidents, recognising that such incidents may reflect underlying issues (for example societal biases in training data) that traditional analysis and reviews may not always be designed to capture. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "performing root - cause analysis and post - incident reviews for AI - related incidents"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Updates can include treating relevant anomalies, drift events and near-misses as triggers to assess and, where needed, refine controls, given the unique non-linear degradation patterns of AI models. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "treating relevant anomalies, drift events, and near - misses as triggers to assess"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Updates can also include refreshing policies, standards and procedures based on learning; making governance mechanisms adapt to new forms of AI that bring new risks (for example GenAI, foundation and frontier models, agentic systems); and incorporating lessons learned into capability development and tooling enhancements. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "implementing governance mechanisms so that they adapt to new forms of AI"</sub>

## Case study: How an adaptive AI strategy delivered industry leadership

- **descriptive / case example**: A G-SIB evolved its AI approach in phases: first investing in internal research, specialised talent and proprietary data infrastructure; after about a year shifting to implementation where AI seemed most fruitful and governable; investing in enterprise-grade data and computing infrastructure; and embedding AI in internal tools used by tens of thousands of employees. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "After about a year, the bank shifted from research to implementation in areas where AI seemed"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: After identifying limits of decentralised development, the G-SIB created a centralised AI group reporting directly to the Chief Executive Officer, acting as an enterprise accelerator that prioritises use cases, coordinates deployment across business lines and ensures consistent risk, security and compliance standards. The approach embeds privacy, security, fairness, accountability and transparency requirements from design and training through monitoring and audit, marking a move from project-based experimentation to industrial scale delivery. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "creating a centralised AI group reporting directly to the Chief Executive Officer"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In a G-SIB case, after about a year of research-first investment the bank moved into implementation in areas where AI seemed most fruitful and governable. These early deployments showed that advanced AI systems could operate reliably and deliver measurable outcomes without undermining risk controls. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These early deployments demonstrated that advanced AI systems could operate reliably and deliver measurable outcomes"</sub>

## Consider compensating controls

- **expected / expectation**: If an institution cannot obtain sufficient assurance and information from third-party AI providers, it implements compensating controls, which are described as sub-optimal but mitigating and may include additional pre-deployment testing, limiting initial use of the third-party AI to low materiality and risk use cases, and more intense human oversight (at least temporarily) such as enhanced monitoring, benchmarking or stricter approval of certain actions. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "If a financial institution cannot obtain sufficient assurance and information from third-party AI providers"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Contractual tools such as warranties and indemnities from third-party AI providers can also help compensate for limited transparency. Where no satisfactory compensating controls can bring relevant risks within agreed appetite, the institution may decide not to use the provider's model or system. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "warranties and indemnities from third-party AI providers can also help compensate for limited transparency"</sub>

## Monitor and manage concentration risks

- **expected / expectation**: Institutions monitor and manage concentration risks at the individual institution (including group level), in particular by identifying and documenting exposures to third-party AI providers and the AI supply chain using AI inventories, third-party registers and similar tools, and seeking assurances on how providers manage supply chain risks (footnote 102 example: asking how a provider tested its ability to handle severe shortages of essential hardware or energy). (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "monitor and manage concentration risks at the individual financial institution (including group level)"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Institutions using third-party AI for high materiality and high risk use cases incorporate concentration risk into business continuity planning and exit strategies, developing robust exit strategies that may include alternative third-party AI providers or the ability to continue the AI-enabled service manually after disruption or termination. Ensuring data access and portability can enable substitutability while minimising disruption risk. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "develop robust exit strategies which may include alternative thirdparty AI providers"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Institutions also explore joint scenario tests and sector exercises with third-party AI providers to mitigate potential sector-wide risks. Footnote 103 notes that in some jurisdictions third-party providers designated as critical by authorities are subject to direct oversight, which can include joint incident management exercises with institutions. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "explore the use of joint scenario tests and sector exercises with thirdparty AI providers"</sub>

## Case study: Financial institutions’ management of third-party AI risks

- **descriptive / case example**: Some internationally active banks uplifted procurement and third-party risk frameworks for AI-specific risks, addressing information gaps (limited visibility into third-party AI such as training data, model details or vendor use of the bank's data) and accountability gaps (institutions remain accountable for AI outcomes even when the AI was not built in-house). (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "information gaps (financial institutions often have limited visibility into third-party AI systems"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: These banks built internal frameworks categorising third-party AI deployment patterns (onboarding with customisation such as fine-tuning; onboarding without customisation including SaaS; and AI embedded within a wider product) and recognised that 'open source' AI spans a spectrum from fully open-source to open-weights models and restricted-use licences, with differing risk and due diligence implications. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "onboarding with customisation (e.g. fine-tuning); onboarding without customisation (including SaaS)"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In the third-party AI risk case study, the practices listed include scheduling periodic reassessments and including contract notification clauses, and ensuring appropriate legal, technical and risk expertise is involved throughout. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "scheduling periodic reassessments and including contract notification clauses;"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The case study practices also include revising contracts to deal with AI-specific matters such as intellectual property, data retention, cybersecurity and notification of changes. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "updating contracts to cover AI-specific issues such as intellectual property, data retention, cybersecurity, and change notification"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: A key principle adopted is to subject third-party AI to the same lifecycle governance as in-house AI, with extra steps where vendor visibility is limited. Practices include requesting standardised vendor disclosures (often 'AI Cards') proportionate to risk; deciding how to respond to incomplete disclosures using mitigants such as indemnities, external attestations or compensatory testing; AI-specific due diligence at procurement and on an ongoing basis; and monitoring for unapproved new AI features via vendor release notes. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "requesting standardised vendor disclosures (often via ‘AI Cards") proportionate to risk"</sub>

## Annex 1: List of Sound Practices for financial institutions’ responsible AI adoption

- **expected / expectation**: Annex 1, Sound Practice 1 (Strategic direction and oversight): the board and senior management align AI adoption and governance with the institution's business model, risk appetite and strategy. Sound Practice 2 (Governance and accountability): institutions define clear roles and responsibilities and maintain an appropriate governance framework to enable responsible AI adoption. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial institutions define clear roles and responsibilities, and maintain an appropriate governance framework"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Annex 1, Sound Practice 3 (Incorporation of AI risks into risk management framework): institutions adopt risk management frameworks that effectively address AI risks and include processes for AI identification, documentation, and materiality and risk assessment. Sound Practice 4 (Organisational adaptability): institutions learn, adapt and adjust oversight, governance, risk management practices and capabilities as AI evolves. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "include processes for AI identification, documentation, as well as materiality and risk assessment"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Annex 1, Sound Practice 5 (Materiality and risk assessment): institutions implement an effective and systematic approach to assess materiality and risk of AI use cases at inception and thereafter. Sound Practice 6 (Selection): when selecting models or systems, institutions consider business objectives, operational and technical needs, and the materiality and risks of use cases. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial institutions implement an effective and systematic approach to assess the materiality and risk of AI use cases"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Annex 1, Sound Practice 7 (Data governance): institutions establish appropriate data governance to maintain data fit-for-purpose for training, testing and using AI (accurate, complete, consistent, reliable, secure). Sound Practice 8 (Explainability and transparency): institutions understand differences in explainability of types of AI, if appropriate and feasible adopt more explainable AI or consider compensating controls, and provide appropriate transparency tailored to different stakeholders. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "establish appropriate data governance to maintain data that is fit-for-purpose for training, testing, and using AI"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Annex 1, Sound Practice 9 (Performance management): institutions evaluate performance of AI use cases proportionately to materiality and risk, including through performance assessments, testing and ongoing monitoring. Sound Practice 10 (Human oversight): institutions implement appropriate and effective human oversight relevant to materiality, risk, autonomy, complexity and explainability of use cases. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial institutions evaluate the performance of AI use cases proportionately to their materiality and risk"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: In the Annex 1 list, Sound Practice 11 (Cyber and ICT risk management) has financial institutions managing AI-related cyber and ICT risks, including, where appropriate, by building AI cyber and ICT risk scenarios into tests and exercises, sharing relevant information, and using AI tools in cyber and ICT risk management. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial institutions manage AI-related cyber and ICT risks, including, where appropriate"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: In the Annex 1 list, Sound Practice 12 (Third-party AI risk management) has financial institutions appropriately managing the risks of using third-party AI, focusing on performance, transparency, data quality, supply chain and concentration risks, and business continuity. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial institutions appropriately manage risks from AI third-party use with a focus on performance"</sub>

## Traditional AI (machine-learning (ML))

- **descriptive / case example**: Annex 2 example, traditional ML: a retail lender's ML approach (hundreds of features, complex interaction terms) outperformed its non-AI model somewhat in initial testing but raised explainability challenges (unclear input-output translation, features included and weights), questions about conceptual soundness and concerns about consumer law violations. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "uses hundreds of features and involves complex interaction terms among them"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In response the lender involved consumer compliance experts before deployment to review input data and output for discrimination or bias; they found features correlated with protected features and these were dropped. Given limited ability to assess conceptual soundness it set higher performance standards and planned close, frequent monitoring, deployed post hoc explainers (recognising limits, for example with correlated features), and planned to run the ML alongside the previous non-AI model to benchmark and 'triangulate' the most accurate outcomes. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "have shown to be correlated with protected features"</sub>

## GenAI

- **descriptive / case example**: Annex 2 example, GenAI: a large insurer deploying a vendor-developed LLM foundation model in claims management (the vendor itself admitted not fully understanding how it reaches outputs) was concerned about hallucinations. It used retrieval augmented generation on its insurance corpus to ground outputs, and a reasoning feature prompting the LLM to show its steps, while noting research that LLM reasoning may not be accurate, with the model possibly describing different steps from those it actually used. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "employ retrieval augmented generation (RAG) based on its existing corpus of insurance information"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Given this uncertainty the insurer deploys in a controlled manner: partial deployment among some claims adjustors with qualified human oversight of output before use; no LLM output shared directly with customers; further rollout only after more testing including stability tests that the LLM operates within a certain range; specific prompt instructions to narrow outputs and minimise hallucinations; and staff training including the LLM's limitations and parameters. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "does not allow any LLM output to be shared directly with customers"</sub>

## Agentic AI

- **descriptive / case example**: Annex 2 example, agentic AI: an institution pairs an AI agent with an LLM for operational tasks; the LLM's probabilistic nature adds a stochastic element, so hallucination or erroneous output could cause the agent to duplicate tasks, perform them in the wrong order or operate outside its designated range. Controls include prohibiting use in any financial transactions until further testing, intentional sensitivity tests by validators or reviewers trying to convince the agent to act beyond its parameters, and sampled monitoring of selected activities and tasks on a regular basis. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "prohibiting its use in any financial transactions until further testing is conducted"</sub>

## Example A

- **descriptive / case example**: Annex 3 Example A: an institution's new ML fraud detection and prevention model for credit cards outperformed the previous non-AI model in initial tests, including reducing both false positives and false negatives. It continues sensitivity tests to find the model's limits, collects realised outcomes to test out of sample especially for overfitting, conducts ongoing bias testing including potential discrimination (given complexity and explainability challenges), and monitors for changes in fraud behaviour to check the model's relationships still apply. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "outperforms the previous non-AI model used, including to reduce both false positive and false negatives"</sub>

## Example B

- **descriptive / case example**: Annex 3 Example B: an institution deployed an LLM for document retrieval and summarisation (evaluating prospectuses, drafting outlines of public financial statements) with no 'ground truth' to compare against. Employees review output for faithfulness to source documents and hallucinations; subject-matter experts evaluate technical topics; stability is tested by running the same query multiple times hours or days apart. It concluded the LLM could continue to be used but a human must review all output for accuracy before internal distribution, staff submit prompts within set parameters, and further sampled testing by experts continues. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "running the same query multiple times and several hours/days apart"</sub>

## Example C

- **descriptive / case example**: Annex 3 Example C: an institution experimenting with AI agents for simple operational tasks tests the full range of permitted tasks, repeats tests at different times to detect performance deviation, has reviewers try to force prohibited actions (for example accessing financial accounts), and collects performance information including from users. Use is limited to areas not involving financial accounts, legal or regulatory compliance, or customer interactions; the agent may be directed to follow regular checklists with staff investigating if it fails; staff monitor on a sampled basis. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "make attempts to force it to take actions that are not permitted"</sub>

## Glossary

- **descriptive / context**: The glossary is a non-exhaustive list of terms compiled from general understanding in the AI community and drawing on previous FSB AI reports and other lexicons. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "provides a (non-exhaustive) list of terms and definitions used in this report"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Data drift: when data used to train, validate, test and run a model ceases to be appropriate, for example because it no longer applies to current circumstances or the feature relationships established by the methodology no longer hold. Data/model poisoning: an attack where malicious actors manipulate training data or model parameters to introduce errors or biases into outputs, compromising integrity, reliability and security. Deep learning: machine learning using algorithms working in 'layers' inspired by the brain, usable for supervised, unsupervised or reinforcement learning. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "When data used to train, validate, test and run an AI model or system ceases to be appropriate"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Disinformation: deliberate creation and dissemination of false or misleading information to deceive or manipulate public opinion, market behaviour or decision-making. Explainability: the ability of a model or system to provide clear and interpretable outputs or decisions, specifically the extent to which humans can understand how it generates outputs from inputs, including (i) factors, logic or processes contributing to outputs, (ii) relative importance of input variables or features, and (iii) how methodological relationships vary across context or use cases; it stems from model characteristics and is a continuum, not binary. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The deliberate creation and dissemination of false or misleading information"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Foundation models: an umbrella term for diverse models usually trained by applying deep learning to massive quantities of data such as text and images; because the expertise, time and computing power to train them from scratch are typically prohibitive for most non-specialist firms, they are usually pre-trained and shared with end-users for further use and refinement. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An umbrella term referring to a diversity of models that are usually trained by applying deep learning"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The glossary defines agentic AI as systems built to carry out complex, extended tasks autonomously, often making decisions and taking actions with limited human oversight; they are distinct from GenAI but may include generative capabilities to improve content generation and decision making. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Systems designed to autonomously perform complex and extended tasks"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The glossary defines Artificial Intelligence (AI) as a machine-based system that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers from the input it receives how to produce outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a machine-based system that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers, from the input it receives"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The glossary defines criticality as how important a service, system or provider is to a financial institution's operations. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The importance of a service, system, or provider to the operations of a financial institution"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Generative AI (GenAI): AI that generates new content such as text, images and videos, often from user prompts, usually powered by foundation models such as LLMs. Large Language Models (LLMs): a type of foundation model trained for natural language tasks such as text generation, document classification, summarisation, question-and-answer and sentiment analysis. LLM hallucinations: when an LLM generates seemingly confident but inaccurate outputs or fabricates nonsensical outputs in response to user inputs. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "generates seemingly confident but inaccurate outputs, or fabricates nonsensical outputs"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Machine learning: a method of designing a sequence of actions (algorithms) to solve a problem, which optimise automatically through experience with limited or no human intervention. Model drift: when relationships between model inputs and outputs change, for example because an assumed link between an input and output variable no longer holds. Model risk: the potential for adverse consequences from decisions based on incorrect or misused models. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The potential for adverse consequences arising from decisions based on incorrect or misused models"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Open-source models: AI models whose full training code, and in some cases training data or its composition, is made publicly available for use and modification; they offer customisability and reduce vendor lock-in but may add risks such as security vulnerabilities, data privacy or data quality concerns. Operational resilience: the ability of an institution to deliver critical operations despite disruptions, including capacity to prepare for, withstand, respond to, recover from and adapt to adverse events. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "full training code, and in some cases the training data or its composition, is made publicly available"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Reinforcement learning: a subset of machine learning between supervised and unsupervised; the algorithm is fed an unlabelled data set, chooses an action for each data point and receives feedback (perhaps from a human) to learn, used for example in robotics, game theory and self-driving cars. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG): an AI technique improving LLM accuracy by retrieving data from a selected corpus such as internal databases or live data feeds to inform outputs. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An AI technique that improves LLM accuracy by retrieving data from a selected corpus of sources"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Supervised learning: the algorithm is fed training data with labels on all observations (for example transactions labelled fraudulent or not) and learns a general classification rule to predict labels for new observations. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a data set of transactions may contain labels on data points identifying those that are fraudulent"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Supply chain: the network of entities providing infrastructure, goods, services and other inputs directly or indirectly used to deliver a service to an institution; for this report its scope is limited to services under a third-party service relationship. Traditional AI: a suite of computational techniques such as longstanding machine learning methods that pre-date advances such as GenAI. (p. 62)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the scope of supply chain is limited to the services under a thirdparty service relationship"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Unsupervised learning: the algorithm detects patterns by identifying clusters of observations with similar underlying characteristics (for example finding securities similar to an illiquid security to help price it). Vertical integration: consolidation of multiple stages of a supply chain or production process within one organisation; in AI, when one company controls layers such as hardware, cloud infrastructure and AI models, offering end-to-end services. (p. 62)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The algorithm is asked to detect patterns in the data by identifying clusters of observations"</sub>

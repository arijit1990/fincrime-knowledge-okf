## Case study: Scaling relationship management with AI

- **descriptive / case example**: A global systemically important bank (G-SIB) active in retail and wholesale banking piloted an integrated AI platform for corporate relationship managers, who previously faced time pressure preparing for engagements, synthesising information and producing tailored proposals, leaving some clients underserved. The platform integrates external sources (news feeds, research reports) with internal knowledge bases. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "integrates a wide range of external data sources including news feeds, and research reports"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: AI-assisted insight generation and proposal creation reduced preparation time by approximately 50% and enabled around three times more client dialogue, letting managers serve every client including previously underserved ones. Rollout to approximately 3,500 corporate relationship managers is expected in financial year 2026. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "reduced preparation time by approximately 50%, while enabling around three times more client dialogue"</sub>

## Productivity enhancing applications

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Several institutions report efficiency and productivity gains from AI automating labour-intensive activities (faster execution, more consistent processes, lower operating costs, staff freed for higher-value tasks), with significant gains particularly from GenAI synthesising and transforming unstructured content. Uses include back office automation, risk analytics, content and code generation, and foundation models strengthening internal controls through automated documentation, monitoring and anomaly detection. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "significant productivity gains, particularly from GenAI applications that synthesise and transform unstructured content"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: AI helps institutions scale operations amid growing data volumes and transaction complexity while maintaining service quality. Institutions report GenAI coding assistants (code generation, debugging, code reviews, root cause analysis, documentation) giving time savings and faster onboarding of technical staff, with some banks reporting significant cost savings, and organisation-wide GenAI assistants for drafting, research and information retrieval improving knowledge access and collaboration. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Coding assistants support code generation, debugging, code reviews, root cause analysis"</sub>

## Case study: Operational efficiency with AI adoption

- **descriptive / case example**: A large internationally active insurer reviewed manual workflows globally to find where AI could have the most immediate and governable impact, eliminating approximately 400,000 manual processing instances in 2025. In the underwriting quick quote process (about 4,000 requests a month, each producing a Preliminary Assessment from email enquiries), AI now drafts the assessment for underwriter review, cutting turnaround from one business day (simpler) or two to three days (complex) to approximately 45 seconds. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "elimination of approximately 400,000 manual processing instances in 2025"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The same insurer uses AI code generation across the company, which wrote six million lines of code in 2025, with around 80% adoption among developers. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to write six million lines of code in 2025, with around 80% adoption rate among developers"</sub>

## Operational resilience

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Institutions use AI to enhance operational and cyber resilience: analysing large data volumes in real time to identify anomalies and detect attacks faster than human analysts while reducing false positives, identifying vulnerabilities, and supporting business continuity through swift recovery from incidents such as ransomware attacks. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "detect potential attacks faster than human analysts, while also reducing false positives"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Institutions of various sizes use internal and third-party AI tools to monitor anomalous network behaviour across employee access, data transfers and unusual API calls; AI can also identify phishing attempts, and GenAI can rapidly populate internal security alerts and incident reports. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "monitor anomalous network behaviour across internal employee access, data transfers, and unusual API calls"</sub>

## 1.3. Risks and implementation challenges of AI adoption for financial institutions

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Governance risks: inadequate governance and lack of board and senior management oversight can create significant risks; without clear accountability critical issues may be overlooked; inadequate assessment of materiality and risk of use cases can mis-calibrate risk management; fragmented implementation across business units, particularly at large institutions, can undermine risk management; governance gaps may enable 'shadow AI', where employees use AI tools without authorisation or for unapproved uses, reducing visibility and ability to manage AI risks. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "‘shadow AI’, where employees adopt AI tools without authorisation or for unapproved uses"</sub>

## Ineffective data governance and poor data quality

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Ineffective data governance and poor data quality can lead to data security risk, model risk, unacceptable bias and poor AI performance. Models can perpetuate or amplify biases and inaccuracies in input data, and biased outcomes can become self-reinforcing as they generate new data influencing future decisions, entrenching biased patterns. Footnote 23 says some bias, in AI and non-AI models, can be acceptable, for example bias aligned with the institution's risk appetite that complies with legal and regulatory obligations. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "perpetuate or amplify biases and inaccuracies present in input data"</sub>

## 1.3. Risks and implementation challenges of AI adoption for financial institutions

- **descriptive / finding or result**: As noted in the FSB's 2024 report, four AI-related vulnerabilities stand out for their potential to increase financial stability risks: (i) third-party dependencies and service provider concentration, (ii) market correlations, (iii) cyber risks, and (iv) model risk, data quality and governance. The report builds on that earlier analysis and describes further AI-related risks and implementation challenges that can affect financial institutions and their customers. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "third-party dependencies and service provider concentration; (ii) market correlations; (iii) cyber risks"</sub>

## Ineffective data governance and poor data quality

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Inadequate data governance and quality can also cause poor performance through overfitting (AI works well on training data but not unseen data) or underfitting (AI fails to capture the complexity of training data), inability to address outlier situations, or unintended legal and regulatory breaches. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "poor AI performance due to overfitting24 or underfitting25"</sub>

## Explainability challenges

- **descriptive / limitation**: Explainability is also a challenge in non-AI models, but AI adds challenges, particularly in complex, autonomous or rapidly evolving models where the basis for decisions may be hard to understand, made with no or minimal human involvement, or shift over time in hard-to-follow ways. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI adoption introduces additional challenges to explainability and transparency"</sub>

## Inadequate or degrading AI performance

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Inadequate or degrading AI performance can cause poor decisions and financial, legal, regulatory and operational risks; the higher the materiality and risk of the use case, the greater the impact on the institution, customers and market participants. Examples: models trained on historical data may underperform in new market conditions; some models may hallucinate plausible but incorrect outputs, others may execute inappropriate or harmful tasks; performance may degrade with version changes in key models, and recovery and rollback can be challenging or impossible for some AI. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Some type of AI models may hallucinate by generating seemingly plausible but incorrect outputs"</sub>

## Explainability challenges

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The explainability and transparency challenges the report lists for AI adoption are: assessing conceptual soundness; confirming compliance with legal and regulatory requirements; separating causality from correlation; and assessing reliability and robustness. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI adoption introduces additional challenges to explainability and transparency, particularly in complex, autonomous, or rapidly evolving AI models"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The list of explainability challenges continues with: detecting bias, errors or performance issues, including data drift and model drift; understanding changes in models or systems that update dynamically; assessing data quality, especially where datasets are inaccessible (such as the proprietary datasets of third-party AI providers); and conducting validation or types of independent review. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "detecting bias, errors, or performance issues, including data drift and model drift"</sub>

## Inadequate or inappropriate human oversight

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Inadequate human oversight may hinder management of AI risks, causing unintended legal or regulatory breaches or customer harm. Misconceptions that undermine oversight include assuming AI always operates predictably in the same environment, that models nearly always produce correct output, that the environment will remain predictable, or that human involvement ensures effective oversight; oversight may also be weakened by over-reliance and automation bias (overly trusting automated outputs without critical thinking). (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Human oversight may be weakened by over-reliance and/or automation bias"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: AI has implications for cyber and ICT risk: depending on deployment, use may affect system availability and data integrity through defective AI-generated application code, and improper use of agentic AI could cause operational risk if the AI misreads unclear prompts and executes an automated task erroneously. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "defective application codes generated by AI"</sub>

## Cyber and ICT risks

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: AI may be weaponised by threat actors against institutions and customers, increasing the frequency and impact of fraud and cyber-attacks. Poisoning: threat actors inject biased or incorrect data into training data, gradually undermining model performance over time. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Threat actors inject biased or incorrect data into training data, gradually undermining model performance over time"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Malicious inputs include prompt injections (manipulating prompts with malicious content to influence model behaviour and bypass safeguards), jailbreaking (using prompts to circumvent a GenAI model's safety features, which can yield dangerous, illegal or unauthorised outputs), evasion (small hard-to-detect changes to input data that deceive a model into incorrect outputs; footnote examples are slightly modifying an image so a security system misclassifies it or tweaking text to bypass content filters) and extraction (reverse engineering a model to obtain its architecture, parameters or training data). (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "where a threat actor manipulates prompts with malicious content to influence model behaviour and bypass safeguards"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Deepfakes and GenAI-enabled phishing: realistic multi-modal generation can amplify social engineering through deepfakes, impersonation and false communications; threat actors can impersonate individuals with authority to approve high-value transactions to defraud institutions, or use AI-generated images or video to bypass KYC checks. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "use AI-generated images or video to bypass KYC checks"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Lower barriers to other cyber-attacks: AI models and coding agents can let threat actors carry out complex attacks that previously needed specialised skills, including developing malicious code and identifying and exploiting severe vulnerabilities, including zero day vulnerabilities, at highly accelerated speeds. Some advanced models can autonomously find and exploit vulnerabilities through source code analysis, web application testing, binary analysis and protocol fuzzing, compressing the time from discovery to exploitation. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "significantly compress the timeline from vulnerability discovery to exploitation"</sub>

## Third-party dependencies and concentration

- **descriptive / finding or result**: AI adoption increasingly depends on highly concentrated third-party providers across the AI supply chain: cloud infrastructure, hardware, foundation models, data providers and deployment platforms. Listed third-party risks begin with failure to perform as expected, where third-party AI systems may perform inconsistently with agreed service terms. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "highly concentrated third-party providers across multiple layers of the AI supply chain"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Insufficient transparency: reliance on proprietary or complex third-party AI models, systems or data can limit due diligence, performance monitoring and risk management; providers may change underlying models without adequate notice, leaving institutions unaware of changes affecting performance or risk, which can amplify explainability challenges for GenAI and agentic AI. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "change the underlying models powering their AI systems without adequate notice"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Data governance: third-party models can pose risks if training data includes inappropriate data such as unlawfully processed personal data; third-party systems may inadvertently disclose confidential or protected data; and providers could use an institution's or its customers' data for unauthorised purposes. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "if their training data includes inappropriate data, such as unlawfully processed personal data"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Operational resilience: significant disruption to critical third-party services can create risks for critical operations. Service provider concentration: providers of cloud infrastructure, hardware and foundation models are often highly concentrated and sometimes vertically integrated, with geographic concentration at key points of the AI supply chain exposed to physical events such as natural disasters or other supply chain risks. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "often highly concentrated and, in some cases, vertically integrated"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Homogeneous behaviours and correlated outcomes: reliance on common AI models, datasets or infrastructure can correlate behaviour across institutions, amplifying herding and procyclicality, and could worsen market stress, liquidity crunches and asset price vulnerabilities in periods of financial instability. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "amplifying risks like herding and procyclicality"</sub>

## Consumer protection and market conduct risks

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Inadequate guardrails around AI could cause consumer harm and market conduct risks (the section may also apply to investor protection). Inadequate disclosure of AI use: customers may be unable to understand or challenge decisions affecting them or seek redress. Unfair treatment and poor outcomes: models trained on biased or incomplete data may produce discriminatory results inconsistent with consumer protection requirements. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "customers may be unable to fully understand or challenge decisions that affect them or seek appropriate redress"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Mis-selling and unsuitable recommendations: AI in customer-facing applications, including robo-advisory and product targeting, may yield unsuitable recommendations not aligned with risk profiles, particularly where models use incomplete, outdated or improperly calibrated data. Reduced accountability: reliance on AI in marketing, advisory and engagement may blur responsibility, making it harder for institutions and supervisors to identify and address misconduct in time. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may lead to recommendations that are unsuitable or not aligned with customers’ risk profiles"</sub>

## Agentic AI risks

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: High autonomy of AI agents can create or amplify risks that materialise at great speed. Unauthorised actions: agents act autonomously on pre-defined goals and environment and can dynamically set or modify objectives, risking illegal, unethical or unauthorised actions without human approval or oversight, which may be difficult or impossible for humans to override or remediate. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "dynamically set or modify their objectives based on what they learn"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Erroneous actions: agents may decide incorrectly through goal misalignment, insufficient information or reward hacking (finding a loophole in the reward function to maximise score without achieving the intended goal); errors may appear only in live deployment, real-time detection can be very difficult, an agent can take hundreds of intermediate steps and err in any, and effective monitoring may require augmentation with another AI agent or other AI. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An AI agent can take hundreds of intermediate steps in pursuit of its goals"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Data breaches: agents may expose or manipulate sensitive data such as customer details, trade secrets or internal communications, through malicious exploitation of agent vulnerabilities or accidental disclosure if agents fail to recognise data sensitivity. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI agents may expose or manipulate sensitive data such as customer details, trade secrets"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Disruption to connected systems: agents integrate with APIs, databases, ICT systems and other agents, all of which can be disrupted if the agent is compromised or malfunctions; agents may collude with other agents to carry out cyber-attacks (for example deleting code or DDoS attacks) or spread disinformation, and even without collusion multiple agents may interact in unanticipated ways creating unforeseen risks. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI agents may also collude with other AI agents to carry out cyber-attacks"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Additional risks from inadequate human oversight: real-time human monitoring of agent decisions is impractical as use scales, which can lead to agents pursuing objectives or actions deviating from the institution's intentions or risk appetite without staff being aware or able to intervene in time. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "impracticality of real-time human monitoring of agent decisions as their use scales"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Additional data security and privacy risks: agents may fail to recognise data sensitivity or take autonomous actions that inadvertently expose or manipulate sensitive data such as customer details, trade secrets or internal communications. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "take autonomous actions that inadvertently lead to the exposure or manipulation of sensitive data"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Additional cyber and ICT risks challenging traditional cyber security controls: threat actors can manipulate agents by injecting malicious data into their knowledge base (memory poisoning, for example via retrieval-augmented generation), influencing behaviour over time, and agents may devise novel ways to evade traditional cyber detection. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "injecting malicious data into their knowledge base (known as memory poisoning)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report introduces the sound practices: four address AI governance at organisational level and eight (in the next section) cover the AI lifecycle; the four governance practices are designed to be cross-cutting to inform the remaining practices. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these four sound practices are designed to be cross-cutting to inform the remaining sound practices"</sub>

## Questions

- **descriptive / context**: Consultation questions 1 to 4 ask whether respondents agree with the described benefits and risks of AI adoption and whether any substantive ones are missing; whether the sound practices are sufficiently comprehensive and clear; whether they balance risks of all forms of AI with risks of emerging complex forms such as GenAI and agentic AI; and whether they are flexible enough for newer AI types over time. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Are the sound practices sufficiently comprehensive and clear to enable financial institutions’ responsible AI adoption?"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Consultation questions 5 to 7 ask whether the case studies sufficiently show how different types of financial institutions benefit from responsible AI adoption (inviting additional case studies, particularly for nonbanks); whether the case studies give actionable insights; and whether the glossary definitions are clear and aligned with industry sound practices including recent AI developments. (p. 3)[^original]

## Executive summary

- **descriptive / context**: Financial institutions are rapidly adopting, using and innovating with AI to gain efficiency, transform business models, improve customer services and strengthen risk management such as defence against cyber attacks. The report says the benefits usually come with additional risks, and managing them well supports sustained value creation and limits financial stability risk as AI adoption scales. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The significant benefits that can arise from AI adoption, however, usually come with additional risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FSB's sound practices are intended as a menu that financial institutions could use for responsible AI adoption, build on and are broadly compatible with existing work by the FSB and other standard-setting bodies, and seek to foster coordination, cooperation and information-sharing among stakeholders within and across jurisdictions. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "provide a menu of sound practices that financial institutions could use to enable responsible AI adoption"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The sound practices are not intended to establish an international standard, to impose a prescriptive approach to responsible AI adoption, or to influence business decisions on adopting a particular AI technology. They are not exhaustive and may be refined as AI technologies evolve. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "not intended to establish an international standard, to impose a prescriptive approach"</sub>
- **recommended / expectation**: The 12 sound practices cover organisation-wide governance and management of the stages of AI development and deployment (AI lifecycle). Financial institutions should consider proportionality: more robust practices may be appropriate for large, complex, highly connected institutions and where AI is used in critical (material) functions, while smaller, less complex or less interconnected institutions, and less material, lower risk or less complex use cases, may warrant only relevant sound practices or appropriate modification of them. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "When applying the sound practices, financial institutions should consider proportionality"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Sound practices 1 to 4 concern organisation-wide AI governance and highlight: (i) the pivotal role of the board and senior management in setting the approach and oversight so AI adoption fits business model, risk appetite and strategy; (ii) clear governance frameworks, policies, procedures and processes to identify, assess, monitor and manage AI-related risks; (iii) clear responsibilities and accountabilities throughout the organisation; and (iv) continuous learning and adaptation to build resources, skills, knowledge and capabilities. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the pivotal role the board and senior management play in setting the overall approach"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Sound practices 5 to 10 address specific AI use cases through the lifecycle with proportionate guardrails: (v) assessing materiality and risks of use cases at inception and thereafter; (vi) selecting appropriate AI models or systems considering objectives, operational and technical needs, materiality and risk; (vii) data governance so training, testing and operating data is accurate, complete, reliable and secure. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "effectively and systematically assessing the materiality and risks of AI use cases at inception and thereafter"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Sound practice 11 covers managing AI-related cyber and information and communication technology (ICT) risks: building AI cyber and ICT risk scenarios into tests and exercises, sharing relevant information with key stakeholders, and, where appropriate, using AI tools within cyber and ICT risk management. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "incorporating AI cyber and ICT risk scenarios into tests and exercises"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Sound practice 12 covers risks from the use of AI by third parties, with a focus on performance, transparency, data quality, supply chain and concentration risks, and business continuity. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "risks from AI third-party use with a focus on performance, transparency, data quality, supply chain and concentration risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Sound practices 8 to 10 are: (viii) understanding differences in explainability of types of AI and, if appropriate and feasible, adopting more explainable AI or considering compensating controls; (ix) evaluating AI use case performance proportionately to materiality and risk, including assessment, testing and ongoing monitoring; (x) appropriate and effective human oversight relevant to materiality, risk, autonomy, complexity and explainability. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "adopt more explainable AI or consider compensating controls"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: The board and senior management of financial institutions are strongly encouraged to reference the sound practices when considering business strategy, technology adoption and risk management in an increasingly AI enabled environment. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "are strongly encouraged to reference the sound practices as they consider business strategy"</sub>

## Introduction

- **descriptive / context**: Financial institutions have long used AI tools, but rapid evolution of generative AI (GenAI) and agentic AI has drawn significant interest. Uses include credit risk assessment, trading, portfolio optimisation, fraud prevention, transaction monitoring, customer service, coding assistance, and document summarisation and review. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "credit risk assessment, trading, portfolio optimisation, fraud prevention, transaction monitoring, customer service"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: AI may amplify or introduce risks; for example, complexity and limited explainability of certain AI types could increase model risk. Institutions may also need to consider the implications of not adopting AI, which may limit their ability to monitor and manage risks such as financial fraud. AI could increase the ease, frequency and impact of cyber attacks (including misuse of models able to autonomously discover and generate working exploits) while also improving defence. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "not adopting AI may limit their ability to monitor and manage certain risks such as financial fraud"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Responsible AI adoption means institutions need to understand and stay updated on AI opportunities and risks and respond with an appropriate adoption strategy and guardrails to manage evolving risks; at financial system level it reduces risks to financial stability. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "need to understand and remain updated on the opportunities and risks of AI"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report sets out benefits and risks of AI in the financial system (Section 1) and a menu of 12 sound practices that institutions could adopt for organisation-wide AI governance (Section 2) and management of AI lifecycle stages (Section 3). (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "contains a menu of 12 sound practices that financial institutions could adopt in their organisation-wide AI governance"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: While all sound practices are generally applicable to all types of AI, certain practices can be more relevant for specific AI technologies, such as responding to risks from the autonomy of AI agents. There is no standard one-size-fits-all set of AI lifecycle stages, since use cases may involve different stages of varying length (see Section 3.1). (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "certain practices can be more relevant for specific AI technologies"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The report states that proportionality should be considered when the sound practices in the report are applied. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Proportionality should be considered when applying the sound practices in this report"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Each financial institution should consider how relevant and applicable the sound practices are to it, based on its business model, complexity, role in the financial system, structure, size, and the extent to which AI is used or deployed in its critical (or material) functions. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Each financial institution should consider the relevance and applicability of these sound practices based on its business model"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Financial institutions may also refer to or adapt certain sound practices, depending on the materiality and risk of a specific AI use case, the impact and outcomes of that use case, and the type of AI being adopted. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial institutions may also refer to or adapt certain sound practices, depending on the materiality and risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Some sound practices are complemented by case studies drawn from actual AI implementations by financial institutions, intended to illustrate practical and proportionate application. The report builds on work by the FSB, other standard-setting bodies and national or regional authorities, and draws on inputs from financial institutions and their technology vendors. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "case studies drawn from actual AI implementation practices by financial institutions"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Adopting the sound practices does not absolve a financial institution from its obligations to meet local legal and regulatory requirements related to AI adoption. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "does not absolve a financial institution from its obligations to meet local legal and regulatory requirements"</sub>

## 1.1. AI adoption in the financial sector

- **descriptive / context**: Financial institutions use AI in anti-money-laundering (AML) and know-your-customer (KYC), credit risk, cyber security, customer engagement, fraud detection, internal process optimisation, collateral and portfolio management, and regulatory compliance, with potential to improve data analysis, risk monitoring and decision-making speed and accuracy and to reduce operating costs. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "anti-money-laundering (AML) and know-yourcustomer (KYC), credit risk, cyber security, customer engagement, fraud detection"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Supply-side drivers lowering barriers to AI adoption are advances in computing power (including cloud), growth in available data and wider availability of foundation models; demand-side drivers are institutions' wish to improve competitive position in digital markets, operational efficiency and risk management. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "greater availability of foundation models have lowered barriers to adoption"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Traditional AI and machine learning (ML) learn from data to make predictions, recommendations or decisions. Large language models (LLMs) analyse and generate human language by processing vast text data; GenAI creates new, often multimodal content (audio, images, text, video); agentic AI uses autonomous systems ('agents') able to plan, reason and execute complex high-level goals comprising multiple tasks independently. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "agentic AI has emerged whereby autonomous systems"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Risks from AI adoption are not all new, but some may be accentuated and some are newer and specific to technologies such as agentic AI. The FSB and relevant standard-setting bodies emphasise proportionate, risk-based, technology-neutral approaches, and authorities stress responsible adoption through strong governance, clear accountability and sound risk management especially in high materiality and high risk use cases. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "proportionate, risk - based, and technology - neutral approaches"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FSB assessed financial stability implications of AI in 2017 and updated that in 2024; given the rapidly evolving technology, the benefits and challenges in the section remain subject to future FSB reassessment. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "remain subject to future reassessment by the FSB"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: AI adoption appears to have accelerated since the 2024 FSB report, though unevenly across entities, sectors and jurisdictions. Surveys and industry engagement suggest internationally active banks, large or sophisticated trading firms and asset managers, and payment providers show relatively advanced use, and several use cases (general AI productivity tools, back and middle-office efficiency, risk management) cut across activities. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "internationally active banks, large or sophisticated trading firms and asset managers, and payment providers show relatively advanced use"</sub>

## 1.2. Use cases and benefits of AI in the financial system

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Capital markets and asset management: systematic trading firms and large asset managers are early adopters of traditional ML for portfolio optimisation and alternative data. Current use cases include algorithmic and high frequency trading, customer service and research, market-surveillance, pattern recognition, sentiment analysis and volatility forecasting; adoption varies and some participants plan to increase it. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "algorithmic and high frequency trading; customer service and research; market-surveillance; pattern recognition"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Payments: AI integration is especially strong in customer facing use cases such as behavioural risk analytics, onboarding and KYC; financial institutions report improved accuracy and processing speed of KYC checks. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "behavioural risk analytics, onboarding, and KYC"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Financial market infrastructures (FMIs): exploring detection of anomalous transactions and data inconsistencies, AI-driven settlement, reconciliation and compliance systems, predictive analytics for enhanced stress testing, and credit default early-warning indicators for early detection of vulnerabilities. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "detection of anomalous transactions and data inconsistencies"</sub>

## Credit scoring

- **descriptive / context**: In credit scoring, AI is adopted to improve accuracy using advanced analytics on large volumes of financial and behavioural data. Banks use models such as decision-trees and neural networks, developed in-house or by a third party and hosted on premise or on external cloud; in some jurisdictions smaller institutions partner with AI providers to build credit scoring tools. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "different types of AI models, such as decision-trees and neural networks"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: AI-based credit scoring can let lenders better assess credit risk where traditional methods are less effective and broaden access to financial services when paired with alternative data such as payment behaviour, cash flow data or non-traditional financial information. Footnote 14 describes alternative data as information not typically in the consumer's credit file at nationwide reporting agencies or not customarily provided by consumers in credit applications. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "broaden consumer access to financial services when paired with alternative data"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Some banks use AI to enhance SME credit underwriting; in several jurisdictions GenAI tools automate data extraction from financial documents and generate structured narratives supporting credit decisions, reducing processing times and improving consistency and efficiency. Several banks have traditional AI credit decision models, particularly in credit card and auto portfolios, combining methods such as gradient boosting machines or neural networks with alternative data to approve or decline credit requests or line increases, which may help lower default rates and expand access. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "gradient boosting machines or neural networks, with alternative data to approve or decline new credit requests"</sub>

## Case study: Machine learning for credit risk management

- **descriptive / case example**: A large regional bank embedded ML in credit risk operations, especially SME lending, with models generating alerts on early cashflow deterioration. In 2022 it identified over 95% of non-performing SME loans at least three months before repayment difficulties, enabling early engagement such as restructuring repayment schedules or adjusting credit facilities; over 80% of identified at-risk borrowers were averted from default altogether. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identified over 95% of non-performing SME loans at least three months before"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: A medium-sized regional commercial bank built an 'AI Approver Assistant' using GenAI in SME credit underwriting to automate data extraction, generate credit narratives and support risk detection. It reached over 80% accuracy in financial data extraction, approvers rated more than 70% of GenAI-generated content valuable reference material, preliminary credit proposal time fell from more than a day to 15 minutes, and it matched manual review in identifying data inconsistencies and potential fraud signals across all test cases. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "achieved over 80% accuracy in financial data extraction"</sub>

## Trading, asset management, and portfolio optimisation

- **descriptive / finding or result**: AI supports automated portfolio reporting, sentiment analysis (for example parsing earnings calls), liquidity forecasting and volatility modelling, pattern detection and signal extraction from alternative data. Research indicates productivity gains and cost savings in trading algorithm design and data processing; there is little evidence of GenAI being used for fully autonomous trading, but institutions are exploring agentic AI that could autonomously sequence trading tasks such as scanning news, generating signals and preparing execution instructions. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "little evidence at present of GenAI being used for fully autonomous trading"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Some asset managers, notably in private equity, use AI-supported tools to identify investment opportunities. Some large internationally active banks and institutional broker-dealers adopted research and analytics assistants for traders; for example a research agent can compare hedging strategies across several market data sources in real time, and initial results suggested significant time savings. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a research agent can compare different hedging strategies by analysing several market data sources in real time"</sub>

## Regulatory compliance, AML/CFT, and fraud detection

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Several institutions use AI to improve fraud detection and financial crime monitoring by analysing large volumes of transactions and unstructured data. Traditional AI such as neural networks and random forest algorithms can enhance anomaly detection and uncover hidden relationships between transactions. AI can enhance AML/KYC checks by automating document verification, identity checks and fraud detection, with biometric verification and contextual analysis strengthening security and reducing manual work. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "neural networks and random forest algorithms) can enhance anomaly detection"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: AI is increasingly used to detect identity fraud, synthetic identities and deepfake-based cyber-attacks, often in real time. Authorities have noted reductions in false positives and negatives and better identification of complex criminal patterns. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Authorities have noted reductions in false positives and negatives"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Some large asset managers use AI for compliance review of marketing materials; some large FMIs, notably exchanges, use AI for market surveillance and abuse detection; banks and payment service providers widely use ML for real-time fraud prevention in payment transactions; and some medium to large institutions report GenAI in financial crime and AML investigations, correlating sources and generating investigative narratives with time savings and improved consistency and quality. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "use of GenAI tools in financial crime and AML investigations"</sub>

## Case study: Strengthening fraud detection with agentic AI

- **descriptive / case example**: A large internationally active bank deployed an agentic AI system to detect emerging fraud and scam patterns in real time. Its existing AI monitored more than 80 million signals a day across transactions, card and online payments and digital banking interactions; the agentic system identifies emerging threats and autonomously proposes detection rules, and was built in-house by data science and engineering teams in three months on a cloud data platform and core banking infrastructure. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "monitored more than 80 million signals each day"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In the agentic AI fraud detection case study, the agent runs continuously, assessing the severity of suspicious patterns, analysing context and generating proposed detection rules for review. The process builds in human-in-the-loop oversight: the bank's fraud analytics team reviews and approves every new rule before it is implemented. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "embeds human-in-the-loop oversight to review and approve all new rules"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The agent has contributed to developing or updating three quarters of the bank's card fraud rules and helped cut fraud losses by over 20% in the first half of the 2026 financial year, compared with the same period in 2025. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "reduce fraud losses by over 20% in the first half of the 2026 financial year"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: On scale, the bank processes millions of payments each day and sends thousands of proactive fraud warning alerts to customers through its mobile banking application. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Daily, the bank processes millions of payments and sends thousands of proactive fraud warning alerts"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Separately, a digital bank enhanced its facial recognition solution (comparing facial images with a customer database for digital fraud detection) to detect suspicious image backgrounds, such as those associated with mule accounts or fraudulent identity attempts. It extracts facial feature vectors for rapid one-to-many comparison while applying object detection and background similarity analysis to flag risk indicators, described as using AI in defence against AI-generated content. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "extracts facial feature vectors for rapid one-to-many comparison"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The enhancement lets the bank identify controllers of mule accounts by correlating facial images with suspicious background attributes, a previously unavailable capability. It creates a risk alert for digital fraud including account takeover, deepfake or injection attacks and loan applications with fake identities, supports network analysis of fraudulent activity and the AML and fraud investigation framework, with comparisons completed in seconds. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identify controllers of mule accounts by correlating facial images with suspicious attributes in image background"</sub>

## Customer-focused applications

- **descriptive / finding or result**: AI is used by some institutions to enhance customer engagement in portfolio management, marketing and customer service, including tailored financial advice. Some institutions experiment with LLM-enabled tools for internal knowledge retrieval, customer complaint triage and document classification. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "automating customer complaint triage and document classification"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Institutions use AI for robo-advisory services (customer profiling, investment planning, portfolio management, rebalancing); some asset managers use AI to automate data collection, market analysis and monitoring for advisory services. AI chatbots and virtual assistants handle routine queries, payments and loan requests, improving response times, availability and satisfaction while reducing reliance on call centres, and AI is widely used in marketing for campaigns, leads and content. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI - driven chatbots and virtual assistants can handle routine queries, payments and loan requests"</sub>

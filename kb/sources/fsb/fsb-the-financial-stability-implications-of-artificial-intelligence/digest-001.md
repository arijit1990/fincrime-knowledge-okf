## Executive summary

- **descriptive / context**: The financial sector has long used AI, but adoption has widened and use cases diversified in recent years, notably with generative AI (GenAI) and large language models (LLMs), driven by deep learning, big data, computational power and software and hardware improvements; large amounts of unstructured data, cloud computing advances and wider deployment of pre-trained models also contributed. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "adoption has become more widespread and use cases have become more diverse in recent years"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The lack of comprehensive data on AI adoption complicates in-depth assessment of use cases, but available evidence suggests notable acceleration of AI adoption in financial services. Most use cases focus on enhancing internal operations and improving regulatory compliance; use cases generating new revenue streams are not widely observed at present. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "use cases generating new revenue streams are not widely observed at present"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: LLMs and GenAI have created new use cases such as document summarisation, information retrieval and code generation. Financial institutions (FIs) are becoming more aware of AI risks; many appear cautious about GenAI, but interest remains high and the technology's accessibility could speed integration. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "While many appear to be taking a cautious approach to using GenAI, interest remains high"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Financial authorities have also adopted AI for supervisory responsibilities and this may increase to keep up with FIs. The fast pace of innovation and integration, together with limited data on AI usage, poses challenges for monitoring vulnerabilities and potential financial stability implications. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "poses challenges for monitoring vulnerabilities and potential financial stability implications"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: AI offers benefits such as improved operational efficiency, regulatory compliance, personalised financial products and advanced data analytics, but may amplify certain financial sector vulnerabilities and pose financial stability risks. Several AI-related vulnerabilities stand out for potential to increase systemic risk (listed in the next points). (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it may also potentially amplify certain financial sector vulnerabilities and thereby pose risks to financial stability"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Vulnerability 1, third-party dependencies and service provider concentration: reliance on specialised hardware, cloud services and pre-trained models has increased AI-related third-party dependencies, and the market for these is highly concentrated, which could expose FIs to operational vulnerabilities and systemic risk from disruptions affecting key providers. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Third-party dependencies and service provider concentration"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Vulnerability 2, market correlations: widespread use of common AI models and data sources could increase correlations in trading, lending and pricing, amplifying market stress, exacerbating liquidity crunches and increasing asset price vulnerabilities; increasing automation in financial markets could exacerbate AI-driven correlations. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could lead to increased correlations in trading, lending, and pricing"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Vulnerability 3, cyber: AI uptake by malicious actors could increase the frequency and impact of cyber attacks; intense data usage, novel ways of interacting with AI services and greater use of specialised service providers increase the number of cyber attack opportunities. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI uptake by malicious actors could increase the frequency and impact of cyber attacks"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Vulnerability 4, model risk, data quality and governance: complexity and limited explainability of some AI methods and the difficulty of assessing data quality for widely used models could increase model risk for FIs lacking robust AI governance; opaque training data sources complicate data quality assessment; and new inaccuracies such as hallucinations complicate understanding output quality and accuracy. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could increase model risk for FIs that lack robust AI governance"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Beyond these vulnerabilities, GenAI could increase financial fraud and the ability of malicious actors to generate and spread disinformation in financial markets; misaligned AI systems not calibrated to operate within legal, regulatory and ethical boundaries can behave in ways that harm financial stability; and longer-term AI uptake could change market structure, macroeconomic conditions and energy use, with possible implications for financial markets and institutions. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "GenAI could increase financial fraud and the ability of malicious actors to generate and spread disinformation"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report says existing financial policy frameworks address many AI-related vulnerabilities, but more work may be needed to ensure they are sufficiently comprehensive. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "existing financial policy frameworks address many of the vulnerabilities associated with use of AI by FIs"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: The FSB, standard setting bodies (SSBs) and national authorities may wish to: consider ways to address data and information gaps in monitoring AI use in the financial system and assessing financial stability implications; assess whether current regulatory and supervisory frameworks adequately address the identified vulnerabilities, domestically and internationally; and consider ways to enhance regulatory and supervisory capabilities for overseeing policy frameworks on AI in finance, for instance through international and cross-sectoral cooperation and sharing of information and good practices. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FSB, standard setting bodies (SSBs) and national authorities may wish to"</sub>

## 1. Introduction

- **descriptive / context**: The report gives a high-level overview of recent AI developments and their potential financial stability implications, revisiting the 2017 FSB report on AI in financial services. The FSB assessed member jurisdictions' experience, reviewed literature and held bilateral and multilateral outreach including an OECD-FSB joint AI roundtable. Footnote 1 adopts the OECD definition of an AI system: a machine-based system that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers from input how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "revisits the 2017 FSB report on AI in financial services"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The 2017 FSB report identified use cases: customer-focused (assessing credit quality, automating client interactions), operations-focused (capital optimisation, model risk management) and applications for trading, portfolio management, RegTech and SupTech. FIs and service providers now explore GenAI uses such as customer support, fraud detection, market analysis, document processing, information retrieval and software development; AI use by FIs and malicious actors may also increase important sources of financial sector vulnerabilities. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "customer support, fraud detection, market analysis, document processing, information retrieval, and software development"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Report structure: Section 2 reviews developments since 2017 including supply and demand-side drivers; Section 3 discusses selected use cases by industry and official sector authorities; Section 4 discusses financial stability implications and how AI could amplify specific vulnerabilities; Section 5 concludes with policy implications; Annex 1 covers the LLM supply chain. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Section 4 discusses implications for financial stability, focusing on how AI could amplify specific types of financial sector vulnerabilities"</sub>

## 2. Developments in AI since 2017

- **descriptive / context**: The most salient AI developments since 2017 are advances in deep learning, big data, computational power ('compute') and GenAI; interest rose particularly after the launch of ChatGPT in November 2022, alongside steady growth in AI professionals and AI patent applications. Supply factors may play a bigger role today than in 2017; demand-side drivers (profitability, competition, regulation) have not materially changed and are reviewed succinctly. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "launch of ChatGPT in November 2022"</sub>

## 2.1. Supply-side factors

- **descriptive / context**: Supply-side factors, comprising technological, data-related and business model developments, have been the major driver of changes in AI adoption by FIs since 2017. Footnote 6 notes that 2017 financial sector factors included electronic trading platforms, computerised markets, retail scoring systems and growing data availability, and that Covid-19 lockdowns further increased digitisation. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supply-side factors have been the major driver of changes in AI adoption by FIs since 2017"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Three developments stand out: (1) continued improvement of deep learning models for handling unstructured data via embeddings; (2) the transformer architecture, which revolutionised natural language processing (NLP) and underpins GenAI and LLMs and makes the technology more accessible through ordinary text and speech; (3) wider integration of GPUs with higher compute, enabling larger datasets and more complex models at lower cost. The IMF estimates AI investment in software, hardware and services in financial services could reach $400 billion by 2027, up from $166 billion in 2023. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could reach $400 billion, up from $166 billion in 2023"</sub>

## 2.1.1. Technological developments

- **descriptive / definition**: Box 1 on early AI: AI and ML were used in finance long before LLMs, mainly for automating routine tasks, detecting fraud and predicting from historical data. ML is a subset of AI using algorithms to learn from data; neural networks, inspired by the human brain, are effective at recognising patterns and complex relationships in large datasets; deep learning, a subset of ML, uses multi-layered neural networks to extract complex patterns and significantly improve prediction accuracy. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "used for automating routine tasks, detecting fraud, and making predictions based on historical data"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Box 1 on LLMs and GenAI: the November 2022 launch of consumer-facing LLM chatbots shows how quickly AI can change; LLM chatbots are a specialised, language-focused application of generative AI, whereas GenAI models generate new content such as images, text or video from user prompts. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "LLM chatbots are a specialised application of generative AI with focus on language"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Box 1 on NLP: NLP enables machines to recognise, process and understand language and has long been used in financial services for customer interaction, regulatory compliance monitoring, automated advice and sentiment analysis of customer feedback. Before LLMs, advanced NLP turned words into tokens and then numerical vectors capturing meaning ('word embedding'), with the limitations that words have multiple meanings (for example 'interest') and whole-text context gives more nuance than surrounding words. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there are limitations to this approach: words can have multiple meanings"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Box 1 on the Transformer: this deep learning architecture, foundational to LLMs, overcomes the word-embedding limitation with an attention mechanism focusing the network on specific parts of text (for example distinguishing 'bark' of a dog from bark of a tree); positional encoding captures word order; a feed-forward mechanism helps predict the next word; and data is processed in parallel rather than sequentially, giving efficiency gains. Footnote 10 notes the attention mechanism is more accurately 'multi-head' attention, running multiple networks in parallel to capture different meanings of the same word. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "incorporating an attention mechanism, which focuses the neural network on specific parts of the text"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Box 1 limitations: LLMs and GenAI mimic human language and creativity, but currently available models do not truly understand the content they generate because outputs result from a stochastic process. Future advances, possibly from other AI sub-fields, could reshape the financial system unpredictably and introduce new vulnerabilities, underscoring continued monitoring, research and policy consideration by authorities. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the currently available models do not truly understand the content they generate"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: There may be growing demand for workers addressing model and data risk (for example in compliance or risk management), but human capital has not kept pace with technology, so uptake may be constrained by staffing limits; workers with specialised AI model development skills are already scarce and costly and will be more so for GenAI. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "human capital has not kept pace with these developments"</sub>

## 2.1.2. Data-related developments

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Increasing digital customer engagement and collection of large amounts of unstructured data from various channels are key drivers of AI adoption; availability, size and use of datasets have grown since 2017. Large unstructured data (for example videos, satellites, images) can be used with traditional quantitative datasets, and complex ML models, particularly GenAI models with billions of parameters, can parse such data while needing large volumes of data for their own training. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "collection of large amounts of unstructured data from various channels are key drivers for AI adoption"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The data-hungry nature of recent models raises the prospect that high-quality real data might be exhausted, with estimates suggesting a limit as early as 2026. Synthetic data, including LLM-produced data, is emerging as a response, also to privacy and intellectual property challenges, but has risks and limitations: quality of the generating model and dataset, reliance on known data generating processes, and diminished information from the tails of the distribution with continued reliance (footnote 15 describes synthetic data as computer-generated data based on real data). (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "diminishes the information coming from the tails of the distribution"</sub>

## 2.1.3. Business model developments

- **descriptive / context**: Two business model developments stand out: wider reliance on cloud computing and increasing development and use of pre-trained AI models. FIs have become more willing to use cloud services in recent years, including for business-critical areas, which may shape readiness to use third-party providers for AI; many firms rely on cloud providers for AI models, particularly GenAI, because of the cost of infrastructure and training. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "wider reliance on cloud computing and the increasing development and usage of pre-trained AI models"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Footnote 18 cites cloud adoption data: a 2019 Information Age survey suggested 70% of financial services firms were only at initial trial or testing stage for cloud adoption; a 2021 Google and Harris Poll survey suggested 83% of 1,300 financial services firms globally had adopted some form of public or hybrid cloud; and a Cloud Security Alliance report suggested cloud adoption in business-critical areas rose from around 17% in 2020 to around 32% in 2023, without defining 'business critical'. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "83% of 1,300 financial services firms globally had adopted some form of public or"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report describes cloud computing as having made it possible to deliver services flexibly, such as computing power, information storage and software use, through virtual data and processing capabilities. It adds that FIs have grown more willing in recent years to use cloud services, including for business-critical areas, which may be shaping their readiness to use third-party providers for related services such as AI. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Cloud computing has enabled the flexible delivery of services, such as computing power, information storage, and software usage"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Wider use of pre-trained models, particularly LLMs, has accelerated considerably and contrasts with traditional in-house development. The shift could drive adoption because FIs can leverage pre-existing models without extensive AI expertise or resources, but could also hinder it because of explainability and accountability concerns. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it could also hinder adoption due to explainability and accountability concerns"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: There appears to be a growing trend toward open-source models. Closed models have traditionally outperformed open-source ones on benchmarks, but open-source performance has improved significantly, with some leading open-source LLMs on par with top closed models for many tasks. Developers typically access closed models through the provider's API or a cloud-based channel, often limited to specific cloud platforms; open-source models are generally free, highly accessible and customisable, reducing investment in LLM applications. Footnote 19 notes debate over how open source LLMs really are, since users often have limited access to code and training data. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "open-source model performance has significantly improved, with some leading open-source LLMs now on par"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: In 2023, 66% of newly released foundation models were open source, up from 33% in 2021, and the number of open-source AI-related projects on GitHub rose from 845 in 2011 to 1.8 million in 2023. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In 2023, 66% of newly released foundation models were open source, up from 33% in 2021"</sub>

## 2.2. Demand-side factors

- **descriptive / context**: The 2017 report identified three demand-side drivers of AI adoption in FIs: profitability, competition and regulation; they remain significant but have not materially changed since 2017. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "factors driving increased adoption of AI in FIs: profitability, competition, and regulation"</sub>

## 2.2.1. Profitability

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Profitability opportunities from AI still drive adoption: revenue generation (better customer targeting with tailored products and services, better onboarding, reduced churn), cost reduction through automating manual tasks, and risk management applications of ML such as predicting expected cash flows, delinquencies and excess losses. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Cost reduction benefits are generated through efficiency savings from automating manual tasks"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: GenAI could improve worker productivity in report writing, email drafting, information retrieval and code generation; multiple studies find GenAI access raises productivity, though some suggest benefits concentrate among novice or low-skilled workers. Efficiency gains could let firms devote more time to revenue-generating activities that are harder to automate. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "some suggest the benefits are concentrated among novice or low-skilled workers"</sub>

## 2.2.2. Competition

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Competition is described as perhaps the most influential demand-side factor: FIs fear being left behind by peers if they fail to adopt AI, particularly GenAI, which has supported cautious experimentation; pressure is likely to intensify as models are deployed in customer-facing applications. Over the medium to long term this could foster innovation, business dynamism and lower prices, but competition may also cause harm if firms deploy AI without adequate testing or safeguards, and may hurt incumbents and workers left behind. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "if firms deploy AI technologies without adequate testing or safeguards in place"</sub>

## 2.2.3. Regulatory compliance

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Improving regulatory compliance more efficiently is another demand-side driver: in 2022 UK FIs cited compliance, anti-money laundering/counter-terrorist financing (AML/CFT) and Know-Your-Customer (KYC) rules as the most critical areas for ML applications in their business. More broadly, increasing regulatory requirements over seven years (for example on data protection), growing use of principles guiding AI development and a growing body of international standards have led firms to leverage AI to enhance compliance capabilities. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "compliance, anti-money laundering/counter-terrorist financing (AML/CFT) and Know-YourCustomer (KYC) rules as the most critical areas"</sub>

## 4.2.1. Third-party dependencies and service provider concentration

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Table 5 factor that increases LLM/GenAI market concentration, cost and complexity: high cost and complexity of training LLMs create barriers to entry; training from scratch needs considerable monetary and human capital; and where end-users' systems are designed around specific models, pivoting to alternative model providers may be costly. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Barriers to entry driven by the high cost andcomplexity of training LLMs."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Table 5 factor that increases concentration, supply chain constraints: persistent scarcity of accelerated computing chips, particularly GPUs; as a key driver of cost, supply constraints may confer a competitive advantage on GPU-rich firms. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Persistent scarcity of accelerated computingchips, particularly GPUs."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Table 5 factor that increases concentration, investment behaviour: significant or controlling investments by incumbent firms in frontier AI labs. Major technology firms are already important benefactors of leading AI labs; funding and infrastructure support have let startup labs compete but also created strong ties between labs and incumbent technology firms. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Significant or controlling investments by incumbentfirms in frontier AI labs."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Table 5 factor that increases concentration, vertical integration: consolidation of multiple AI supply chain activities in a limited number of firms, with certain firms important providers of various combinations of hardware, software, models and infrastructure services. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Consolidation of multiple AI supply chain activities ina limited number of firms."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Table 5 factor that increases concentration, multimodality: development of and demand for models that perform many or most GenAI tasks better than task- or domain-specific models; such model training is more likely to be dominated by a limited number of AI firms. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is more likely to be dominated by a limited number of AI firms."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Table 5 factors that could decrease concentration: (a) open-source models, through continued performance improvements and availability of open-source LLMs (footnote 63: competitive open models could decrease concentration vulnerabilities but could increase certain cyber-related risks); (b) architectural breakthroughs in ML architectures that improve efficiency and scalability of LLM training relative to the dominant transformer architecture; (c) GPU market competition that increases supply and reduces prices for GPUs; (d) model specificity, i.e. development of and demand for task- and domain-specific models, which could support market competition. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Continued performance improvements in andavailability of open-source LLMs."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 62: Kapoor and others describe publicly available foundation models as "open" rather than "open source" because often only the model weights are released. Footnote 64: Gu and Dao introduced a language modelling architecture (Mamba) whose computing power needs scale linearly with input sequence length, as opposed to quadratically as in the Transformer. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "describe publicly available foundation models as"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The systemic relevance of third-party dependencies and service provider concentration will depend on technological penetration and the criticality and substitutability of AI services. If AI emerges as a critical service in financial markets, the interaction of dependencies and concentration may reduce FIs' ability to mitigate losses from operational impairments, including cyber events, supply chain disruptions and other shocks affecting third- and nth-party providers. Such losses can stem from disruption to important services, breakdowns in risk management products, and failures in cyber event detection systems, among other sources. Synergies with a given provider may in certain cases promote risk management efficiency and effectiveness, and overall resilience. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "will depend on technological penetration and the criticality and substitutability of AI services."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 65: the FSB third-party risk management toolkit defines a critical service as a service provided to a financial institution whose failure or disruption could significantly impair its viability, critical operations or ability to meet key legal and regulatory obligations; critical services are institution-specific and can change over time. Footnote 66: an nth-party service provider is part of a third-party service provider's supply chain and supports ultimate delivery of services to one or more financial institutions. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "whose failure or disruption could significantly impair a financial institution"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Some FIs may already use AI for core business lines and critical operations, but the criticality of AI in these use cases is unclear. Lenders, insurers and asset managers already deploy AI in areas central to their business, including credit decisioning, claims processing and portfolio management, and some firms use AI for risk management, where adequate management of credit, liquidity and market risk is essential for viability. The lack of representative data about AI usage makes it challenging to ascertain whether AI plays a central or complementary role and how integral service providers are. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The lack of representative data about AI usage makes it challenging to ascertain the extent"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Although most FIs appear to take a risk-based approach to GenAI adoption, interest is high and the technology's accessibility could facilitate rapid deployment. Major technology firms' focus on commercialising GenAI could soon increase viability of integrating it in critical operations, and several of the largest, most systemically relevant FIs have publicly unveiled LLM-based applications for price discovery and investment advisory services. As firms vet GenAI's information security, risk management and customer protection implications, it could be deployed for important regulatory compliance and risk management tasks and customer-facing services such as recommending payment, credit, insurance and investment products. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Several of the largest, most systemically relevant FIs have publicly unveiled LLM-based applications"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: One of the most compelling GenAI use cases in finance is coding assistance. Code generation tools could soon support engineers in developing and maintaining software that drives important services such as core banking, payment execution, claims processing and loan servicing platforms. Operational vulnerabilities could increase if FIs widely adopt and rely on the same core set of code generation tools. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Operational vulnerabilities could increase in financial markets if FIs widely adopt and come to rely on the same core set"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Third-party risk management frameworks establish expectations for mitigating risks from AI service providers. Micro-prudential authorities in many jurisdictions maintain third-party risk management standards applying to AI service providers, which typically include expectations for FIs to conduct due diligence and ongoing monitoring, including maintaining active vendor inventories and carrying out periodic risk assessments. The FSB's third-party risk management toolkit complements these by presenting strategies for assessing criticality of services, monitoring supply chain risks and identifying systemic third-party dependencies. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "including maintaining active vendor inventories and carrying out periodic risk assessments"</sub>

## 4.2.2. Market correlations

- **descriptive / finding or result**: AI uptake by FIs could be associated with widespread use of similar data sources and models, giving rise to greater market correlations. Common data and models could be driven by: herding behaviour (market participants imitate others' data and model choices); network externalities (third-party-trained models may improve through interaction with more end-users, incentivising multiple agents to use specific models); limited choice (few data sources and models meet acceptable performance, possibly driven by service provider concentration); and lack of transparency (model providers may not disclose training data sources, so end-users could unknowingly rely on the same data as others). (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The use of common data and models could be driven by several factors, including:"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Correlated AI approaches in portfolio management and trading execution, including identifying price signals, developing investment strategies, forecasting clients' trading patterns and conducting market impact analysis, can amplify volatility, exacerbate liquidity crunches in downturns and increase the probability of flash crashes. Models trained on similar data are more likely to make correlated predictions, which can lead to unexpected interconnections among FIs and has relevance beyond trading, such as credit decisioning and insurance pricing. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "can amplify volatility, exacerbate liquidity crunches during downturns, and increase the probability of flash crashes"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 71 states that regulatory frameworks already exist that aim to lessen the negative effects of market correlations so as to protect market integrity and efficiency, and cites two IOSCO publications: a 2011 paper on regulatory issues raised by the impact of technological changes on market integrity and efficiency, and a 2018 paper on mechanisms trading venues use to manage extreme volatility and preserve orderly trading. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There are existing regulatory frameworks that aim to mitigate the negative effects of market correlations"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Footnote 73 says that, although little is known about hedge funds' actual strategies, a few hedge funds claim that their strategies rest solely on AI, naming Aidyia Holdings, Cerebellum Capital and Taaffeite Capital as examples. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a few of claim their strategies are solely based on AI"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Shocks acting on a segment of the financial sector using the same models and data could affect that segment as if it were a single institution. Models calibrated to similar risk management standards can give rise to homogeneity in risk assessments and exacerbate pro-cyclicality, and many of these issues can be amplified by poor governance around model risk and lack of explainability (see Section 4.2.4). (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could affect the segment as if it were a single institution"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: FIs may derive correlated outputs from LLM and GenAI usage. FIs using LLMs for domain-specific applications generally rely on models already trained by a technology firm, and even when customised the core model is typically pre-trained. Most LLMs use the same underlying architecture and many are trained at least partly on common web crawl data (footnote: it is widely believed many foundation models are trained on the Common Crawl dataset). Homogenisation of training data and architecture can lead to correlated outputs that could amplify market stress and exacerbate liquidity crunches. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "many are trained, at least in part, on common sources of web crawl data."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Data-related correlations could increase if the volume of GenAI outputs on the internet grows and is recycled into future training. The impact on market correlations depends on how FIs integrate these technologies: if widely used to develop investment strategies, inform risk management and create new AI applications, usage could significantly increase correlation vulnerabilities; if used primarily for internal efficiencies such as document drafting, employee education and information retrieval, the impact is likely to be minimal. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Data-related correlations could increase if the volume of GenAI outputs on the internet grows"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: AI-driven market correlations could be exacerbated by increasing automation in financial markets: highly automated trading execution is a growing feature; corporations increasingly have semi-automated treasury management services; and many financial market infrastructures and payment systems are working to offer high-speed, around-the-clock money movement. Semi-automated corporate depositor behaviour reacting in real time to news and data about FIs and yields could have implications for funding and liquidity vulnerabilities, and coordinated reactions to extreme conditions, such as synchronous pulling of "kill switches", could deepen stress events. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI-driven market correlations could be exacerbated by increasing automation in financial markets."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: AI could also help reduce market correlations if it facilitates customised trading and investment strategies and increases diversity; if AI enables investment firms and advisors to develop truly customised trading and portfolio management approaches, correlations could fall. Asset and wealth management firms have been early adopters of customised LLM and GenAI tools for investment professionals, and if AI-powered customer engagement lowers barriers to entry in financial markets, capital market bases could diversify. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI could also help reduce market correlations if it facilitates building customised trading and investment strategies"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Transparency requirements and volatility control mechanisms could help mitigate AI-driven correlation vulnerabilities; regulators have implemented enhanced transparency requirements and exchanges have adopted circuit breakers in the past against risks from new technologies, automation and extreme volatility, and these could help here. Better data and surveillance mechanisms would be important complements, since correlations play out across firms and consistent monitoring can help authorities and market intermediaries take pre-emptive action on accumulating correlation-related imbalances. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Transparency requirements and volatility control mechanisms could help mitigate AI-driven correlation vulnerabilities."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: AI could increase cyber-related operational vulnerabilities by expanding threat actors' capabilities and attack opportunities. GenAI tools could increase the quantity, novelty and success of cyber attacks, lower barriers to entry for threat actors, speed up attack prototyping and aid social engineering, business email compromise, malware development, impersonation and synthetic identity creation. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "GenAI could lower barriers to entry for potential threat actors, speed up cyber attack prototyping and aid in social engineering"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Research has shown that leading LLMs can autonomously carry out successful cyber attacks. Intense data usage and unique ways of interacting with modern AI systems increase attack opportunities; important attack types in training and interacting with AI models include data and model poisoning (attackers manipulate training data or model weights) and prompt injection (attackers manipulate GenAI tools or LLMs to extract confidential information). (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "data and model poisoning (where attackers manipulate training data or model weights)"</sub>

## 4.2.3. Cyber

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report says the vulnerabilities discussed could raise both the likelihood and the impact of cyber attacks on the financial sector, which it describes as one of the most attacked industries. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could increase the likelihood and impact of cyber attacks on the financial sector"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Cyber attacks can damage financial stability when they are aimed at financial market infrastructures, central banks, systemically important firms, critical service providers, or more generally critical financial services that cannot easily be substituted. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Cyber attacks can harm financial stability if they target financial market infrastructures, central banks"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: AI advances could help cut cyber vulnerabilities, but over the near term malicious actors may gain more than legitimate ones from GenAI breakthroughs. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "in the near term, malicious actors may benefit more than legitimate actors from GenAI breakthroughs"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: FIs and public authorities already apply AI to detecting cyber anomalies and see value in GenAI tools for strengthening cyber defences. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs and public authorities already use AI for cyber anomaly detection and see value in GenAI tools"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In a recent survey of the Global Cyber Resilience Group, a body of cybersecurity experts from central banks, most respondents judged that GenAI brings more cybersecurity benefits than risks. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a majority of respondents saw GenAI as bringing more cybersecurity benefits than risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: LLMs and GenAI can be deployed to improve systems that detect cyber events, shorten incident response times, and automate routine tasks to free resources for investigative work. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "LLMs and GenAI can be deployed to help improve cyber event detection systems, speed up incident response times"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: GenAI can also be used to diagnose potentially malicious code and to help teach employees important cybersecurity standards and techniques. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "GenAI can also be used to diagnose potentially malicious code and to help educate employees"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Service disruptions or loss of confidence resulting from cyber incidents can amplify volatility and increase funding and liquidity vulnerabilities in markets. Cyber vulnerabilities also have micro-financial implications: banks and other FIs have seen increasing operational losses from a growing number of cyber incidents in recent years. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Banks and other FIs have seen increasing operational losses from a growing number of cyber incidents in recent years."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Evidence suggests FIs and regulators are taking a cautious, risk-based approach to adopting GenAI. In the short run, asymmetric uptake between legitimate and malicious actors, who likely do not observe the same guardrails, could increase cyber vulnerabilities. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "asymmetric uptake between legitimate and malicious actors"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The FSB, SSBs and national authorities have published a wide range of standards, guidance and toolkits supporting cybersecurity risk management and incident response. The US Treasury highlighted practices shared by FIs for managing AI-related cybersecurity risks, many derived from existing risk management standards: developing an AI risk management framework; integrating AI risk management into existing enterprise risk management frameworks (whether based on the three lines of defence recommended by the Basel Committee or a different principles-based approach); and integrating AI-specific questions in vendor due diligence, among others. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "integrating AI risk management in existing enterprise risk management frameworks"</sub>

## 4.2.4. Model risk, data, and governance

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Wider AI uptake could increase model risk to the extent models are harder to validate, monitor and correct, especially during crises. Limited explainability can impede evaluation of a model's suitability and soundness; excessive complexity and opacity can make it difficult to find independent, knowledgeable validators who can challenge development approaches; explainability issues can complicate end users' ability to diagnose and quickly address significant inaccuracies, more likely in extreme volatility or crisis; and AI systems may suffer accountability issues across their lifecycle. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Wider AI uptake could increase model risk in the financial system to the extent that such models are"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Understanding the quality and accuracy of LLM outputs is inherently challenging: outputs are typically unstructured text, making it more challenging, though not impossible, to generate error rates and conduct effective outcomes analysis. GenAI has also given rise to hallucination, where a model gives a seemingly confident but inaccurate response, which can be difficult to detect and evaluate. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "GenAI has also given rise to a new type of model inaccuracy, the hallucination"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Massive unstructured data sources and lack of transparency in training data for pre-trained models make data quality harder to assess; assessing data quality is a key aspect of model risk management and issues may be more pronounced with AI given the importance of data in driving model specifications. Training data sources may be opaque or unavailable (often for pre-trained models), and training may consume data types FIs are not used to evaluating. Footnote 102 notes explainability and data governance issues can expose FIs to legal, reputational and consumer compliance risks. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Assessing data quality is a key aspect of model risk management."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Model risk and data quality are difficult to manage without robust governance structures for vetting and monitoring AI usage and data sources. Policymakers have expressed concern that the accessibility and utility of modern AI tools can incentivise rapid adoption without commensurate controls, while the complexity of AI approaches and size and opacity of some training data can challenge governance mechanisms, especially if FIs lack the skills and expertise to evaluate AI adoption. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Model risk and data quality are difficult to manage if firms do not have robust governance structures in place"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnotes: explainability generally refers to how an AI approach uses inputs to produce outputs; in the AI context, accountability refers to the ability to audit, explain, justify and take responsibility for AI decisions and actions and their potential consequences. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "accountability refers to the ability to audit, explain, justify, and take responsibility for the decisions and actions of AI"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Regulated FIs under model risk management regimes are responsible for addressing AI-related model risk and data quality challenges. Model risk management standards typically set expectations for model validation, ongoing monitoring, outcomes analysis and assessing data quality; the use of AI will not absolve FIs from applicable risk management expectations; and the standards may recommend mitigation techniques for challenging circumstances, such as when data are limited or unavailable. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The use of AI will not absolve FIs from applicable risk management expectations."</sub>

## 4.2.5. Other vulnerabilities

- **descriptive / finding or result**: AI could be used to perpetuate fraud and disinformation in the financial system, while poorly aligned AI systems could introduce novel vulnerabilities. Table 6 and the section analyse how AI developments could increase financial fraud and disinformation, and concerns about AI systems not calibrated to operate within legal, regulatory and ethical boundaries. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI could be used to perpetuate fraud and disinformation in the financial system"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Table 6, fraud: GenAI is being used to perpetuate financial fraud, which was already on the rise in many jurisdictions globally; differing GenAI adoption rates between malicious and legitimate actors and challenges in detecting fake content could worsen fraud in the short run. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "GenAI is being used to perpetuate financial fraud, which was already on therise in many jurisdictions globally."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Table 6, disinformation: GenAI could enable malicious actors to generate and spread disinformation that causes acute crises, such as flash crashes and bank runs. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "GenAI could enable malicious actors to generate and spread disinformationthat causes acute crises"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Table 6, misalignment: misaligned AI systems, meaning those that do not work as intended or in line with legal and regulatory standards, can engage in behaviour that harms financial stability. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Misaligned AI systems—those that do not work as intended or in line with"</sub>

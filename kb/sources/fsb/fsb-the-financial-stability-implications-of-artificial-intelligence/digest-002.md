## 3. Selected use cases

- **descriptive / limitation**: The lack of comprehensive data on AI adoption by financial services firms complicates an in-depth assessment of use cases. Some survey evidence suggests firms are willing and able to switch quickly from experimentation to deployment, but the data present a mixed picture: many sources indicating high adoption are voluntary surveys focused on larger firms, whereas economy-wide measures indicate low adoption, at least for customer-facing activities. The gap between perceived and actual AI use could be due to competitive pressure to be seen to pilot or adopt AI, as evidenced by firms' marketing initiatives. (p. 13)[^original]

Table 1 is a non-exhaustive overview based on the OECD classification of AI systems tailored to financial services. Different AI models can be applied to the same use cases, and developers typically test multiple models depending on the task and the limitations of any model. Annex 2 includes further definitions. (p. 13)[^original]

## 3.1. Industry use cases

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Discussions with industry and authorities strongly suggest AI adoption has increased considerably since the 2017 FSB report, with material developments in the main 2017 categories (customer-facing, operations-focused, trading and portfolio applications) and new GenAI-enabled use cases such as summarisation and code generation. Increased regulatory scrutiny in some jurisdictions and greater awareness of risks have led to a cautious approach; many firms prefer less complex, easily interpretable AI models and "co-pilot" applications that support rather than replace human decision-making. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "many firms prefer to use less complex, easily interpretable AI models and so-called"</sub>

## 3.1.1. Customer-focused use cases

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Use of AI models in credit underwriting, insurance pricing, client-facing chatbots and marketing has grown since 2017. Greater data availability through initiatives such as open banking has enabled firms to build AI models assessing creditworthiness, which may particularly benefit customers with thin credit files or no credit record and help firms better predict default risk. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The use of AI models in credit underwriting, insurance pricing, client-facing chatbots and marketing has grown since 2017."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Survey evidence suggests firms are increasingly relying on ML to supplement traditional credit scoring by processing unstructured or large volumes of data during pre-approval. A UK survey found some firms starting to move away from traditional credit scoring by introducing ML-based decisioning for personal loans, such as auto finance. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "by processing unstructured or large volumes of data during the pre-approval process"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: AI applications in marketing customise products and improve customer retention; the 2017 report identified ML analysis of user behaviour to tailor campaigns and improve clickthrough and conversion rates, and these approaches have become more widespread. AI is now used to assign financial advisors who can potentially offer appropriate products at the right time, and GenAI may increase the efficiency and scalability of digital marketing by creating text and visual content customised to market segments. Footnote 34 notes recent research suggesting GenAI-generated advertisements outperform traditional advertisements. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI is now being leveraged to intelligently assign financial advisors who can potentially offer appropriate products at the right time."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Firms use ML models to improve pricing and risk management of insurance policies, including upgrading earlier approaches (e.g. generalised linear models) to ML models that predict the risk of specific policies or estimate individuals' behaviour (e.g. in automotive insurance). Other applications include automated insurance claims handling, which often incorporates human oversight in decision making. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Other applications include automated insurance claims handling, but these often incorporate human oversight in decision making."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: GenAI has moved deployed chatbots from rules-based towards LLM-based approaches offering more complex interactions. Since 2017 NLP-based chatbots went from experimentation to wider deployment, and LLM-based chatbots are a key area of ongoing experimentation likely to reach wider deployment across use cases including robo-advisory. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "NLP-based chatbots went from experimentation stage to wider deployment."</sub>

## 3.1.2. Operations-focused use cases

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Firms have shown greater willingness to pilot, test and deploy AI models in operations-focused use cases, including capital optimisation, model risk management, market impact analysis and code generation. Although customer-facing use cases get more attention, these back-office use cases may be of even greater importance for firms' bottom line and the wider financial system. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Firms have demonstrated greater willingness to pilot, test, and deploy AI models in operationsfocused use cases."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Some firms use AI to better manage volatility and liquidity risk; for example, some financial firms have built ML models to better assess their stock options book if volatility is higher or lower than predicted by traditional models. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Some firms are using AI to better manage volatility and liquidity risk."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Some firms are looking at AI for riskier uses such as optimising their regulatory capital requirements. A European Banking Authority (EBA) consultation response reports that some firms use, or plan to use, machine-learning techniques in fields such as default probability modelling, but explainability and similar issues still stand in the way of full-scale adoption. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "although issues such as explainability remain a barrier to full-scale adoption"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: GenAI has enabled new operations-focused use cases: improving information search and retrieval, content generation (automated text, image and video), voice transcription (voice-to-text and text-to-summary service requests), and code generation or legacy code streamlining. GenAI code generation could expand the 2017-identified use cases to more firms, since access to engineering staff with coding skills has historically been a barrier for many smaller firms. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These include improving information search and retrieval, content generation"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Many code generation tools are quite advanced, enabling firms to accelerate deployment of more traditional use cases such as fraud detection or credit underwriting; as a result GenAI could stimulate wider adoption of traditional AI models. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "enabling firms to accelerate the deployment of more traditional use cases such as fraud detection or credit underwriting"</sub>

## 3.1.3. Trading and portfolio management

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Quantitative approaches have long been used in trading and portfolio management and are expanding beyond traditional AI towards GenAI. Some firms use GenAI to assess market sentiment from text data such as earnings calls or regulatory disclosures, or to implement reinforcement learning for trade execution. LLMs may enable trading algorithms to better understand subtle relationships between words rather than relying on frequency counts, and portfolio management may benefit from increased automation of insights via GenAI tools alongside existing quantitative tools. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "LLMs may enable trading algorithms to better understand subtle relationships between words"</sub>

## 3.1.4. Regulatory compliance

- **descriptive / finding or result**: FIs' use of AI to comply with regulatory requirements (RegTech) has seen significant uptake, with AI tools deployed across a greater number and variety of fraud and AML/CFT use cases. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI tools are being deployed across a greater number and wider variety of fraud and AML/CFT use cases."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: AI for AML/CFT compliance and fraud detection was already identified in the 2017 report but has been more widely deployed since, to facilitate investigations into sanctions evasion, identify misuse of legal persons and legal arrangements, uncover trade fraud and trade-based money laundering, and detect tax evasion, fraud/scams and money mules. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to facilitate investigations into sanctions evasion, to identify misuse of legal persons and legal arrangements"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Footnote 43: the global RegTech market is forecast to reach $19.5 billion by 2026 as regulatory demands on firms increase. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The global RegTech market is forecasted to reach $19.5 billion by 2026"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Traditional AI may do better on specific tasks such as spotting potentially fraudulent transactions, whereas GenAI could potentially automate the drafting of financial crime reports by drawing on a variety of sources, supporting investigators. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "GenAI can potentially automate the generation of financial crime reports, incorporating a range of different sources"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report expects these regulatory-compliance uses to remain critical as firms face rising regulatory requirements and as financial fraud, itself partly driven by the use of technology, grows. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These are likely to remain critical use cases as regulatory requirements for firms increase"</sub>

## 3.2. Regulatory and supervisory use cases

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Financial authorities engage with AI through varied use cases, including supervisors' use of technology (SupTech). SupTech use has increased, with 59% of authorities surveyed using various applications in 2023, a 5-percentage point increase from 2022. AI can also assist central banks in tasks such as enhancing oversight of payment systems and information collection and statistical compilation supporting real-time analysis of economic activity. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "with 59% of authorities surveyed using various applications in 2023"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Many central banks and regulators are adopting AI to meet supervisory responsibilities more efficiently: exploring 'faster indicators' for real-time economic analysis, alternative data for supervisory assessments, and experimenting with LLMs for research, analysis and drafting. Some regulators use NLP and GenAI to analyse textual sources such as earnings call transcripts and management discussion and analysis sections of public filings. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Many central banks and regulators are adopting AI to meet their supervisory responsibilities more efficiently."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: NLP and GenAI are being tested for inspections: they can extract relevant paragraphs from large volumes of inspection documents and summarise and draft inspection reports. LLMs could be used to augment data quality assessments and customise regulatory reporting error messages for specific circumstances and jurisdictions. Proposed uses include GenAI to model social media interactions in the context of bank runs or stress testing, both in designing stress scenarios and in operationalising stress testing models in code. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these methods can extract relevant paragraphs from large volumes of inspection documents"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The use of AI in SupTech may increase in future, partly driven by authorities' need to keep up with and understand the rapid uptake of AI by FIs. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The use of AI in SupTech may increase in the future"</sub>

## 4. Financial stability implications of AI

- **descriptive / context**: Responsible, productive use of AI could bring significant benefits to financial institutions (FIs) and their customers, but financial stability could be harmed by fast AI uptake at FIs that lacks matching risk management and controls, by weak monitoring by financial authorities, and by novel uses by malicious actors. The report says the vulnerabilities it goes on to examine are not unique to AI, yet wider AI adoption by FIs could make them more relevant to financial stability. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "rapid AI uptake by FIs without commensurate risk management and controls, inadequate monitoring by financial authorities"</sub>

## 4.1. Key developments and monitoring challenges

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Table 2, development 1: wider integration of AI in financial services. In recent years powerful and complex AI methods have seen wider uptake in finance; FIs use AI for core business lines and operations, but the centrality of AI in these use cases remains unclear. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs use AI for core business lines and operations, but the centralityof AI in these use cases remains unclear."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Table 2, development 2: technological breakthroughs in LLMs and GenAI. Many FIs appear to take a prudent, risk-based approach to incorporating GenAI, but the technology's accessibility and competitive pressures could facilitate more rapid deployment; LLMs and GenAI will likely enable FIs to develop and deploy more traditional AI applications in coming years; and GenAI's increased automaticity, speed and ubiquity relative to previous AI generations could amplify several AI-related vulnerabilities in financial markets. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "LLMs and GenAI will likely enable FIs to develop and deploy moretraditional AI applications in the coming years."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Table 2, development 3: greater importance of specialised hardware and infrastructure services. AI application development by FIs and their AI service providers increasingly relies on a highly concentrated market for accelerated computing chips, and cloud services (already used by FIs for other computing) have become tightly integrated in aspects of AI development. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI application development by FIs and their AI service providersincreasingly relies on a highly concentrated market"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Table 2, development 4: increasing usage of unstructured and/or opaque training data sources. Diverse unstructured sources FIs may not be used to evaluating (text files, social media activity, images) are now widely used to train AI models, and several data-as-a-service providers have emerged to help firms process unstructured data. Training data sources are often opaque or unavailable for pre-trained models, so FIs using them face challenges applying traditional data quality assessment methods. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs seeking to use these models face challenges inapplying traditional data quality assessment methods."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Financial authorities face two key challenges in evaluating the financial stability implications of AI: significant uncertainty amid rapid innovation, and limited data on AI uptake. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial authorities face two key challenges in evaluating the financial stability implications of AI"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Recent AI developments (summarised in Table 2) have the potential to amplify certain financial sector vulnerabilities and so affect financial stability. The vulnerabilities, covered further in section 4.2, concern the interaction of third-party dependencies and service provider concentration, market correlations, cybersecurity and cyber fraud, and model and data risk, plus other emerging ones such as AI-driven disinformation. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "third-party dependencies and service provider concentration, market correlations, cybersecurity and cyber fraud, model and data risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 52, citing the FSB Financial Stability Surveillance Framework (2021), defines a vulnerability as a feature of the financial system that reflects the build-up of imbalances, may raise the likelihood of a shock, and may lead to a system disruption when a shock acts on it. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a property of the financial system that: (i) reflects the accumulation of imbalances"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 52, citing the same FSB framework, defines financial stability as the capacity of the global financial system to withstand shocks, containing the risk of disruption to financial intermediation and other system functions that is severe enough to adversely affect the real economy. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "capacity of the global financial system to withstand shocks, containing the risk of disruptions"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The rapid rate of change creates uncertainty about available AI technologies, services and uses, and vulnerabilities could evolve considerably with the pace of innovation and degree of AI integration. Some national authorities have data on AI model usage at regulated entities, but it tends to be irregular snapshots focused on narrow sets of institutions; scarcity of consistent, representative data poses significant challenges for vulnerabilities surveillance, and authorities have an even more limited view of AI use at FIs less subject to financial regulation or outside the regulatory perimeter. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The scarcity of consistent, representative data on AI usage poses significant challenges for conducting vulnerabilities surveillance"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Supervisory effectiveness could suffer if regulators' AI-related skills and knowledge do not keep pace; inadequate investment by authorities in the skills and resources needed to critically assess AI developments and FIs' use of AI could also pose risks to the financial system. In a survey of SupTech approaches at 64 financial regulators globally, most respondents reported skills deficiencies in data science and essential IT capabilities, and a survey of central bank cyber experts expressed similar concerns about skills and IT capital. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In a recent survey of SupTech approaches at 64 financial regulators globally"</sub>

## 4.2. Financial sector vulnerabilities

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Four AI-related vulnerabilities stand out for potential to increase systemic risk. First, AI-related third-party dependencies and concentration among technology and AI service providers could increase domestic and international interconnections, as major providers are located in few jurisdictions, exposing FIs to losses from operational impairments and supply chain disruptions affecting key vendors. Second, widespread use of AI models with similar behaviour or training data could increase market correlations, expand interconnections, amplify market stress and increase asset price vulnerabilities. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Four types of AI-related vulnerabilities stand out for their potential to increase systemic risk in financial markets."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Third, AI uptake by malicious actors could increase cyber vulnerabilities, through improved threat actor capabilities and more attack opportunities from expanding AI usage. Finally, the limited explainability of some AI methods and difficulty assessing the data quality underlying more widely used AI models could increase model risk for FIs without robust AI governance. Table 3 summarises how the Table 2 developments may affect these vulnerabilities. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the limited explainability of some AI methods and the difficulty of assessing data quality underlying more widely used AI models"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Table 3, third-party dependencies and service provider concentration: wider AI uptake in finance, increasing importance of specialised hardware and cloud services, and greater use of pre-trained models have created more AI-related third-party dependencies; complexity in AI supply chains, highly concentrated markets for AI development inputs and consolidation in the financial data aggregation market could increase service provider concentration vulnerabilities; and depending on the trajectory of AI penetration, greater reliance on and concentration among AI service providers can increase systemic third-party dependencies. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Depending on the trajectory of AI penetration in finance, greater relianceon and market concentration among AI service providers"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Table 3, market correlations: broader AI usage could lead to common modelling approaches and training data sources across FIs; greater uptake of pre-trained AI models could increase market correlations; and AI-driven correlation vulnerabilities could interact negatively with increasing automation in financial markets and with greater speed and accessibility enabled by financial market infrastructures. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Broader usage of AI in financial markets could lead to common modellingapproaches and training data sources across FIs."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Table 3, cyber: LLMs and GenAI could enhance cyber threat actors' capabilities and increase the frequency and impact of cyber attacks; intense data usage and novel modes of interacting with AI services increase the number of cyber attack opportunities; and greater use of specialised service providers exposes FIs to operational risk from cyber events affecting those vendors. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Greater usage of specialised service providers exposes FIs tooperational risk from cyber events affecting these vendors."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Table 3, model risk, data quality and governance: wider uptake of complex AI approaches could increase model risk for FIs unable to effectively validate, monitor and, when necessary, correct AI models; the importance of massive unstructured training datasets and lack of transparency in the training data of leading LLMs pose challenges for data quality assessments; and the accessibility of modern AI tools may incentivise rapid adoption without commensurate governance and controls. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The accessibility of modern AI tools may incentivise rapid adoptionwithout the development of commensurate governance and controls."</sub>

## 4.2.1. Third-party dependencies and service provider concentration

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Recent trends in AI usage and development have given rise to more third-party dependencies, which can create operational vulnerabilities for FIs. Third-party providers in the AI supply chain help FIs deploy effective AI applications within acceptable time and budget, but also expose them to operational vulnerabilities. Wider AI uptake, the increasing importance of specialised hardware and infrastructure services, and the prohibitive cost and complexity of training LLMs for non-specialist firms have increased the potential for third-party dependencies. Table 4 summarises three sources of dependencies (hardware, cloud services, models) provided by BigTech companies. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Recent trends in AI usage and development approaches have given rise to more third-party dependencies"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Table 4, hardware: FIs that develop AI models, customise pre-trained models and deploy AI in applications increasingly rely on accelerated computing chips such as GPUs and application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs), which they acquire directly from chip suppliers or rent from cloud service providers (CSPs). (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "increasingly rely on accelerated computing chips, such as GPUsand application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Table 4, cloud services: CSPs, which offer flexible computing power, easy access to cutting edge AI toolsets and convenient LLM access channels, have become tightly integrated in AI development, and major CSPs also train and disseminate LLMs. FIs have indicated that one of the primary drivers of cloud adoption involves AI capabilities, and FIs increasingly assume indirect cloud exposure through specialised AI service providers that often use cloud services. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs have indicated that one of the primary drivers of cloudadoption involves AI capabilities.57"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Table 4, models: at present the cost and complexity of training LLMs from scratch are generally prohibitive for non-specialist firms, so FIs customising and deploying LLMs in domain-specific applications generally use pre-trained third-party models. FIs have long used vendor-provided models for targeted applications such as fraud and cyber anomaly detection, and the scale and scope of AI model usage in vendor-provided risk management services is increasing. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs seeking to customise and deploy LLMs indomain-specific applications generally use pre-trained, third-party models."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Highly concentrated service provider markets exist across the AI supply chain, including hardware, infrastructure and data aggregation. Concentration vulnerabilities increase when many FIs rely on a limited set of providers for specific services or on the same provider for multiple services. Markets for accelerated computing chips and cloud services are dominated by a limited number of entities, and vertical integration exists as certain entities provide various combinations of hardware, software, cloud services and models. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Highly concentrated service provider markets exist across important aspects of the AI supply chain"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The financial data aggregation market has become increasingly consolidated as major data providers have acquired smaller competitors, and data from these providers are often key inputs to predictive models. AI services and related infrastructure tend to be increasing-returns-to-scale businesses, so some level of market concentration is difficult to avoid. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Fundamentally, AI services and related infrastructures tend to be increasing returns to scale businesses."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Service provider concentration in the market for LLMs and GenAI is a significant and growing concern from an operational vulnerability perspective. Factors that could increase LLM market concentration are high costs, complexity, persistent supply chain bottlenecks, investments and acquisitions, vertical integration, and demand for multimodal models (which process multiple forms of data such as text, images, audio and video); factors that could support a competitive market include competitive open-source models, architectural innovations, hardware market competition and task- and domain-specific models (Table 5; the table itself begins after this excerpt ends). (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is a significant and growing concern from an operational vulnerability perspective"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 59, attached to the statement that certain entities are key providers of various combinations of hardware, software, cloud services and models, notes that FIs use a wide range of important computing services from cloud service providers (CSPs) beyond those relevant to AI. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial institutions use a wide range of important computing services offered by CSPs beyond those relevant for AI."</sub>

## Fraud

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Financial fraud is on the rise in many jurisdictions and AI has played a role in facilitating fraud schemes, so the cost of tackling and rectifying fraud is increasing for many FIs. GenAI voice and video generation could pose considerable problems if malicious actors use it to generate fake IDs or profiles, including voices or video images (deepfakes), to bypass security checks or defraud customers. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial fraud is on the rise in many jurisdictions, and AI has played a role in facilitating fraud schemes."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: There is a risk that GenAI could be used to defraud FIs through, for example, submitting false insurance claims or business email compromise schemes. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "through, for example, submitting false insurance claims or through business email compromise schemes"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: AI can help FIs and authorities fight fraud, and fraud detection and prevention are consistently cited as key AI use cases in the financial sector, but GenAI may benefit malicious actors more than legitimate ones in the short run. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI can help FIs and authorities fight fraud, but GenAI may benefit malicious actors more than legitimate"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Two asymmetries could benefit fraudsters in the short run: (1) differing rates of AI usage between malicious actors and financial services providers, the latter proceeding more cautiously; and (2) synthetic content detection methods are still nascent and can be challenging to implement. In the near term it will likely be easier to generate fraudulent content with GenAI than to detect it. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "asymmetries along two dimensions could benefit malicious actors in perpetrating fraud in financial systems"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: In addition to FIs' efforts to fight fraud, improving financial and AI literacy of consumers and investors would also help mitigate fraud risks. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "improving financial and AI literacy of consumers and investors would also help mitigate the risks of fraud"</sub>

## Disinformation

- **descriptive / finding or result**: GenAI could enable more sophisticated and convincing disinformation at scale with financial stability implications if it causes acute crises such as flash crashes or bank runs, adversely affecting investors particularly in stress. Financial markets are susceptible to a wide range of information shocks and malicious actors need not target markets directly. Footnote examples of triggering disinformation include fake images of depositor withdrawal lines or deepfakes of finance executives and authorities expressing negative information about institutions or markets. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "GenAI could enable more sophisticated disinformation campaigns that have financial stability implications if they cause acute crises"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In May 2023 a fake image of an explosion at the US Pentagon, which was likely AI-generated, appeared to have affected equities markets, albeit briefly. OpenAI research found threat actors use GenAI tools to conduct influence operations, though it is unclear how far such campaigns have influenced public opinion. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In May 2023, a fake image of an explosion at the U.S. Pentagon, which was likely AI-generated"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: As the technology and threat actors' competence improve, disinformation could increase in financial markets. Such vulnerabilities could be exacerbated by social media's role in information dissemination, lack of financial and AI literacy among investors and consumers, and the increasing ease of transferring funds between FIs (see Section 4.2.2). (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Nevertheless, as the technology and threat actors"</sub>

## Misalignment

- **descriptive / finding or result**: In optimising profit maximisation objectives, poorly aligned AI systems could autonomously spread disinformation or engage in other behaviour that negatively affects markets. Alignment refers to AI systems that work as intended and in line with legal and regulatory standards. Since AI systems optimise pre-defined objectives, it can be difficult to specify up front all relevant objectives meeting regulatory and ethical expectations, with potentially adverse consequences for financial stability. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Alignment refers to AI systems that work as intended and in line with legal and regulatory standards."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Illustrative example: an AI system could implement a profit maximisation strategy that spreads disinformation about a bank with the goal of catalysing a bank run while simultaneously shorting the firm's stock. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "spreading disinformation about a bank with the goal of catalysing a bank run"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: There is growing evidence that AI systems may strategically coordinate and collude; research shows AI-powered algorithms consistently learn to charge higher prices through collusive strategies even without direct communication. Strategic coordination and algorithmic autonomy could be facilitated by reinforcement learning breakthroughs that have enabled autonomous AI systems to beat humans in highly complex strategy games. Footnote defines reinforcement learning as a branch of machine learning in which simulated agents learn strategies leading to the greatest long-term rewards through trial-and-error, solely from rewards and punishments. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is growing evidence that AI systems may strategically coordinate and collude"</sub>

## Longer-term considerations

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Box 2, competitive landscape: the 2017 FSB report projected the effect of AI uptake on financial market consolidation as ambiguous and scenario-dependent, and no clear evidence has emerged since. AI development and deployment currently require significant data and computing resources, and larger, better-resourced financial firms appear more engaged in evaluating AI for internal efficiencies. If building customised AI applications unlocks significant value, larger FIs could be best placed; if the most value lies in using accessible GenAI products as productivity tools, smaller FIs could compete more effectively, including by deploying traditional AI use cases more easily. Uneven regulatory and supervisory treatment within the sector and for new entrants may lead to regulatory arbitrage between sectors. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "no clear evidence has emerged since then"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Beyond the vulnerabilities already identified, AI uptake could bring wider changes to the structure of the financial system, to macroeconomic conditions and to energy use, which under certain circumstances could have implications for financial markets and institutions (see Box 2). The report says these effects would play out over the longer term and are not well understood at present. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The potential effects of these changes are longer-term in nature and not well-understood as of now."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Box 2, macroeconomic conditions: AI could drive long-term changes such as shifts in labour markets, inflation and interest rates, though magnitude, timing and distribution remain uncertain. GenAI could lead to productivity gains and affect output and wages; its facility with cognitive tasks poses risks for some high-skilled workers whereas most historical innovation waves automated routine tasks. AI-driven labour market dislocations could increase financial sector vulnerabilities through asset-quality and leverage channels by increasing delinquencies and debt-to-income ratios in unexpected ways, and structural changes in inflation and interest rates could affect borrowing and investment incentives. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI could drive long-term changes in the economy such as shifts in labour markets, inflation, and interest rates."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Box 2, energy use: AI-related energy consumption, estimated to account at present for about 1% of global energy consumption, is expected to increase and could affect energy demand. Inference may be more energy intensive than model training due to growing end-user interactions and massive computing clusters. To the extent FIs and markets depend on AI, their functioning would be exposed to broader energy supply issues, and sustained growth in AI energy use could affect climate change risks if not from clean energy. Mitigating factors include some technology firms' clean energy commitments and investments, and more energy efficient model training architecture. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "estimated to account at present for about 1% of global energy consumption"</sub>

## 5. Conclusion

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Since 2017 AI adoption in financial services has become more widespread and use cases more diverse. AI could deliver benefits such as improved operational efficiency, enhanced regulatory compliance, more personalised financial products and advanced data and analytics capabilities. Use cases generating new revenue streams are not widely observed at present, but rapid technological advancement and growing integration could change this; RegTech (e.g. for AML/CFT) in FIs and SupTech in the official sector have seen notable uptake since 2017. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Although use cases generating new revenue streams are not widely observed at present"</sub>

## Longer-term considerations

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Box 2 says that training, developing and running large AI models and applications need large amounts of reliable and competitively priced energy, which may compete with other energy consumers to secure the energy input required. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "require large amounts of reliable and competitive energy, which may compete with other energy consumers"</sub>

## 5. Conclusion

- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 127, attached to the point about technology firms' clean energy commitments, records that Microsoft has pledged to offset all of its carbon consumption by 2030, while Google has set the more ambitious goal of running its data centres on carbon-free energy sources 24/7 by 2030. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Microsoft has pledged to offset all carbon consumption by 2030."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Existing financial policy frameworks address many AI-related vulnerabilities, but additional work may be needed to ensure they are sufficiently comprehensive. Existing regulatory and supervisory frameworks already require FIs to address cyber and operational risks and to manage model and third-party risks, but AI developments could raise other issues needing policy consideration; some authorities have adopted or are considering AI-specific guidance beyond existing regulation. Future developments could introduce new vulnerabilities, underscoring continuous monitoring, research and policy consideration by authorities. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Existing financial policy frameworks address many of the vulnerabilities associated with AI adoption"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnotes: the US Executive Order on AI articulates that AI must be safe and secure and requires AI systems to comply with applicable Federal laws and policies; the FSB third-party risk management toolkit outlines strategies for monitoring and mitigating concentration risks and supply chain complexities and for identifying critical services and systemic third-party dependencies; the European Parliament and Council reached political agreement on the AI Act in December 2023 as they believe existing legislation is not sufficient for challenges such as transparency and explainability of general-purpose AI models; the OECD review of regulatory approaches was based on a survey of 49 jurisdictions. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "reached a political agreement on the AI Act in December 2023"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The conclusion states that AI use in financial services could amplify certain financial vulnerabilities, with potential implications for financial stability. It lists factors that have raised the potential systemic impact of AI use in finance: wider uptake of powerful and complex AI methods, novel characteristics of LLMs and GenAI, greater importance of specialised hardware and infrastructure services, and increasing use of unstructured and opaque data sources. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "greater importance of specialised hardware and infrastructure services, and increasing usage of unstructured and opaque data sources"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: These developments have the potential to increase vulnerabilities relating to third-party dependencies and service provider concentration, market correlations, cybersecurity and fraud, and model risk, together with other emerging vulnerabilities such as AI-driven disinformation. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "third-party dependencies and service provider concentration, market correlations, cybersecurity and fraud, model risk and other emerging vulnerabilities"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: AI uptake could also drive longer-term changes in macroeconomic conditions, in competition in financial markets and in energy use, which could have implications for financial markets and institutions. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI uptake could also drive longer-term changes in macroeconomic conditions, competition in financial markets, and energy use"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report identifies two key challenges for financial authorities in effective surveillance of vulnerabilities: how fast AI is changing, and the shortage of data on AI use in the financial sector. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the speed of AI change and the lack of data on AI usage in the financial sector"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report says these developments are not occurring in isolation but reinforce existing trends towards greater automaticity and speed in the financial system. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "reinforce existing trends towards greater automaticity and speed in the financial system"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The report says these developments underline the necessity for authorities to monitor AI developments and related innovations closely and holistically. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "They underscore the necessity for authorities to monitor AI developments and related innovations closely and holistically."</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: It may also be beneficial for authorities to intensify engagement with private sector participants, including FIs, AI developers and other third-party service providers, as well as academics, to stay abreast of developments. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "also be beneficial for authorities to intensify their engagement with private sector participants"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: The FSB, SSBs and national authorities may wish to assess whether current regulatory and supervisory frameworks adequately address the identified vulnerabilities, domestically and internationally. The FSB, along with SSBs and national authorities, could consider implications of sector-specific frameworks for the level-playing field across sectors and between established firms and new entrants such as fintech firms. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Assess whether current regulatory and supervisory frameworks adequately address the vulnerabilities identified in this report"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: The FSB, SSBs and national authorities may wish to consider ways to enhance regulatory and supervisory capabilities for overseeing policy frameworks related to AI in finance, for instance through international and cross-sectoral coordination. The FSB, with relevant SSBs, could consider facilitating cooperation by enhancing sharing of information and good practices across member jurisdictions, which may require involvement of non-financial authorities such as those responsible for data and privacy. Separately, financial authorities may consider leveraging AI-powered tools to enhance supervisory and regulatory capabilities through SupTech and RegTech. (p. 34)[^original]

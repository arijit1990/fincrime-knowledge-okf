## 6 Concept Drift

- **descriptive / context**: Fraud detection is an adversarial problem domain: when the decision system blocks a fraudster's attempt, the fraudster is likely to modify the attack until it succeeds, so fraud detection systems often experience concept drift nearly constantly. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "fraud detection is an adversarial problem domain. When the decision system is successful"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Data distributions generated by genuine users can also drift, for example through seasonal patterns, unexpected events such as COVID-19, or changes in the e-commerce platform; this drift is largely independent of blocked fraud attempts and is thus not adversarial. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Examples include seasonal patterns, unexpected events (e.g., COVID-19), or changes in the e-commerce platform."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Updates to upstream models (for example a login-time fraud model) shift the data reaching downstream models (for example a payment-time model), because the population of fraudsters already caught upstream changes with the update. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An update to a logintime fraud detection model shifts the distributions of the data"</sub>

## 6.1 Summary of the Literature

- **descriptive / definition**: Drift is categorised by distributional type: covariate shift is a shift in P(X), prior shift a shift in P(y), and real concept drift a shift in P(y|X). (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "covariate shift concerns a shift in _P_ ( _X_ ), _prior shift_ a shift in _P_ ( _y_ )"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Drift is also categorised temporally as sudden, gradual, incremental or recurring, and as adversarial (aimed at beating a detection system) or natural (exogenous to it). (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it can be _sudden_ , _gradual_ , _incremental_ , or _recurring_ ."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Fraudsters' changing attack patterns typically cause gradual and incremental drift, as successful undetected attacks grow in frequency while detected and unsuccessful ones decline. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "fraudsters tend to gradually increase the frequency of their successful and undetected attacks"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Fraudsters can cause recurring drift by occasionally retrying old attempts to check whether the detection systems still catch them. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they occasionally retry old attempts to check whether the fraud detection systems still catch them"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Drift from platform changes is often sudden because changes deploy at once, but changes may first be evaluated in A/B tests, so drift may initially affect only a fraction of users; it is natural rather than adversarial drift. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Drift due to changes in the e-commerce platform is often _sudden_ because changes"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Much adversarial concept drift literature ignores that tasks often must be performed in the presence of sudden and natural drift caused by platform changes. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Much of the adversarial concept drift detection and adaptation literature ignores that such tasks"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: There is a practical need for methods that can distinguish fraudsters' gradual adversarial drift from sudden, natural drift caused by platform changes. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is a practical need for methods that can distinguish the fraudsters"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: With delayed labels, concept drift is only detectable when a change in P(y|X) is accompanied by a change in P(X), and adaptation to a change in P(y|X) is not possible without a change in P(X). (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Until the labels are known, concept drift is only detectable when a change"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Methods for concept drift adaptation under delayed labels include positive unlabeled learning and explicit survival modelling of expected label delay per instance. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "including _positive unlabeled_ (PU) learning [40,22], or by explicit modeling"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Dal Pozzolo et al. proposed a fraud-specific solution training two models: one on labels from fraud investigators and one on labels from the much more delayed escalations. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The first model is trained on the labels found by _fraud investigators_"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Some fraud use cases have theoretically upper-bounded label delay, such as credit card chargebacks with a deadline set by card issuers. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "such as in the case of credit card chargebacks that have a deadline set by the credit card issuers"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: To the authors' knowledge, concept drift detection under upper-bounded label delay has not been studied yet. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "concept drift detection under _upper-bounded label delay_ has not been studied"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Evolving data stream classification offers state-of-the-art methods such as adaptive random forest and streaming random patches, focused on incrementally updating models rather than retraining from scratch for computational efficiency. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "State-of-the-art methods include _adaptive random forest_ [27] and _streaming random patches_ [28]."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: There is limited insight into how fraudsters respond, adapt attacks and cause drift; fraudsters control feature vectors only indirectly through platform interactions, and methods to identify unobserved but possible attacks are lacking. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they instead control it only _indirectly_ through their interactions with the e-commerce platform"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: One possible direction is attack trees, a cybersecurity method for mapping possible attack angles. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "One possible direction is the use of _attack trees_ [51]"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Supervised methods often outperform purely unsupervised anomaly detection for fraud detection in industry applications, and supervised models in particular outperform anomaly detection in detecting continuations of fraud attacks that were ongoing when the model was trained. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervised models, in particular, outperform anomaly detection models in detecting fraud instances that are continuations of fraud attacks"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The paper states that e-commerce organisations do have the computational resources required to retrain fraud models daily, in contrast to the evolving stream classification field's focus on updating models rather than retraining them. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In practice, however, e-commerce organizations do have the computational resources that are required to retrain models daily."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Adversarial robustness is an ML research area building models that make it hard for attackers to create adversarial examples, i.e., points the model predicts wrongly. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "focuses on building ML models that make it hard for attackers to create"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Adversarial robustness work focuses on computer vision and NLP, while most fraud systems use tabular data, so robustness methods for tabular data are an open challenge. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Adversarial robustness methods for tabular data are an open research challenge"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Anomaly detection separates normal data points from outliers and strongly links to density estimation, being in a sense its inverse. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The task of anomaly detection strongly links to _density estimation_"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Novelty detection concerns detecting novel behaviour emerging after drift, typically using anomaly detection: what is normal before drift is likely an outlier after it. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Novelty detection typically uses anomaly detection– what is normal w.r.t. pre-drift data"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Several empirical benchmark studies have compared anomaly detection methods and often found isolation forest performs consistently well. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "often identifying _isolation forest_ [48] performs well consistently"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Not every outlier is fraudulent: genuine-user behaviour drift from platform changes or events like COVID-19 also produces unusual feature values, and not every distribution shift stems from changed fraud attacks. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Therefore, not every outlier can be assumed to be fraudulent"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Flagging all outliers for investigation creates spikes of false positives, which would be expected with every release of a platform change. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Marking all outliers as possible fraud cases that require investigation"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: The authors call for investigation into methods that account for 'harmless' outliers caused by external changes. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This calls for investigation into methods that account for the existence of"</sub>

## Challenge 11: Separating platform changes from changes in attack patterns

- **descriptive / expectation**: Concept drift detection methods that alert on fraudster-driven drift but not on platform-driven drift would be of practical value. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Concept drift detection methods that alert in the latter case but not in the former"</sub>

## Challenge 12: Accounting for platform changes in novelty detection

- **descriptive / limitation**: Platform changes can cause large spikes in outliers flagged by novelty detection, limiting practical use; methods are needed to detect novel fraud types but not platform-driven outliers. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Changes in the e-commerce platform can cause large spikes in the number of outliers"</sub>

## Challenge 13: Mapping attack angles

- **descriptive / expectation**: There is a need for methods and frameworks to map possible attack angles in an e-commerce platform and for decision frameworks to use them in work prioritisation. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There is a need for methods and frameworks to map possible attack-angles"</sub>

## Challenge 14: Methods for adversarial robustness for tabular data

- **descriptive / limitation**: Many fraud systems work on tabular data, an understudied modality in adversarial robustness research. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "which is an understudied data modality in the research field of adversarial robustness"</sub>

## Challenge 15: Balancing anomaly detection and supervised methods

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Supervised methods are more accurate for recurring fraud types while anomaly detection can detect new attacks; the open question is whether a combination strategy can be automatically inferred. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "While _supervised methods_ are more accurate in detecting recurring fraud types"</sub>

## Challenge 16: Concept drift adaptation in the delayed label setting

- **descriptive / expectation**: Supervised concept drift adaptation often assumes immediate labels; open questions include adapting under delayed labels and whether a theoretical label-delay upper bound can be exploited. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervised methods_ for _concept drift adaptation_ often assume that labels are immediately available"</sub>

## 7 ML-investigator interaction

- **descriptive / context**: Fraud investigations stop fraudulent behaviour through manual action and generate new labels for the ML model; two objectives, identifying fraud and generating useful labels, can compete. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Two objectives are involved here: the goal to _identify fraud_"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Active learning prioritises unlabeled data points for investigator labelling according to expected model improvement, followed by retraining and a new prioritisation round; it can help mitigate label delay. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The AL paradigm decides which unlabeled data points to prioritize for labeling"</sub>

## 7.1 Summary of the Literature

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Active learning has not been widely applied in industry despite extensive academic research; possible reasons include uncertainty over techniques, extreme class imbalance, engineering overhead and assumption validity. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AL has not been widely applied in industry context so far despite extensive academic research"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Carcillo et al. showed on credit card fraud data that selecting the instances with the highest fraud probability maximises learning and obtained high precision under high class imbalance. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "simply selecting the instances with the highest probability of being fraudulent maximizes learning"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Uncertainty sampling selects data points close to the model's decision boundary. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The popular uncertainty sampling [74] method explores those data points with high proximity"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: More recent active learning work distinguishes epistemic from aleatoric uncertainty, since investigation time is wasted where uncertainty cannot be reduced. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The rationale is that the fraud investigator's investigation time is wasted time"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Interrater disagreement among investigators can cause aleatoric uncertainty, and traditional AL methods that ignore the distinction tend to repeatedly select high-aleatoric instances. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Traditional AL methods that do not distinguish between the two uncertainty types"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: Fraud detection AL systems would benefit from sampling by epistemic uncertainty, since new fraud patterns likely come from high-epistemic regions; this research area lacks real-life fraud evaluations. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Therefore, fraud detection AL systems would benefit from focusing on sampling instances"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Guided learning asks investigators to search for fraudulent examples themselves rather than label model-selected instances; it requires sufficient domain knowledge, which is typical in fraud. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Guided learning [5] contrasts by asking fraud investigators to search themselves"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Guided learning may be particularly useful when prevalence is very low and classes are disjunct, such as different fraud modus operandi; its cost per label is likely higher than AL. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Guided learning can be particularly useful when prevalence is very low"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Investigator searches induce investigator-specific selection bias whose impact has not been studied to the authors' knowledge. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "relying on investigator searches induces selection bias that is unique to each investigator"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Guided learning can be supported by empowering investigators to generate queries based on model input features, a potential but less-studied research area. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Practically, guided learning can be supported by empowering investigators to generate queries"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Guided learning and AL can complement each other; hybrid variants have shown some success, and guided learning is particularly successful versus AL at low prevalence. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Guided learning and AL can complement each other"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Weak supervision (e.g. snorkel) infers labels using labeling functions created by fraud investigators, which are imperfect and analogous to rules, and uses a generative model to infer reliable labels as ground truth. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Weak supervision techniques such as _snorkel_ [65] solve the problem of inferring labels"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Existing weak supervision user interfaces focus on textual data, whereas many fraud problems use tabular data needing deeper investigation, so further UI research is required. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In existing literature, like [65], the focus of this user interface is on textual data"</sub>

## 7.2 Open Research Challenges

- **descriptive / expectation**: Challenge 17: the exploration/exploitation trade-off in active learning, balancing labelling useful for the model against finding more fraud, is an open research challenge. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is important to trade-off this goal with finding more fraud cases"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: Challenge 18: avoid wasting resources on high-aleatoric regions; epistemic uncertainty sampling is promising but lacks practical evaluation in adversarial fraud settings. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Epistemic uncertainty sampling is a promising direction of research"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Challenge 19: the relative value of labelling versus searching depends on investigation costs and fraud prevalence; searching is most likely more expensive, but the conditions are unclear. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In many situations, searching is most likely more expensive than labeling"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: Challenge 20: weak supervision depends on quickly and accurately assessing observed data classes, which is hard; better decision support tools for investigators are needed. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Therefore, better decision support tools for fraud investigators are needed"</sub>

## 2.1 Shortcomings of Popular Tabular Datasets

- **descriptive / context**: The authors know of no public large-scale bank account opening fraud dataset; two relevant general banking fraud datasets exist, both on transaction fraud. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "To the best of our knowledge, there are no public large-scale bank account opening fraud datasets."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The first transaction dataset (about 300k European credit card transactions from September 2013) uses principal components with generic names, making fairness study impossible. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Its fundamental drawback is that the features of the dataset are principal components of the original features"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The second dataset is a suite of 5 synthetic mobile transaction fraud datasets with informative feature names but no sensitive attributes, limiting fairness use. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the data contains no sensitive attributes, limiting its use in the context of algorithmic fairness"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Bank account opening is an important use case for Fair ML because access to it is essential and gate-keeping can seriously hinder individual well-being. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "opening a bank account is essential in today’s society, and gate-keeping such a service can seriously hinder the well-being of an individual"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The UCI Adult dataset, the most popular fairness benchmark, has been criticized for its poorly documented sampling variable fnlwgt. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a) the sampling strategy, based on the poorly documented variable"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The UCI Adult income task (above 50,000 dollars) is arbitrary and not connected to any real census task. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the arbitrary choice of task — predicting individuals whose income is above 50,000 dollars — which is not connected to any real census task"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The UCI Adult data is old, based on 1994 US census data. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the age of the data itself (it is based on 1994 US census data)."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The COMPAS risk assessment dataset, the second most popular fairness benchmark, has measurement biases, missing values, label leakage and sampling incongruities. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This dataset is afflicted with measurement biases [13], missing values, label leakage [11] and sampling incongruities [31]."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: In criminal justice, measuring fairness solely on ML predictions gives an unrealistic picture because several agents influence decisions. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "measuring fairness in this domain solely based on ML model predictions often provides an unrealistic picture"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: COMPAS raises privacy concerns because accused individuals can be identified from criminal records and other PII. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is possible to identify accused individuals based on criminal record and other Personal Identifiable Information (PII)"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The German Credit dataset, third most popular, has documentation issues; the applicant's sex cannot be retrieved from the 'Personal status and sex' attribute because values overlap. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Here, the sex of the individual is not retrievable by the `“Personal status and sex”` attribute"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: A later release of the German Credit dataset confirmed that sex cannot be retrieved through that attribute, limiting its fairness utility. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "also confirms that retrieving the applicant’s sex through the aforementioned attribute is not possible [32]"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: German Credit applicants date from 1973 to 1975, which hinders generalization to today. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the dataset is composed of applicants from 1973 to 1975, which hinders the generalization of any insights to today’s world."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A study of Machine Learning Research identified a funneling tendency, with increasingly fewer datasets used for benchmarking, often repurposed for tasks other than intended. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a study on the datasets used in Machine Learning Research (MLR) identified a funneling tendency in the field"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: None of the 12 additional less-frequently used Fair ML datasets fulfil all desiderata, for example having small unrealistic samples, being too old, or lacking a specific task. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "while maintaining that none of them fulfill all the desiderata listed in Section 1"</sub>

## 2.2 Privacy-Preserving Approaches and Generative Models

- **mandatory / requirement**: To avoid privacy-breaching use of published datasets, information leading to identification of an individual must be removed, transformed or obfuscated. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is required to either remove, transform, or obfuscate any information that leads to the identification of a particular individual."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Differential privacy measures the maximum difference in a measurement or transformation induced by any individual instance; lower values mean higher privacy. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This metric determines the maximum difference in an arbitrary measurement or transformation applied to a dataset induced by any individual instance."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Upper-bound differential privacy levels are met by several generative models, but default generative implementations ignore common tabular problems such as categorical and non-normal continuous variables. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "However, the default implementations of generative models do not take into consideration common problems faced in the tabular data domain."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: CTGAN handles tabular problems but lacks differential privacy guarantees by default, a gap relative to image-domain models. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This architecture, however, does not have differential privacy guarantees by default"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: DPCTGAN and PATECTGAN, differentially private CTGAN extensions, have shown mixed empirical results in generated data utility. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "with mixed empirical results in terms of the generated data’s utility [40]"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The authors add noise to the original data before GAN training so the GAN never accesses specific applicant data, judging the other methods insufficiently informative. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "We take this approach for our suite of datasets, since the former methods would not output sufficiently informative data to be useful in practice."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: There is no consensus on evaluating generative models; image work uses distance measures such as Inception Score and FID, while tabular practice validates via train-on-generated models, feature distance statistics and paired correlations. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There is still no consensus on the evaluation of generative models [42]."</sub>

## 3.1 Original Dataset Overview

- **descriptive / context**: The original dataset concerns detection of fraudulent online bank account opening applications at a large consumer bank, where fraudsters use identity theft or fictional identities and max out credit lines, with all costs borne by the bank. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "All costs are sustained by the bank, as there’s no way of tracing the fraudster true identity."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A positive prediction (flagged fraud) rejects the application, a punitive action, while a negative prediction grants account and credit card access, an assistive action. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A positive prediction ( _i.e._ , flagged as fraudulent) leads to a rejection of the customer’s bank account application (a punitive action)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Holding a bank account is a basic right in the European Union, making fraud detection pertinent from a societal perspective. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "holding a bank account is a basic right in the European Union [27]"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A drop of a few percentage points in predictive performance can represent millions in fraud losses, making Fair ML requirements particularly stringent. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a potential drop of a few percentage points in predictive performance often represents millions in fraud losses"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Each instance is an individual application made on an online platform with explicit consent to store and process data; the label is in the is_fraud column. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The label of each instance is stored in the `"is_fraud"` column."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The original dataset covers eight months, February to September, with fraud prevalence between 0.85% and 1.5% across months, higher in later months. (p. 5)[^original]

## 3.3 Bias Patterns

- **descriptive / definition**: Bias patterns follow a taxonomy from prior work: the suite's six variants each contain predetermined, controllable bias patterns to stress test predictive performance and fairness. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the suite contains six datasets (variants of the base dataset) each one with pre-determined and controllable bias patterns"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Group size disparity exists when group frequencies differ across the groups of a protected attribute, which is observed in the original dataset with more applications from the younger age group. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This pattern is observed in the original dataset, with a higher proportion of applications being made by the younger age group."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Prevalence disparity occurs when the class probability depends on the protected group; the proposed datasets show higher fraud rates for older age groups, like the original. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Similarly to the original dataset, the proposed dataset shows higher fraud rates for older age groups."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A proposed reason for higher fraud among older groups is that fraudsters target older people because banks give them larger credit lines to max out before detection. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "banks provide older applicants with larger lines of credit once an account is opened, which fraudsters try to max out before being caught."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Separability disparity occurs when the joint distribution of features and label differs across groups; the ATM illumination example shows a feature helping detect fraud only for one age group. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Separability disparity extends the previous definition by including the joint distribution of input features _X_ and label _Y_"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The first two disparities are induced by controlling generative sampling by group and label; the third is induced by appending two columns with multivariate normal distributions whose means depend on group and label, with controllable linear separability. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the third disparity is obtained through appending two columns with different multivariate normal distributions"</sub>

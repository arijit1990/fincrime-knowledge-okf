## Case Study 44 – Italy

## Case Study 45 – Italy

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 45 (Italy): an AML compliance inspection of a TCSP led to the investigation, which concerned acquiring a well-known Italian transport company through a trustee mandated in the name of a foreign company with no specified ownership. Documents showed several trustee registration files indicating offenses by the legal representative. The TCSP screened the transfer to Italy of illegally generated funds concealed abroad, and investigating the foreign company's beneficial ownership linked investigated persons to considerable assets fraudulently moved abroad and used to buy the transport company. (p. 136)[^original]

## Case Study 46 – Italy

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 46 (Italy): a trust structure for the son of Mr. X, client of a UK law firm, held funds illegally diverted from an Italian company Mr. X ran. A BVI company was owned by an Irish company and owned 100% of a Luxembourg company, which received money from the Italian company for fictitious sales. The Irish company's director and the BVI company's director were each partners of the same UK law firm; a close associate of Mr. X held power of attorney in the BVI company; the Irish company's shares were held in trust for Mr. X's son (beneficial owner of the trust) by a Jersey TCSP connected to the UK firm. (p. 136)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A trust structure was setup for the son of Mr. X, a client of a UK law firm"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In Case Study 46 the scheme left no apparent link between the funds diverted from the Italian company and their beneficial owner; the only link was the trust. (p. 136)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there was no apparent link between the funds diverted from the Italian company"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Case Study 46 (Italy) indicators: entity formed in a low-tax jurisdiction or international trade/financial centre; employees of professional intermediary firms acting as nominee directors or shareholders; complex structures lacking commercial sense; nominee owners and directors. (p. 136)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Employees of professional intermediary firms acting as nominee directors or shareholders"</sub>

The operations ran through foreign corporate vehicles managed by a Swiss trust fiduciary. The suspects were charged with conspiracy, money laundering, corruption and embezzlement. Bank records showed he owned 100% of a Cyprus-based company, and the tax register confirmed his date and place of birth and Italian tax residency. The Italian Official Register showed that through the Cypriot company he owned 50% of a Rome limited liability company whose corporate purpose is buying and constructing buildings it owns. (p. 137)[^original]

He held shares or stakes in several companies in Russia and Cyprus, including two banks and the Cypriot company; he, the Cypriot company and the Roman company owned several properties in various Italian provinces. His assets and resources, in his own name or through corporate vehicles, had been under freezing orders since 2014. (p. 138)[^original]

## Case Study 49 – Jersey

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 49 (Jersey): the main fraud centred on a software business in the suspect's home country that sold its intellectual property rights to an Irish company, which transferred them to a BVI company; the business then took license and distribution agreements with the BVI company and carried on as before. The resulting fees paid to the BVI company significantly cut its taxable income. All 3 entities were owned and controlled by the same person ('X'), who allegedly claimed deductions from 'sham' license and distribution arrangements. X set up a trust structure with underlying companies using a Jersey financial service provider; these entities allegedly served as conduits for fund transfers or held assets from the scheme. (p. 138)[^original]

(p. 139)[^original]

## Case Study 50 – Latvia

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 50 (Latvia): foreign national Mrs V opened an account in Latvian bank B and shortly after received USD 3 827 000 and EUR 208 000 from foreign Company M, which had got the funds from foreign Companies R and W. Public information showed M and W had the same shareholder, an offshore legal entity, while the beneficiaries presented to the bank were two other individuals, raising concern about obscuring beneficial ownership. Mrs V sent USD 2 980 000 to Individuals E, O and A at foreign Bank F, citing a gift to grandchildren, and USD 840 000 to her own account at Bank F. (p. 139)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "raised concerns of a scheme to obscure beneficial ownership"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 50 (Latvia), continued: all beneficiaries had the same address, suggesting Mrs V lived in a different country from that on bank CDD records. USD 220 000 was later received from Individual L and transfers of USD 300 000 initiated to A and E. In response to Bank B's EDD request, documents sent electronically said she sold two paintings to Individual B for USD 220 000 via L, but the signatures looked digitally embedded. Individual A came to the bank claiming to be her grandchild and said she was deceased but could not provide a death certificate. (p. 139)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "but the signatures on the agreement appeared digitally embedded"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Case Study 50 (Latvia) outcome: the FIU confirmed with Mrs V's country of residence that she was deceased and that transactions since her death were made by third parties, and issued an order freezing USD 350 000 in her accounts. (p. 139)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FIU issued an order to freeze USD 350 000 in Mrs V’s accounts."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Case Study 50 (Latvia) indicators: client reluctant or unable to explain why activities are conducted in a certain manner; foreign nationals with no significant dealings in the country where they obtain services; transactions that appear strange given an individual's age; director, controlling shareholder(s) and/or beneficial owner(s) listed against accounts of other legal persons or arrangements; falsified paper trail; questionable connections between parties the client cannot sufficiently explain. (p. 139)[^original]

## Case Study 52 – Mexico

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 52 (Mexico): four shell companies asked the Mexican Tax Administration Service (SAT) for Value Added Tax refunds on non-existent operations in 2008 and 2009. In total 26 companies took part in simulating transactions and 48 individuals were partners, administrators and legal representatives. Part of the illegal proceeds went to US bank accounts and then to accounts in Las Vegas, Nevada, held by casinos and by individuals who gambled. (p. 140)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Four shell companies requested from the Mexican Tax Administration Service (SAT) the refund of the Value Added Tax"</sub>

(p. 141)[^original]

## Case Study 53 – Namibia

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 53 (Namibia): Namibian national A, as sole owner, registered two close corporations with false national identity documents and opened accounts for each at two local banks; those at one bank were active, those at the other stayed dormant and were closed. A authorised foreigners B and C to manage the accounts, and they used online banking for huge inward and outward transfers. Funds moved from foreign jurisdiction SA to Namibia and were immediately re-routed to other foreign jurisdictions, including back to SA; transfers started small and grew larger, and funds were generally withdrawn in under 48 hours after deposit. (p. 141)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "registered two close corporations using false national identity documents"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Case Study 53 (Namibia) indicators: signatory to company accounts without sufficient explanation; declared income inconsistent with assets, transactions or lifestyle; registered at an address that does not match the company profile; large number of transactions with a small number of recipients; transacts with international companies without sufficient corporate or trade justification; bank balance close to zero despite frequent incoming and outgoing transactions; activities inconsistent with corporate profile; multiple bank accounts without good reason; falsified records or counterfeited documentation; fabricated corporate ownership records; falsified paper trail; no real business activities; exclusively facilitates transit transactions; repeat transaction between parties over a contracted period; transaction appears cyclical; complicated routings without sufficient explanation or trade records. (p. 141)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Signatory to company accounts without sufficient explanation"</sub>

## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 1: criminals use a range of techniques and mechanisms to obscure ownership and control of illicit assets, and identifying true beneficial owner(s) or controlling individual(s) is a significant challenge for prosecutors, law enforcement and intelligence practitioners worldwide. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Identifying the true beneficial owner(s) or individual(s) exercising control represents a significant challenge"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Paragraph 1: schemes to obscure beneficial ownership often use a "hide-in-plain sight" strategy, leveraging global trade and commerce infrastructures to appear legitimate; visibility does not equate to transparency, and tools designed to encourage business growth, such as limited liability corporations and nominee directorship services, can be used to facilitate money laundering, tax evasion and corruption. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "visibility does not equate to transparency"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 1: globalisation of trade and communications has increased this threat, and countries now face the challenge of enforcing national laws in a borderless commercial environment. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries now face the challenge of enforcing national laws in a borderless commercial environment"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 2: the joint FATF-Egmont report takes a global view of how legal persons, legal arrangements and professional intermediaries can help criminals conceal wealth and illicit assets; its purpose is to help national authorities including FIUs, financial institutions and other professional service providers understand the risks they face. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The purpose of the report is to help national authorities including FIUs, financial institutions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 3: analysis of 106 case studies shows that legal persons, principally shell companies, are a key feature of schemes to disguise beneficial ownership, while front companies and bearer shares are less frequently exploited. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Analysis of 106 case studies demonstrates that legal persons, principally shell companies"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Definitions in the report: a shell company is an incorporated company with no independent operations, significant assets, ongoing business activities or employees; a front company is a fully functioning company with the characteristics of a legitimate business that serves to disguise and obscure illicit financial activity; a shelf company is an incorporated company with inactive shareholders, directors and secretary, left dormant for a longer period even if a customer relationship has already been established. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Shell company – incorporated company with no independent operations, significant assets, ongoing business activities, or employees."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Executive summary paragraph 4 says that individuals and groups wanting to conceal ownership of assets are most likely to control them through a mix of direct and indirect control rather than strictly one or the other. In a majority of cases the beneficial owner combined layered and direct ownership chains with professional intermediaries and third parties exercising control on the owner's behalf. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In a majority of cases, the beneficial owner used a combination of layering and direct ownership chains"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 4 (continued): in a limited number of cases the beneficial owner exercised only indirect control, and rarely retained direct control through a complicated structure without an intermediary. Thus the beneficial owner often keeps some direct control but rarely without an intermediary or "straw man" (informal nominee shareholders and directors such as spouses, children, extended family and other personal or business associates). (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In a limited number of cases, the beneficial owner exercised only indirect control"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 5: nominee directors and shareholders, particularly informal nominees ("straw men"), are a key vulnerability identified in a large majority of the case studies. A nominee's role is often to protect or conceal the identity of a company's or asset's beneficial owner and controller, and nominees can help overcome jurisdictional controls on company ownership and circumvent directorship bans imposed by courts and authorities. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Nominee directors and shareholders, particularly informal nominees (or “straw men”), are a key vulnerability"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 5: appointment of nominees is lawful in most countries, but the ongoing merits of the practice are described as questionable given the significant ML/TF vulnerabilities associated with their use. (p. 8)[^original]

## Specialist and professional intermediaries

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 6: specialists and professional intermediaries are a key feature of concealment schemes, particularly where proceeds of crime are significant; the majority of the case studies involved professional intermediaries. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The use of specialists and professional intermediaries is a key feature of schemes designed to conceal beneficial ownership"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 6: although not always explicit, approximately half of all intermediaries involved were assessed as complicit, showing complicity is not necessary to facilitate such a scheme and professionals can be unwitting or negligent. This highlights the importance of effective regulation of designated non-financial businesses and professions and of increased awareness among professional service sectors; law enforcement experience in some jurisdictions nonetheless indicates intermediaries are more likely to be complicit than unwitting in money laundering cases. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "approximately half of all intermediaries involved were assessed as having been complicit in their involvement"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In the case sample, trust and company service providers (TCSPs) were the highest proportion of professional intermediaries involved in establishing legal persons, legal arrangements and bank accounts, and were significantly more likely to provide nominee, directorship and other company management services, to provide services to other professionals on behalf of third-party clients, and to serve clients based internationally. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "trust and company service providers (TCSPs) represented the highest proportion of professional intermediaries"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Despite their involvement, TCSPs appear less likely to be the architect of concealment schemes; those assessed as complicit were more likely wilfully blind than fully complicit, or may also have provided legal, accounting or other financial services. This suggests the TCSP role is more likely transactional, at the behest of a client or other intermediary often based in another country, and that TCSP services are vulnerable to exploitation by criminals and other professional intermediaries. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "TCSPs appear less likely to be the architect of schemes designed to obscure beneficial ownership"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Accounting professionals were the least represented sector in the cases but significantly more likely to be complicit than legal professionals and TCSPs. They showed the least direct involvement in establishing legal persons, arrangements or banking relationships, suggesting their key role is expert advice. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Accounting professionals were the least represented sector in the cases analysed for this report"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Accounting professionals were the highest proportion of scheme designers and promoters in the cases and were more likely to promote their own scheme to prospective clients than to facilitate a client-designed scheme; they were the only professional sector not identified as providing services to another professional intermediary on behalf of a third-party client. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Accounting professionals represented the highest proportion of scheme designers and promoters in the case studies"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report considers it likely that accountants' financial acumen and ease in identifying suspicious financial activity limit their vulnerability to unwitting exploitation, and that criminals and complicit professionals may be unwilling to involve an accountant unless complicity can be assured in advance. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "limit their vulnerability to being unwittingly exploited to facilitate the concealment of beneficial ownership"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Legal professionals' role varies by situation: compared with accountants they were more involved in establishing legal persons, arrangements and bank accounts, but less so than TCSPs, and the same held for nominee and directorship services. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Legal professionals were more involved in the establishment of legal"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Lawyers were the most likely of the three professions to be involved in acquiring real estate as a means of laundering proceeds and obscuring beneficial ownership. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Lawyers were the most likely of the three professions to be involved in"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Legal trust accounts and client accounts were more frequently used to disguise beneficial ownership, although accountants showed a similar proportion of this technique; legal professional privilege was also identified as a barrier to recovering beneficial ownership information. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Legal professional privilege was also identified as a barrier to the successful recovery of beneficial ownership information."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In several cases legal professionals appeared unwitting or negligent, suggesting that despite their fairly high involvement in establishing legal persons and arrangements they are not sufficiently aware of their inherent ML/TF vulnerabilities, likely exacerbated by the low level of regulation of legal professionals in many countries. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "legal professionals are not sufficiently aware of their inherent money laundering and terrorism financing vulnerabilities"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 7 (truncated at the excerpt's end): analysis indicates that the services of both lawyers and accountants are rarely required to facilitate the same money laundering scheme, since the involvement of one is typically sufficient; the next sentence, about TCSPs being present in almost all cases, is cut off. (p. 9)[^original]

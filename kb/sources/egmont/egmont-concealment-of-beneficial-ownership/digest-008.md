## Continuum of complicity

- **descriptive / limitation**: Intelligence gap factor: limited AML/CFT obligations on DNFBPs in many countries owing to partial or non-compliance with Recommendations 22 and 23, and ineffective implementation of obligations in some countries. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "partial compliance or non-compliance with Recommendations 22 and 23"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Intelligence gap factor: reluctance by professional intermediaries to comply with AML/CFT obligations due to perceived conflicts with their duty to the client or obligations to protect client confidentiality and legal professional privilege. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "perceived conflicts with their duty to their client"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Intelligence gap factor: professional intermediaries are often not primary targets of law enforcement investigations and details of their activities are not universally recorded on law enforcement indices. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "professional intermediaries are often not the primary targets of law enforcement investigations"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: It is not possible to accurately quantify with certainty professional facilitators' involvement or complicity; the report draws conclusions from case studies provided by 34 participating countries. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is not possible to accurately quantify the degree to which they are involved"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Approximately one-third of all cases were assessed as involving a complicit professional intermediary; in most of those, the intermediary designed the scheme and promoted it to clients (predominantly as an effective tax minimisation method) and was often the subject of the primary investigation. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Approximately one-third of all cases were assessed as involving a complicit professional intermediary."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Of the three sectors, accountants were most likely to be complicit; legal and accounting professionals were more likely to be scheme designers than complicit participants in others' schemes; legal professionals were more likely than accountants to be unwitting or wilfully blind. Reasons suggested: accountants' financial acumen and the nature of cases (often tax evasion and fraud orchestrated by corrupt professionals). (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the accounting profession was most likely to be complicit in their involvement in schemes"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: An innocent, unwitting or negligent intermediary can be as valuable to a laundering scheme as a complicit one if their services give the criminal a desirable outcome, particularly because services like establishing legal persons are commonplace; law enforcement in some jurisdictions observed more ML investigations involving complicit than unwitting intermediaries. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An innocent, unwitting or negligent intermediary can be as valuable as a complicit intermediary"</sub>

## OVERVIEW OF COMMONLY EXPLOITED INTERMEDIARIES

- **descriptive / context**: The section gives an overview of the legal, accounting and TCSP sectors to contextualise sectors commonly exploited to establish complex ownership structures and conceal beneficial ownership information. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "contextualise the sectors commonly exploited by criminals to establish complex ownership structures"</sub>

## Legal Professionals

- **descriptive / context**: The legal sector is principally small business enterprises (sole practitioners or partnerships with minimal non-partner staff in most countries), unlike banking, which is dominated by a few large banks; most firms specialise in one segment but may still do other work, e.g. personal and family law firms may also set up companies. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the legal sector is principally characterised by small business enterprises"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The legal sector has historically had low globalisation, with most firms serving local clients, but information technology and a growing transnational market have led large firms to expand and merge or affiliate with international firms. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The legal sector has historically demonstrated a low level of industry globalisation"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Legal professionals in most countries must belong to a professional body (law society or bar association) imposing strict rules and codes of conduct, often acting as self-regulating bodies where lawyers face AML/CFT oversight; these rules supplement legislation and breaches can bring severe financial or professional sanctions. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "required to maintain a membership with a professional body, such as a law society or bar association"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The notarial sector differs from the legal sector, particularly in civil law countries: some notaries do not represent contract parties, many lack long-term client relationships and must be impartial and independent, and many carry public office duties, which influence what a notary must do to assess ML risk. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The notarial sector differs from the legal sector in many countries, particularly civil law countries."</sub>

## Role in the Establishment and Management of Legal Persons and Arrangements

- **descriptive / context**: Legal representation is commonly sought to establish companies and other legal persons and arrangements, and where not strictly required legal expertise is often engaged as a precaution, particularly where a foreign jurisdiction is involved. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Legal representation is commonly sought in most countries to facilitate the establishment of companies"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Multinational law firms help establish legal persons in one country for clients in another and can support legitimate transnational structures, but their cross-border structuring expertise can also be used to conceal the beneficial ownership of illicit assets. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "their expertise in setting up cross-border structures to be utilised to conceal the beneficial ownership"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Without an international presence, law firms use professional associations and global alliance networks; formal alliances often operate under a code of conduct that does not necessarily include a compulsory AML/CFT compliance program, and not every member firm is subject to AML/CFT regulation. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this does not necessarily include a compulsory AML/CFT compliance program"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: One-third of analysed cases specifically referred to legal professionals (including notaries): 25 cases referred to lawyers, five to notaries and four to both. Some TCSP cases likely involved lawyers or legally qualified TCSPs; use of 'TCSP' as a catch-all term was identified as a possible reporting issue. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The use of the term TCSP as a catch-all term for professionals involved in company establishment"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Where assessable, the majority of legal professionals worked on behalf of a direct client; a small number provided services to another professional intermediary on behalf of a third-party client. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the majority were found to have been working on behalf of a direct client"</sub>

## Accountants

- **descriptive / context**: The accounting sector is large but more focused than legal, with audit, tax and advisory services the vast majority of business; it has moderate globalisation through large multinational firms (increasing via acquisitions) but is still characterised by small enterprises and sole proprietors. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "audit, tax, and advisory services representing the vast majority of business"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Most accounting enterprises (including sole proprietors and those with fewer than 20 people) serve individuals or small businesses, while large multinationals serve large companies and public sector authorities. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "employing fewer than 20 people, typically service individuals or small businesses"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Accountants in accredited bodies are governed by a code of ethics, but in many countries are not required to belong to any independent oversight body (e.g. Mutual Evaluation Reports of Andorra, Bahamas, Bhutan, Denmark, Ireland, Mexico and Slovenia); with many sole proprietors, it is hard to monitor the sector's AML/CFT awareness and adherence, and FIUs and supervisors face challenges supervising it. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "accounting professionals in many countries are not required to maintain a membership to any independent oversight body51"</sub>

## Role in the Establishment and Management of Legal Persons and Arrangements

- **descriptive / context**: Accountants primarily give expert advice on business structures, asset management and domestic and international taxation, and in many countries are the first professional consulted by small businesses and individuals; where a matter is outside their competence they refer on or procure another professional. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the provision of expert advice on business structures, asset management, and taxation obligations"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In most countries accountants may establish companies for clients, but most firms only serve established businesses or advise on proposed structures; those that offer company establishment are likely to keep a significant financial management role including account signatory. In the cases, only one accountant established legal persons in their own country and three did so in a foreign jurisdiction. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "only one was involved in the establishment of legal persons or arrangements in their own country of operation"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Accounting firms operating across jurisdictions leverage their global presence, but few have a global footprint, so smaller firms rely on professional associations and alliance networks or act as intermediaries with overseas accountants, lawyers and TCSPs; the majority of accountants in the cases facilitated international activities for clients. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "smaller firms often rely on professional associations and alliance networks to service transnational clients"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Accountants are rarely relied on to establish trusts but advise on them and may act as settlor, trustee or protector; unlike the legal sector, the accounting sector places few restrictions on accountants holding these positions, though only one accountant in the cases offered directorship/trustee services. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the accounting sector places few restrictions on accountants maintaining these positions in a legal arrangement"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Accountants were the least represented sector in the cases (possibly because some were recorded as TCSPs or only the TCSP was recorded); where identified, almost half involved accountants plus professionals from another sector, and a small number involved multiple accountants in one scheme. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The accounting profession was the least represented sector in the cases analysed for this report."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: All accountants identified worked for a direct client, indicating they are less likely to be approached by other intermediaries to carry out a scheme concealing beneficial ownership. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "All accounting professionals identified in the case studies were assessed as working on behalf of a direct client."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Accountants' expertise lets most identify suspicious and high-risk client activity, making them less susceptible to unwitting exploitation than lawyers and TCSPs; a significant majority of accountants in the cases were complicit and over half designed and promoted the scheme to minimise clients' taxation. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a significant majority of accounting professionals were complicit in their involvement"</sub>

## Trust and Company Service Providers

- **descriptive / context**: The TCSP sector (excluding legal and accounting professionals offering company formation) is difficult to describe or quantify and varies across jurisdictions: robust and regulated in some (registration, professional body oversight, AML/CFT regulation), less clearly defined with weaker oversight in others; services come from finance, legal and accounting sectors and stand-alone providers. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the TCSP sector (excluding legal and accounting professionals who provide company formation"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The FATF standards define trust and company service providers as any provider carrying out transactions for a client concerning: acting as formation agent of legal persons; acting as (or arranging for another to act as) director or secretary of a company, partner of a partnership or similar position; providing a registered office, business address, accommodation, correspondence or administrative address for a company, partnership or other legal person or arrangement; acting as (or arranging for another to act as) trustee of an express trust or equivalent function; and acting as (or arranging for another to act as) a nominee shareholder. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "acting as (or arranging for another person to act as) a nominee shareholder for another person."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Much literature on TCSPs covers all providers of those services regardless of whether they represent their core (excerpt ends mid-sentence). (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "regardless of whether they represent their core"</sub>

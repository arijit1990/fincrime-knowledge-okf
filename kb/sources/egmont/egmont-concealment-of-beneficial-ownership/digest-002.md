## Anti-money laundering obligations and supervision

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Seventeen per cent of jurisdictions in the FATF Horizontal Study of supervision and enforcement of beneficial ownership obligations impose no AML/CFT obligations or supervision on any DNFBPs, although the report notes this is a requirement of the FATF Standards. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Seventeen per cent of jurisdictions that participated in the FATF’s Horizontal Study"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report says the absence of DNFBP regulation may stem from resistance from the sectors or professions, or from an unfinished aspect of the AML/CFT system; it calls the lack of supervision a major vulnerability and says professionals in such countries are an unregulated 'back-door' into the global financial system. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "represent an unregulated “back-door” into the global financial system"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A country with a weak AML/CFT regime exacerbates the vulnerabilities of legal persons, legal arrangements and professional intermediaries; Immediate Outcomes 4 and 5 and Recommendations 10, 11, 12, 22, 23, 24, 25 and 28, amongst others, relate to the risk profile of these in a jurisdiction. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Immediate Outcomes 4 and 5, and Recommendations 10, 11, 12, 22, 23, 24, 25 and 28"</sub>

## INTRODUCTION

- **descriptive / context**: Over three decades, convergence of international trade and global financial systems and the rise of the internet have opened new opportunities for criminals to misuse company and business structures; criminals disguise activity as legitimate corporate trade, and visibility does not equate to transparency. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "visibility does not equate to transparency"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Criminals use shell companies, trusts, other legal arrangements, nominees and professional intermediaries to conceal the true intent of their activities and the associated beneficial ownership. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "shell companies1, trusts, other legal arrangements, nominees, and professional intermediaries"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Leaks of confidential information from two large international law firms in 2015 and 2017 (Mossack Fonseca, Panama, 2015; Appleby, Bermuda, 2017) raised public awareness of how legal structures conceal wealth and illicit assets. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The leak of confidential information from two large international law firms"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: For the report, 'shell companies' are companies that are incorporated but have no independent operations, significant assets, ongoing business activities or employees. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "no independent operations, significant assets, ongoing business activities, or employees"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Misuse of legal persons and arrangements has been studied by the OECD, World Bank, UNODC and FATF; FATF and the Egmont Group identified need for further analysis of beneficial ownership vulnerabilities, with particular focus on professional intermediaries, which this report attempts to address. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identified the need for further analysis of the vulnerabilities associated with beneficial ownership"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: In the report, 'legal arrangements' means express trusts or other similar legal arrangements. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Legal arrangements – refers to express trusts or other similar legal arrangements."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: In the report, 'legal persons' means any entities other than natural persons that are able to form a permanent customer relationship with a financial institution or otherwise own property. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Legal persons – refers to any entities other than natural persons that can establish a permanent customer relationship"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 2 defines 'beneficial ownership' or 'beneficial owner' as the natural person or persons who ultimately own or control a customer and/or the natural person on whose behalf a transaction is carried out, and says the term also covers those who exercise ultimate effective control over a legal person or arrangement. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "refers to the natural person(s) who ultimately owns or controls a customer and/or the natural person on whose"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 18 says the publication adopts a global, macro-level perspective on inherent vulnerabilities and is meant to support further risk analysis by governments, financial institutions and other professional service providers. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "takes a macro-level, global view of inherent vulnerabilities"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: In further risk analysis, countries and private sector professionals should consider how the geopolitical and economic environment and their own risk mitigation strategies affect vulnerabilities of legal structures and the intermediary sectors that form and manage them. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries and private sector professionals should consider how the geopolitical and economic environment"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The project was co-sponsored by the FATF and Egmont Group, led by Australia, Germany and France, with experts from multiple countries, FATF-style regional bodies, the IMF, OECD Global Forum, World Bank and FATF TREIN. (p. 15)[^original]

## METHODOLOGY

- **descriptive / method**: The project team analysed typologies studies, intelligence assessments, mutual evaluation reports and academic reports (public sources listed in Annex A), plus non-public intelligence reports from FIUs, criminal intelligence and law enforcement agencies and other competent authorities; only desensitised information is used. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the project team analysed typologies studies, intelligence assessments, mutual evaluation reports"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: An intelligence exchange workshop at the FATF Joint Experts Meeting in Moscow in April 2017 had 13 delegations (Indonesia, Italy, Israel, Kyrgyz Republic, the Netherlands, Poland, Russia, Spain, Sudan, Switzerland, Venezuela, Europol and the European Commission) present case studies; a session with private sector representatives from banks, law societies and TCSPs was also held. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "during which 13 delegations4 presented case studies and intelligence insights"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The team sought comments from 12 international organisations and associations representing global financial institutions, DNFBPs, data providers, FinTech and RegTech firms and NGOs; comments came from the Financial Transparency Coalition, Institute of International Finance, International Banking Federation, International Federation of Accountants, International RegTech Association, International Union of Notaries, Society of Trust and Estate Practitioners, the Wolfsberg Group and the International Bar Association's AML and Sanctions Expert Working Group. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the project team sought comments from 12 international organisations and associations"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The private sector comments included additional information on vulnerabilities, additional risk indicators and methods for identifying beneficial ownership. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "additional information on vulnerabilities, additional risk indicators, and methods for identifying beneficial ownership"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The primary sources were case studies from FATF, the Egmont Group and FSRB members; FATF TREIN analysed 106 case studies and typologies submitted by 34 jurisdictions, a relatively small sample weighted toward a few jurisdictions (the Netherlands submitted 19 cases, Egypt eight, Australia and the United States seven each). (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FATF TREIN undertook an analysis of the 106 case studies and typologies submitted by 34 jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The analysis was limited to information known to competent authorities and communicated in case summaries; in some cases the predicate offence or location of the ultimate beneficial owner was apparently unknown, and in others details such as legal person type were not communicated or the jurisdiction was anonymised. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FATF TREIN’s analysis was limited to the information known to the competent authorities"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Despite data limitations, case descriptions were substantially more detailed than those in recent Mutual Evaluation Reports; cases with identified dates were generally recent (2010 to 2017), and the average sum laundered per case exceeded USD 500 million. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "was in excess of USD 500 million"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The report focuses on vulnerabilities and misuse techniques for legal persons, legal arrangements and professional intermediaries; it does not cover threats by predicate offence, how predicate offences affect concealment methods, or consequences of residual risk, and gives no definitive list of high-risk jurisdictions because jurisdiction-specific variables make it untenable globally. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "No effort has been made to provide a definitive list of high-risk jurisdictions"</sub>

## Declaring Numerous Beneficiaries

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 38 (Israel): a scheme hid funds from social engineering fraud and other offences under an international trade cover story (European and US merchants paying East Asian suppliers); the suspect, owner of a registered MSB, ran a second unregistered MSB and used natural persons in East Asia to engage local TCSPs to set up international companies and bank accounts, with local straw-men and passport-based shareholders registered and addresses and accounts in East Asia. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The suspect, the owner of a registered MSB, operated a second, unregistered MSB."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 38 (continued): some funds went to an Israeli account opened by the suspect, with more than 60 beneficiaries declared to the bank so it could not tell which transaction was for which beneficiary; the receiving bank did not know the sending companies were under the suspect's control. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "More than 60 beneficiaries were declared to the bank as beneficiaries"</sub>

## Use of Professional Intermediaries in Forming and Managing Legal Persons and Arrangements

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Use of specialists and professional intermediaries (lawyers, accountants, TCSPs) is a key feature of the ML and organised crime environment; they significantly enhance criminals' capacity for sophisticated schemes and have been assessed as posing a high money laundering risk in most countries. Their vulnerabilities are detailed in Section 3. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "professional intermediaries have been assessed as posing a high money laundering risk in most countries"</sub>

## Falsifying activities

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Some techniques for hiding beneficial ownership are purely criminal, designed to falsify activities and commit crime by deception; false loans and invoices used to fraudulently disguise the beneficial ownership of a transaction are the most common, while manipulation of company prospectuses and annual reports has been identified infrequently. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "some techniques used to hide beneficial ownership are purely criminal"</sub>

## Use of False Loans and Invoices

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: False loans, often called 'loan-back' or 'round-robin' schemes, involve money sent to companies owned or controlled by or for the same individual and returned in the guise of a loan, generally in two steps. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "often referred to as a “loan- back” or “round-robin” scheme"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Step 1, payment of business invoices: the individual or business pays invoices to a company (often abroad) it controls, or to an associate or intermediary; funds may pass through numerous legal persons as seemingly legitimate transactions and pool in an international company acting for the beneficial owner, reducing taxable income by increasing apparent business expenses. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "will ultimately pool in the account of an international company that is operating in the interests of the beneficial owner"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Step 2, third-party loan: pooled funds are returned to the original company/individual, a close relative (commonly spouse or child) or associate as a private loan, occasionally with false loan documents but often recorded only in the bank transfer description, returning wealth to the beneficial owner in a manner exempt from income taxation. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "often the loan is recorded only in the description of the bank transfer"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Loan-back schemes can involve interest payments (a further means of channelling money into international accounts and reducing domestic tax, as in Case Study 7, Australia) but need not; there may be no real obligation to repay, and the scheme disguises that lender and borrower are beneficially owned by the same natural person. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there may be no actual obligation for the beneficial owner to repay the false loan"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Loan-back schemes are sometimes promoted and facilitated by professional service providers; the international company in the structure is controlled by the promoter, who takes a portion of the laundered funds as payment, further separating beneficial ownership and reducing detection likelihood (Case Study 6, an Australian accountant using Hong Kong and BVI companies). (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the international company used in the loan structure is controlled by the scheme promoter"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 6 (Australia): suspect A, an importer, joined a tax evasion scheme run by an accountant who controlled company 3 (registered in Hong Kong, with an Australian bank account); over five and a half years it issued false invoices for supposed 'brokering services' to suspect A's companies 1 and 2, which paid more than AUD 2 million, and the funds less the accountant's 10% fee were returned to suspect A and associates. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The funds paid to company 3, less the accountant's 10% fee, were returned to suspect A"</sub>

## Manipulation of a company´s prospectus, annual report etc.

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Manipulating a company's financial status via false and misleading information in its prospectus and annual report (Case Study 14) allowed it to qualify for a stock exchange listing; many AML/CFT regimes allow simplified due diligence for companies listed on regulated markets, so manipulative listing may reduce CDD obligations and support later concealment, including use as a front company. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Many AML/CFT regimes allow simplified due diligence measures for corporate entities that are listed on organised and regulated markets"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 14 (Cayman Islands): the managing director of an overseas company issued a prospectus with false information in the annual report, overstating group revenue by 275%, and gave it to the securities commission for listing; he set up a revocable trust and Cayman company, opened an overseas bank account in the company's name with power of attorney to trade. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "He overstated the company’s group revenue by 275%."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 14 (continued): the structure hid his trading in the overseas company and assets from illegal activities; the Cayman company held over USD 1 million; the Financial Reporting Authority made an onward disclosure to the FIU of the foreign national's home country, where he was charged with three counts of providing misleading and false information. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The foreign national has been charged in his home country with three counts of providing misleading and false information."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Professional intermediaries (lawyers, accountants, TCSPs) mostly act with integrity, but their reputation makes them targets of criminals and corrupt actors, and some become involved in concealing beneficial ownership through coercion, corruption, negligence or failure to identify suspicious activity. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "either through coercion or corruption, or through negligence or a failure to identify suspicious activities"</sub>

## SECTION 3 — VULNERABILITIES OF PROFESSIONAL INTERMEDIARIES

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Criminals use professionals for specialist advice on complex financial, business, company and tax matters to disguise ownership or source of assets; operating through a professional gives a veneer of legitimacy and creates distance from illicit wealth. The majority of case studies involved a professional intermediary. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "provides a veneer of legitimacy to criminal activities"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Four general categories describe the role of professional intermediaries in company formation: systems where legal persons can be established without intermediaries; systems requiring professional intermediaries (other than notaries); notarial systems; and systems where the company registrar tests filing accuracy or takes on the intermediary's CDD obligations (hybrids are possible). (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "systems in which professional intermediaries (other than notaries) are required"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Criminals may use several intermediaries at once, each with a separate role; more than one-third of cases used more than one professional sector, a similar number used multiple intermediaries in the same sector, and among multi-intermediary cases TCSPs were a large majority, legal professionals (including civil notaries) also common, and accounting professionals rare. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "more than one-third involved the use of more than one professional services sector"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Among cases with multiple intermediaries in the same sector, TCSPs were the overwhelming majority, and multiple TCSPs were almost always in multiple jurisdictions; multiple lawyers/accountants were mostly in the same jurisdiction and approximately half of cases involved unwitting or negligent intermediaries. Criminals are likely limiting engagement with any one professional, though the small number of cases makes a definitive assessment difficult. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "approximately half of the cases involved unwitting or negligent intermediaries"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Increasingly global organised crime and finance drive demand for intermediaries operating across or connected to many jurisdictions, so criminal groups connect with multiple intermediaries across countries; a majority of intermediaries in the cases operated on behalf of international clients. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a majority of intermediaries were operating on behalf of international clients"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Footnotes 41-42: of 50 jurisdictions assessed against the 2012 FATF Recommendations at April 2018, 34 have major or moderate shortcomings for Recommendation 22 (DNFBP CDD), 30 for Recommendation 23 (other DNFBP measures including STR reporting) and 36 for Recommendation 28 (regulation and supervision); of the 11 with minor or no shortcomings in DNFBP regulation and supervision mechanisms, 8 do not supervise, monitor and regulate DNFBPs appropriately. The Horizontal Study (Questions 4-6) confirmed this. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "34 jurisdictions have major or moderate shortcomings in their measures for Recommendation 22"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Paragraph 107 states that the FATF Standards require DNFBPs, including lawyers, notaries, accountants and TCSPs, to carry out customer due diligence (CDD), keep CDD and transaction records, and file suspicious transaction reports. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF Standards require DNFBPs, including lawyers, notaries, accountants, and TCSPs, to perform CDD"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 107 says the DNFBP obligations took effect when the standards were revised in 2003, but many countries have not yet put them into law. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These obligations came into effect when the standards were revised in 2003"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 107 says that, among countries that have imposed obligations on DNFBPs, many have not implemented them effectively through appropriate supervision and monitoring, a finding the Horizontal Study also confirmed. As a result, professional intermediaries are often subject to limited AML/CFT obligations. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Of those countries that have implemented obligations on DNFBPs, many have not implemented those obligations effectively"</sub>

## Continuum of complicity

- **descriptive / context**: In its 2013 report on legal professionals, the FATF assessed that involvement of legal professionals in ML could not be described simply as complicit or unwitting but follows a continuum from 'innocent involvement' to 'complicit' (Figure 1). (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "tended to follow a continuum from “innocent involvement” to “complicit”"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Figure 1 (text garbled in the excerpt) shows stages: unwitting/innocent involvement with no red flag; alert and proactive stages with low or higher suspicion where an STR is made where required and the professional proceeds with caution or stops acting; basic CDD with red flags missed or their significance misunderstood; wilfully blind, where further questions are not asked, transactions are completed and often no STR is filed, persisting for repeat instructions; being corrupted; and complicit, with actual knowledge of the criminality. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Actual knowledge of the criminality in which they are involved."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The continuum of complicity applies equally to all professional intermediary sectors and is not unique to the legal profession. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "can be equally applied to all professional intermediary sectors"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Although intermediaries are widely acknowledged as enablers of ML/TF, little is understood about how they are sourced or recruited and the degree to which they are innocently, negligently or complicity involved; the report lists factors exacerbating this intelligence gap (continued in the next excerpt). (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "little is understood about how these intermediaries are sourced or recruited"</sub>

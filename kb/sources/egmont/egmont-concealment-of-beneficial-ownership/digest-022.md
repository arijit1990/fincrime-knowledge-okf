## Case Study 54 – Namibia

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 54 (Namibia): two Namibians and three Chinese subjects registered two Proprietary Limited companies and a Namibian close corporation, then opened nine bank accounts at five local banks with one Chinese and two Namibian directors/shareholders as signatories. The entities and individuals received significant deposits and transfers derived from Namibian accounts and moved them to a foreign jurisdiction. (p. 142)[^original]

## Case Study 55 – Namibia

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 55 (Namibia): Mr. X said he was in the taxi business but regularly made large cash deposits into two accounts, followed immediately by large cheque withdrawals to other businesses and accounts of his close corporations and a relative. The corporate entity was registered for retail, mining, construction and fishing; withdrawals were exclusively electronic transfers. The account also received monthly funds from various individuals and large electronic transfers from a South African company into a Namibian Registered Trust's account. X owned several high-value properties in Namibia and South Africa bought in cash, some registered under legal entities, and was found guilty of drug dealing. (p. 142)[^original]

## Case Study 56 – Namibia

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 56 (Namibia): an STR was filed on Y on suspicion of illegal diamond dealing and co-mingling proceeds of crime with legitimate income in a business account. Y was the sole member of a close corporation whose principal business was 'manufacturing, recycling and cleaning'. Substantial deposits, mostly electronic transfers from individuals in America and Asia, were withdrawn by Y in cash, and Y presented himself online to foreign buyers as an authorised diamond dealer in Namibia. (p. 143)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An STR was filed on Y on suspicion that he might be involved in illegal diamond dealing"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Case Study 56 (Namibia) indicators: long inactivity after incorporation followed by a sudden unexplained increase in financial activity; bank balance close to zero despite frequent transactions; activities inconsistent with corporate profile; business-account transaction with an anomalous large cash deposit or withdrawal inconsistent with company profile; funds unusual in the context of the client's profile. (p. 143)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Long period of inactivity following incorporation, followed by a sudden and unexplained increase in financial activities"</sub>

## Case Study 57 – Namibia

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 57 (Namibia): subjects and entities used electronic banking to channel proceeds of crime abroad. Funds deposited into close corporations and a subject's personal account were then structurally withdrawn in the foreign jurisdiction under the pretext of business-related funds. Subject 1, a Chinese national, opened a personal account and registered a close corporation (Entity 1), which opened accounts with three different financial institutions. (p. 143)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Funds deposited into close corporations and subject’s personal account and then structurally withdrawn"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 57 (Namibia), continued: Subject 1 'assisted' Namibian woman 1 to open personal accounts at the same three institutions and to register four close corporations in her name (Entities 2-5) with accounts at one institution, and assisted Namibian women 2 and 3 to open accounts at two institutions. He controlled the ATM cards of Entities 1-5 and the accounts of women 1 and 2 and transacted on them. Woman 3 never collected her ATM cards and, when the bank asked why she opened accounts, disappeared and could not be traced. Funds into the accounts of women 1-3 and Entities 2-5 came from Subject 1, whose own accounts were funded by Chinese-owned entities. (p. 144)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Subject 1 controlled the ATM cards of Entities 1-5 and accounts in name of Namibian women 1 and 2"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Case Study 57 (Namibia) indicators: client reluctant to provide personal information; client reluctant or unable to explain business activities and corporate history; actively avoiding personal contact without sufficient justification; refusing to co-operate or provide information, data and documents usually required for a transaction; transactions strange given an individual's age; registered at an address that does not match the company profile; director or controlling shareholder(s) without an active role in the company; director, controlling shareholder(s) and/or beneficial owner(s) listed against accounts of other legal persons or arrangements; bank balance close to zero despite frequent transactions; multiple bank accounts without good reason; disinterested in the structure of a company they are establishing; no real business activities; exclusively facilitates transit transactions; questionable connections between parties; business-account transaction with an anomalous large cash deposit or withdrawal. (p. 144)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Disinterested in the structure of a company they are establishing"</sub>

## Case Study 58 – Netherlands

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 58 (Netherlands): Mr. B, a Dutch taxpayer, put money in a Jersey trust and did not declare it to the tax authorities. He did not state his involvement in the trust in his income tax returns and intentionally answered a tax questionnaire incorrectly or incompletely. The court found he intentionally gave incorrect information to a public servant of the Netherlands Tax and Customs Administration, resulting in too little tax being levied. (p. 144)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Mr. B, a Dutch taxpayer, had put money in a Jersey trust and had not declared this"</sub>

(p. 145)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Focused on aggressive tax minimisation strategies • Correct documents not filed with the tax authority"</sub>

## Case Study 59 – Netherlands

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 59 (Netherlands): a doctor received payments from the pharmaceutical industry, varying per contract, which were income but were paid not into his Dutch accounts but into Luxembourg numbered accounts in the name of a foundation. He never declared the balances of these accounts in his income tax returns. (p. 145)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "were not paid into one of the suspect’s Dutch bank accounts, but into Luxembourg numbered accounts"</sub>

## Case Study 60 – Netherlands

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 60 (Netherlands): a tax evasion scheme sent false invoices from a BVI company the suspect had incorporated to a Dutch company, creating the illusion that services were provided; the Dutch company paid the invoices, reducing turnover and profit. From the BVI the money went to private accounts of the suspect and a co-suspect in Cyprus, accessible in the Netherlands by debit/credit card, and the suspect used funds to finance real estate. (p. 145)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "sending false invoices from a company incorporated by the suspect in the BVI to the Dutch company"</sub>

## Case Study 61 – Netherlands

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 61 (Netherlands): the FIU received a financial institution's notification of an international transfer to a foreign company in Italy whose beneficial owner appeared to be the client's ex-wife. The client regularly sent money from his private and business accounts to his ex-wife and her businesses, and through 'loan agreements' the money was deposited back into his account. The notification was declared suspicious and forwarded to investigation teams. (p. 146)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The beneficial owner of this company appeared to be the ex-wife of the client"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Case Study 61 (Netherlands) indicators: transacts with international companies without sufficient corporate or trade justification; family members with no role listed as beneficial owners; finance from a lender other than a known credit institution with no logical explanation or commercial justification; business transaction involving family members without legitimate business rationale. (p. 146)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Family members with no role or involvement in the running of the business are listed as beneficial owners"</sub>

The Netherlands FIU asked Andorra for information and found the company's UBO appeared to be the same person as the purchaser; the notification was declared suspicious and forwarded to investigation teams. ## Case Study 63 – Netherlands

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 63 (Netherlands): a Dutch target company received loans, described as 'repayment loan', from a Swiss TCSP with a bank account in Montenegro; the TCSP is also its sole shareholder. The money was re-loaned via a TCSP subsidiary in Moldavia to the UBO in the Netherlands, and the company was also used by other TCSP clients, re-loaning funds to operational companies in Italy and England managed by the UBOs. The Montenegro account was topped up from a Swiss account in the UBO's name. The FIU suspects this way of re-loaning one's own money via the Swiss TCSP is used by others. (p. 147)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The received money was subsequently re-loaned again via a subsidiary of the Swiss TCSP in Moldavia"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Case Study 63 (Netherlands) indicators: entity formed in a low-tax jurisdiction or international trade/financial centre; transacts with international companies without sufficient corporate or trade justification; client is both ordering and beneficiary customer for multiple international funds transfers; finance from a lender other than a known credit institution with no logical explanation or commercial justification. (p. 147)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Legal person or arrangement conducts transactions with international companies without sufficient corporate or trade justification"</sub>

## Case Study 64 – Netherlands

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 64 (Netherlands): a criminal investigation into money laundering and the purchase and financing of premises and apartment rights in the Netherlands by two Liechtenstein trusts. The UBOs and source of funds are shielded by the trusts and several facilitators; the purchase and financing total almost EUR 2 million, presumably from drug trafficking. The trusts have their registered office in Liechtenstein and are represented by family members of the suspects. (p. 147)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The two trusts have their registered office in Liechtenstein and the persons who represent the trusts are family members"</sub>

(p. 147)[^original]

## Case Study 65 – Netherlands

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 65 (Netherlands): a Dutch investment fund put investors' deposits into foreign life insurance policies; investors participated in a trust that owned the policies, with payout on the death of the insured third parties going to the fund and then investors. The longevity risk was re-insured, and re-insurers took over the policy from the trust fund, paying investors an amount equal to the policy's death benefit value. All deposits, EUR 175 million, went through the trust companies' foreign accounts; only a limited part appears to have been invested in the promised second-hand policies, and a large part went immediately to accounts of the suspect and the trustee. (p. 148)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "All deposits, EUR 175 million, went through the foreign accounts of the trust companies"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Case Study 65 (Netherlands) indicators: unusually large number of beneficiaries and other controlling interests; bank accounts in multiple international jurisdictions without good reason; funds unusual in the context of the client's profile. (p. 148)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Unusually large number of beneficiaries and other controlling interests"</sub>

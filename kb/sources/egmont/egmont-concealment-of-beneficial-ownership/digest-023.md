## Case Study 68 – Netherlands

The provider let the suspects conceal these cash flows from third parties, with invoices for its services paid via the Swiss account. (p. 149)[^original]

## Case Study 70 – Netherlands

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 70 (Netherlands): Mr. and Mrs. X were directors of a Dutch holding company and a Dutch operating company, founders of a Unit Foundation and beneficial owners in an Offshore Investment Holding Company. Agreements for directorship and/or nominee shareholder services appear to have been drawn up. The offshore company's invoices list several services including opening a bank account, with no charge for management services. An employee of the Dutch TCSP has signing powers on its bank account, while Mr. and Mrs. X decide whether funds go from the underlying companies to the offshore company and on to the Unit Foundation, so it can be argued the employee in Cyprus only carried out their wishes and they are the de facto managers. (p. 150)[^original]

## Case Study 71 – Netherlands

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 71 (Netherlands): a criminal investigation into a Dutch TCSP began because of systematic failure to report unusual transactions and money laundering. It was presumed to involve facilitating fake transactions for foreign clients, e.g. so their assets were scarcely taxed or parked funds were moved by fake transactions to another jurisdiction, using complicated structures of companies and trusts in various countries, instructed by a financial service provider and discussed by the suspect with the Dutch civil-law notary. Dutch entities and Dutch foundations registered at an international address were part of them; a structure sometimes had eight entities in various countries, and the suspect reportedly did not in several cases know the identity of the actual beneficiaries of companies he incorporated. (p. 151)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A criminal investigation into a Dutch TCSP was instigated on account of the systematic failure to notify unusual transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Case Study 71 (Netherlands) indicators: entity formed in a low-tax jurisdiction or international trade/financial centre; relationships with foreign professional intermediaries without genuine business transactions in the professional's country of operation; focused on aggressive tax minimisation strategies; requests formation of a complex company structure without sufficient business rationale; complex structures lacking commercial sense; professional intermediary involved without due cause or apparent justification. (p. 151)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Relationships with foreign professional intermediaries in the absence of genuine business transactions in the professional’s country of operation"</sub>

## Case Study 72 – Netherlands

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 72 (Netherlands): the owner of a TCSP posed as a 'Business Lawyer' without being a registered lawyer. Clients paid remuneration for trust services that was partly paid into the suspect's accounts in three international jurisdictions, and a TCSP in an international jurisdiction was also reportedly used. He evaded tax on these amounts for years and committed immigration fraud by putting clients on the payroll of one of his companies to produce false employment contracts and/or salary slips. Ownership of Dutch company shares was often veiled by foundations and foreign structures via a low-tax jurisdiction; the Dutch companies were mainly used to channel money, and he reportedly laundered money in buying real estate for himself, his family or TCSP clients. (p. 151)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The owner of a TCSP posed as a “Business Lawyer” but was not registered as a lawyer"</sub>

## Case Study 76 – Netherlands

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 76 (Netherlands): international company A, headquartered in the Netherlands, paid corruption funds to a government employee via letter box companies. A company in an international jurisdiction named the government employee as beneficial owner but had nominee shareholders and directors. Payments went via a Dutch account of a subsidiary to a foreign company's account in Estonia and via a Hong Kong enterprise, then to accounts in an international jurisdiction and on to a Luxembourg account of the company. ## Case Study 77 – New Zealand

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 77 (New Zealand): a New Zealand shell company was set up by a New Zealand TCSP based in Vanuatu on behalf of an unknown overseas client, using nominees to hide beneficial owners. Its actual business was not apparent or indicated by its name, and its registered address was the TCSP's own virtual office in Auckland. The nominee director lived in Seychelles; the nominee shareholder was a nominee shareholding company owned by the TCSP that was itself substantially a shell and had been nominee shareholder for hundreds of other shell companies registered by the TCSP. (p. 154)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The address listed on the companies’ register was the same virtual office in Auckland as the TCSP"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 77 (New Zealand), activity: the company did trade transactions with several Ukrainian companies including a state-owned weapons trader; contracts were cancelled after funds were transferred and refunds went to different third-party international companies. It also transacted with three other New Zealand shell companies registered by the same TCSP, using the same nominee director, nominee shareholder and virtual office address. News reports indicated all four shell companies were involved in laundering USD40 million for the Sinaloa drug cartel based in Mexico. (p. 154)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "all four shell companies had been involved in laundering USD40 million for the Sinaloa drug cartel"</sub>

## Case Study 78 – New Zealand

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 78 (New Zealand): a New Zealand law firm was linked to clients implicated, arrested or convicted of offences including embezzlement, bribery, corruption, tax evasion and money laundering. The firm, based in New Zealand, served overseas clients using its knowledge of New Zealand tax, trust and company law. It routinely used its employees as nominee directors and shareholders, so beneficial owners (sometimes offenders and associates) were not publicly named. (p. 155)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A New Zealand law firm was linked to clients who had been implicated, arrested or convicted"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 78 (New Zealand), structures: chains of companies were often established (one the shareholder of another, which was shareholder of another), adding complexity and removing the beneficial owner further from the assets; sometimes a New Zealand shell company was trustee of a trust. The companies were usually all shell companies with nominee directors, shareholders and addresses, forming complex structures of companies, partnerships and trusts that can hide and protect wealth.

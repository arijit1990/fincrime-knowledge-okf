## Case Study 14 – Cayman Islands

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 14 (Cayman Islands): the managing director of an overseas company issued a prospectus with false information in the annual report, overstating group revenue by 275%, and gave it to his country's securities commission for the listing proposal. He set up a revocable trust and underlying Cayman company, then opened an overseas bank account in the Cayman company's name on which he held power of attorney and could trade. The structure hid his trading in the overseas company and assets from his illegal activities; the Cayman company held over USD 1 million in the account. (p. 121)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "He overstated the company’s group revenue by 275%"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 14 (Cayman Islands), outcome: the Financial Reporting Authority (FRA) made an onward disclosure to the FIU of the foreign national's home country, where he has been charged with three counts of providing misleading and false information. (p. 121)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Financial Reporting Authority (FRA) made an onward disclosure to the FIU"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Case Study 14 (Cayman Islands) indicators: falsified paper trail; entity formed in a low-tax jurisdiction or international trade/financial centre; bank accounts in multiple international jurisdictions without good reason; repeat transactions where the executing customer is a signatory to the account but is not listed as having a controlling interest in the company or assets. (p. 121)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Bank accounts in multiple international jurisdictions without good reason"</sub>

## Case Study 15 – China

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 15 (China): the suspect used the identities of close relatives and company employees to set up eight shell companies while keeping actual control, and fabricated false documents and sales contracts to obtain financing from six banks. He also defrauded 3 state-owned enterprises through financing and false trading using illegal financial institutions such as underground banks, and moved the money to his private accounts for personal use and repaying personal debt. (p. 122)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The suspect used the identity of his close relatives and company employees to establish eight shell companies"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Case Study 15 (China) indicators: false invoices for services not carried out; falsified paper trail; family members with no role listed as beneficial owners; nominee owners and directors including informal nominees such as children, spouses, relatives or associates not involved in running the enterprise; questionable connections between parties; finance from a lender other than a known credit institution with no logical explanation or commercial justification; business-account transaction that appears to fund personal purchases (assets or recreational activities) inconsistent with company profile; transaction between two legal persons with similar or identical directors, shareholders or beneficial owners. (p. 122)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Nominee owners and directors including informal nominees, such as children, spouses, relatives or associates who do not appear"</sub>

## Case Study 16 – China

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 16 (China): suspect A used his influence as an enterprise manager to help Company X (owned by suspects A, B and C) win a tender, receiving dividends in proportion to capital stock. After the win, B took over control. A asked B to open an offshore account for him in Hong Kong and transfer funds under the guise of a housing purchase; offshore companies and accounts were opened in the names of B's wife and sisters, and after a portion was deposited the accounts passed to A's control. A fled; B had Company X's vice president transfer funds to the Hong Kong accounts of his family members, and the money was moved back to China via underground banks and spread to five new domestic accounts in an employee's name. (p. 122)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Suspect A asked Suspect B to open an offshore account for him in Hong Kong"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Case Study 16 (China) indicators: director or controlling shareholder(s) cannot be located or contacted; regularly sends money to low-tax jurisdictions or international trade/finance centres (the source repeats a phrase here); multiple bank accounts without good reason; family members with no role listed as beneficial owners; nominee owners and directors including informal nominees; transaction between connected parties without apparent business or trade rationale; business transaction involving family members of a party without legitimate business rationale; transaction appears cyclical; two legal persons with similar or identical directors, shareholders or beneficial owners; funds sent to or received from a low-tax jurisdiction or international trade/finance centre (phrase repeated in the source). (p. 123)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Director or controlling shareholder(s) cannot be located or contacted"</sub>

## Case Study 19 – Ecuador

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 19 (Ecuador): public officials with relatives and individuals connected to law firms created companies in several countries to receive bribes. Bribes were paid through individuals linked to companies supplying goods and services to a public oil-sector institution; to send payments and hide the real beneficiaries, the suppliers created companies in Panama, Hong Kong, British Virgin Islands, Bahamas, Uruguay and the US. (p. 124)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Public officials along with relatives and individuals connected to law firms created a series of companies"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Case Study 19 (Ecuador) indicators: client reluctant or unable to explain source of wealth/funds; politically exposed persons or associates; long inactivity after incorporation followed by sudden unexplained increase in financial activity; entity formed in a low-tax jurisdiction or international trade/financial centre; regularly sends money to such jurisdictions or centres; transacts with international companies without sufficient corporate or trade justification; relationships with foreign professional intermediaries without genuine business transactions in the professional's country of operation; discrepancy between the supposed wealth of the settlor and the object of the settlement; false invoices for services not carried out; employees of professional intermediary firms acting as nominee directors or shareholders; complex structures lacking commercial sense; nominee owners and directors; funds sent to or received from a low-tax jurisdiction or international trade/finance centre. (p. 125)[^original]

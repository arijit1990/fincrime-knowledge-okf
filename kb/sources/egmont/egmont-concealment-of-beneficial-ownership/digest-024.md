## Case Study 79 – New Zealand

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 79 (New Zealand): assets believed bought with proceeds of crime were allegedly linked to the settlor of certain trusts, some structures set up via a NZ TCSP. No assets are held directly by the trustees but through various US domestic and foreign entities; activities appear US-based, with orders against US entities indirectly owned via overseas companies. The scheme involved two trusts and four companies with nominee directors and shareholders employed by a law firm, and the complex web of shell companies and trusts prevented law enforcement from obtaining beneficial ownership information. ## Case Study 80 – New Zealand

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 80 (New Zealand): shell companies in Panama, Belize and the UK with nominee shareholders and directors were used to open Latvian bank accounts for hundreds of millions of dollars of international payments, mostly on behalf of Vietnamese entities for imported goods or to Vietnamese expats for purportedly Vietnam-based senders. This distinct Vietnamese connection indicates the accounts may be controlled or administered from Vietnam. New Zealand accounts (held by students or fruit wholesalers and exporters) received funds from accounts in Latvia, Cambodia and China, and more than 15 NZ properties were bought with funds from the Latvian accounts through NZ law firms. The Latvian accounts were also 'topped up' by shell company accounts in international jurisdictions, indicating coordinated layering. (p. 157)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Shell companies based in Panama, Belize, and the UK with nominee shareholders and directors have been used to open Latvian bank accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Case Study 80 (New Zealand) indicators: declared income inconsistent with assets, transactions or lifestyle; entity formed in a low-tax jurisdiction or international trade/financial centre; activities inconsistent with corporate profile; complex structures lacking commercial sense; complicated routings without sufficient explanation or trade records; funds unusual in the context of the client's profile; funds sent to or received from a low-tax jurisdiction or international trade/finance centre. (p. 157)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Transaction involves complicated transaction routings without sufficient explanation or trade records • Funds are unusual in the context"</sub>

## Case Study 81 – New Zealand

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 81 (New Zealand): companies registered in New Zealand by a Vanuatu-based TCSP run by New Zealand citizens were suspected shell companies facilitating crime abroad. The TCSP acted as nominee shareholder and supplied nominee directors living in places such as Vanuatu, Panama and the Seychelles; for Company A the recruited employee likely knew nothing of the activities. Crimes include smuggling of illegal goods, arms smuggling, tax fraud, investment fraud and money laundering. Company A leased the plane caught smuggling arms, and 73 companies registered in New Zealand by the TCSP were suspected of acting as such shell companies. (p. 158)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "73 companies registered in New Zealand by the TCSP were suspected of acting as shell companies"</sub>

## Case Study 82 – Norway

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 82 (Norway): seven Norwegian citizens, in different combinations, owned four small Norwegian IT companies. A major listed Norwegian company offered to buy all of them at a price much higher than their share capital (input value for taxation). The owners created new offshore companies, sold their shares to them with minimum profit, and those companies immediately resold the shares to the actual buyer in Norway, so the sales profits were realised abroad with no tax. (p. 158)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the owners established new companies in offshore jurisdictions and sold their shares to those companies with a minimum of profit"</sub>

## Case Study 84 – Norway

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 84 (Norway): the head of a shipping company committed breach of trust by buying ships and equipment meant for the company through a structure of companies he ultimately controlled, then selling the assets to the company at an inflated price. He also defrauded the banks financing the ships by alleging the ships were bought at market price. Although beneficial ownership was determined, legal challenges remain in confiscating assets frozen in foreign bank accounts that were not party to the criminal case. (p. 159)[^original]

## Case Study 85 – Panama

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 85 (Panama): the purported purpose was real estate development and construction funded by small investors, but funds from the settlor or third-party adherents came from illegal activities (corruption of public servants and illicit enrichment). A BVI company with nominee directors was ultimately controlled by a PEP who was a client of a bank with a relationship with the TCSP. The TCSP set up a real estate trust to receive money and assets, which were invested in a real estate project and given as a warranty to the bank financing 60% of it. The ultimate beneficial owner of the project was the PEP's son. (p. 160)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The ultimate beneficial owner of the real estate project was the son of the PEP"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Case Study 85 (Panama): the trustee did not conduct extensive due diligence and relied on the due diligence of the bank that referred the client, since both client and trustee had a business relationship with that bank. (p. 160)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The trustee did not conduct extensive due diligence and relied on the due diligence performed by the bank"</sub>

## Case Study 86 – Peru

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 86 (Peru): concerns a Peruvian PEP, his wife, his mother-in-law and other close individuals after property purchases. Two mortgages were paid in advance with funds from a Costa Rican company established only six months before the wire transfer instructions; the offshore company paid the loan in just four months despite the financial loss. Peruvian authorities established the funds came from corrupt activities by the PEP during his administration. The mother-in-law's purchase of a luxury property, which she lacked the economic capacity to make, led to a case at the FIU and SARs from reporting entities. (p. 160)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Two mortgages were paid in advance using funds from a Costa Rican company"</sub>

## Case Study 87 – Russia

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 87 (Russia): a state customer contracted Contractors #1 and #2 for research work and special software development. Analysis showed neither did research itself but passed budgetary funds to subcontractors, some with real laboratories. Most of Contractor #1's funds went to a subcontractor that channelled them into a shadow financial scheme of multiple layers of shell companies, finally withdrawn in cash. Most of Contractor #2's funds went to a real estate company that invested in its business, bought luxury cars and gave zero-interest loans to several individuals. (p. 161)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these contractors did not conduct any research activities themselves, but transferred budgetary funds to subcontractors"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 87 (Russia), ownership analysis (of ownership data, address registry, an air tickets booking database, financial transactions and law enforcement data): Contractor #2 was previously owned by Mr. X before passing to straw men uninvolved in the scheme, and the real estate company was formerly his before transfer to his daughter. Contractor #1 was owned by straw men who knew nothing of its business, took instructions from Mr. X and received a 'salary' from the company's account. The state department director responsible for research was Mr. X's brother; that director's daughter, and a woman who flew jointly with Mr. X, bought expensive real estate with cash deposited into their accounts in advance. (p. 161)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Contractor #1 was owned by straw men who had no idea about the company’s business activities"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Case Study 87 (Russia) indicators: politically exposed persons or associates; false invoices for services not carried out; family members with no role listed as beneficial owners; nominee owners and directors including informal nominees such as children, spouses, relatives or associates; loans from private third parties without supporting loan agreements, collateral or regular interest repayments; business-account transaction with an anomalous large cash deposit or withdrawal inconsistent with company profile; multiple large cash payments used to pay down a loan or mortgage. (p. 162)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Transaction involves the use of multiple large cash payments to pay down a loan or mortgage"</sub>

## Case Study 88 – Russia

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 88 (Russia): embezzled public funds of RUB 300 million (11 million USD) went from Company K's account to Company R, a Delaware corporation owned and managed by the Russian wife of the suspect, a state official. The same day R sent USD 11 million as a loan to Company A (BVI) at a Cypriot bank; A sent more than USD 11 million to Company D (US) to buy real estate in France, and D sent more than USD 12 million to a French Notaries Bureau. (p. 162)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Company R, a Delaware corporation, was owned and managed by the Russian wife of the suspect, a state official"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 88 (Russia), continued: information from the Luxembourg FIU showed a US bank acted as guarantor for the suspect's wife in buying shares of a French company, the holder of the real estate, via an S.S. company (a French subsidiary of a Luxembourg S.D. SA incorporated and owned by the same individual). Analysis showed the two chains were interrelated and the real estate was bought with embezzled public funds for the benefit of the official's wife. (p. 162)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Analysis showed that these two chains were interrelated"</sub>

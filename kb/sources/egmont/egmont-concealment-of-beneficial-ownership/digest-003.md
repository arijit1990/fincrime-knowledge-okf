## DEFINITION OF BENEFICIAL OWNER

- **descriptive / definition**: The FATF standards define beneficial owner as the natural person(s) who ultimately own(s) or control(s) a customer and/or the natural person on whose behalf a transaction is conducted, including persons exercising ultimate effective control over a legal person or arrangement. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "natural person(s) who ultimately7 own(s) or control(s) a customer and/or the natural person on whose behalf"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The beneficial owner definition differs from 'beneficiary'/'beneficiaries', which can include natural and legal persons and arrangements and often relate to recipients of charitable, humanitarian or other assistance through an NPO, or persons entitled to benefit under a trust or insurance policy. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "which can include both natural and legal persons and arrangements, and often relate to"</sub>

## DEFINITION OF BENEFICIAL OWNER

- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 7 explains that 'ultimately owns or controls' and 'ultimate effective control' indicate situations where ownership or control is exercised through a chain of ownership or by means of control other than direct control; footnote 8 says the definition should also apply to the beneficial owner of a beneficiary under a life or other investment-linked insurance policy. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "indicates situations in which ownership/control is exercised through a chain of ownership"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The beneficial owner/beneficiary distinction relies on 'ultimate' control or benefit, meaning the natural person who ultimately controls or benefits from an asset or transaction through direct or indirect means. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "relies on the concept of “ultimate” control or benefit"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: A beneficial owner must always be a natural person because legal persons are always controlled directly or indirectly by natural persons; a legal person or arrangement can be a beneficiary, but determining the beneficial owner requires discovering the natural person(s) who ultimately control or benefit. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a “beneficial owner” must always be a natural person"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Determining ultimate control is often the principal challenge in determining beneficial ownership; in a company control can be exerted by shareholders, directors and senior management, and shareholders are generally considered to exert the greatest control. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is often the principal challenge of determining beneficial ownership"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Paragraph 29: ultimate benefit and control are central to separating 'beneficial' from 'legal' ownership. The legal owner is the natural or legal person or arrangement holding legal title to an asset, yet legal ownership is not always needed to control or benefit from an asset, especially one held in trust or owned by a legal person. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "legal ownership is not always essential in order to exert control over, or benefit from, an asset"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 29 concludes that it is essential to identify the natural person who controls an asset, rather than the asset's legal owner. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is therefore essential to determine the natural person who controls an asset"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Shareholders have the greatest control because they can dismiss directors and senior staff and stand to benefit from profits, but the role of directors and senior management cannot be overlooked. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the role of directors and senior management cannot be overlooked"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In trusts, the trustee controls the asset but is legally bound to act in the beneficiary's interests, the beneficiary generally cannot control the trust, and the settlor and protector may retain some control or influence, which complicates identifying the beneficial owner and can require further efforts to determine the true nature of the trust relationship. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the trustee exerts control over an asset but is legally bound to act in the interests of the beneficiary"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Control can be exerted through third parties, including professional intermediaries, family members, associates, nominees and other natural persons recruited or coerced to act for the ultimate beneficial owner; this complicates identification because the beneficial owner may not appear in formal company or trust records in many jurisdictions. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Control can also be exerted via third parties, including professional intermediaries, family members, associates, nominees"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 31: while it is important for competent authorities to be able to understand the identity of the natural person controlling an asset, it is also important that they understand who benefits from it. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is also important for competent authorities to understand who benefits from it"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 32 points to the FATF Guidance on Transparency and Beneficial Ownership for further guidance on the definition of 'beneficial owner'. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Further guidance on the definition of “beneficial owner” is available in the FATF Guidance on Transparency and Beneficial Ownership"</sub>

## SECTION 1 — MISUSE OF LEGAL PERSONS AND ARRANGEMENTS

- **recommended / expectation**: The section's information is meant to assist financial institutions and intermediaries in risk analysis and does not suggest any form of legal person or arrangement is high-risk or low-risk by default; private sector entities are encouraged to apply a risk-based approach to customers and transactions on a case-by-case basis. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Private sector entities are encouraged to apply a risk-based approach to customers and transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Numerous kinds of legal persons exist under many company laws, making asset tracing across countries difficult; companies feature in most schemes to obscure beneficial ownership, and almost all analysed cases involved at least one company, with separation of legal and natural personality a key reason for their popularity. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Almost all of the cases analysed for this report involved at least one company"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The report did not compare forms of legal person because that exceeded project scope and most case studies gave no insight into the types and legal peculiarities of legal persons used; it focuses on broader characteristics. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "most of the case studies did not provide specific insights into the types and legal peculiarities"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: One factor possibly contributing to higher misuse of a type of legal person is the absence of accurate, up-to-date information on its ownership and management, which the Horizontal Study (Question 3) shows remains a challenge in many jurisdictions. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the absence of accurate and up-to-date information on its ownership and management"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In a general partnership, ownership and control are exercised by all partners, so the ability to misuse it to disguise beneficial ownership is significantly reduced; the same applies to general partners of a limited partnership, whereas limited partners can have some anonymity by acting solely as investing partners but generally have limited control. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the ability to misuse a general partnership to disguise beneficial ownership is significantly reduced"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Capital companies focus on shareholders' capital participation rather than personality, are always a separate legal entity, and are often owned through shares that can be transferred and sold regularly without affecting the company's existence; LLCs (private company limited by shares) and foundations are hybrids discussed separately. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "capital companies are always a separate legal entity"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A company's main feature is strict separation between the natural person owning it via shares and its legal personality, allowing it to hold assets and incur liabilities in its own name and letting a natural person take part in business without disclosing their identity; shareholders usually appoint a board of directors to manage it in a fiduciary capacity. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This legal structure allows a natural person to take part in business without disclosure of their personal identity15"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnotes state that securities laws may provide some transparency (e.g. notification when a shareholder exceeds a certain shareholding in listed companies), and that Van der Does de Willebois et al. (2011) found companies to be the most misused corporate vehicle documented in their study. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "companies are the most misused corporate vehicle documented in the study"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Private companies such as LLCs combine elements of partnerships and companies, are restricted in ways that vary by jurisdiction, do not offer interests to the public and are therefore generally subject to less stringent reporting and oversight; share transfers are often limited. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "generally subject to less stringent reporting and oversight regimes"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: LLC ownership and control rules are set by a contract that may not be publicly available and gives members a high degree of freedom, allowing latitude to exploit nominees and obscure true ownership and control arrangements. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "allowing latitude to exploit nominees and obscure true ownership and control arrangements"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 17 says 'limited liability company' is intended to cover forms such as LLC (US), Pvt Ltd. (UK, Ireland, India, Hong Kong), GmbH (Germany, Austria, Liechtenstein), BV (Netherlands) and SARL (France). (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "GmbH in Germany, Austria, Liechtenstein; BV in the Netherlands; SARL in France"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Foundations are separate legal entities with no owners or shareholders, generally managed by a board of directors and generally restricted to public-benefit services, though several jurisdictions allow private foundations; safeguards usually ensure independence from the founder, but foundations are vulnerable to money laundering exploitation, particularly where laws allow the founder to exert control. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Foundations are separate legal entities with no owners or shareholders"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Only a small number of analysed cases involved a foundation, and a very small number involved a partnership; almost all involved a company, indicating companies are significantly attractive for misuse. No specific conclusions were drawn on vulnerabilities of particular forms because cases lacked information. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "almost all of the cases analysed for this report involved the use of a company"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 19 describes private foundations as pursuing not-for-profit activities for members or founders, found in countries including Germany, Bulgaria, Panama, the Netherlands and Sweden, usually funded by an individual or small group, with legal personality via a written act and supervisory authority recognition; registration is usually faster and less demanding, accounting simpler and costs lower than for public foundations. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Private foundations pursue not-for-profit activities on behalf of their members or founders"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 20 defines a 'shelf company' as an incorporated company with inactive shareholders, directors and secretary, left dormant for a longer period even if a customer relationship has been established; footnote 21 notes shelf companies can also be considered shell companies, particularly after sale or transfer, so jurisdictions may have called former shelf companies shell companies in case studies. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an incorporated company that has inactive shareholders, directors, and secretary and is left dormant"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 22 defines a 'front company' as a fully functioning company with all characteristics of a legitimate business, which ultimately serves to disguise illicit financial activity; front companies are often cash intensive businesses. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a fully functioning company with all the characteristics of a legitimate business"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Paragraph 45 says a range of characteristics allow criminals to exploit legal persons to conceal beneficial ownership; the excerpt, which ends mid-list, names the use of shell, shelf and front companies and the construction of complex chains. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A range of characteristics have been identified which allow legal persons to be exploited by criminals"</sub>

## Bearer shares and bearer share warrants

- **descriptive / definition**: Bearer shares are company shares in certificate form legally owned by whoever physically holds the certificate; ownership and control can be exchanged anonymously by physical exchange alone, with no record documented or reported. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "legally owned by the person that has physical possession of the bearer share certificate"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Because the owner of a bearer share cannot be accurately ascertained or monitored, determining beneficial ownership of legal persons controlled by bearer shares is nearly impossible; bearer shares and warrants have historically been recognised as a significant ML risk, particularly for concealing beneficial ownership. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "determining beneficial ownership of legal persons controlled by bearer shares is nearly impossible"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The report states that FATF Recommendation 24 requires member countries to take measures to prevent the misuse of bearer shares and bearer share warrants. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "which requires member countries to take measures to prevent the misuse of bearer shares and bearer share warrants"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In most jurisdictions bearer shares have been reformed or eliminated through dematerialisation into a computerised register; where still permitted, the financial sector often requires them to be placed in trust before a client relationship starts, and other jurisdictions require an intermediary to make transfers lawful. Their use has markedly declined, and only four case studies involved bearer shares, though the difficulty of identifying their owners may limit detected cases. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "only four involved the use of bearer shares"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Footnote 38: of 50 jurisdictions assessed against the 2012 FATF Recommendations at April 2018, 45 have no bearer shares or warrants in circulation or existence and five have no restrictions but it is unclear whether any circulate. Of the 45, 17 prohibit them, 15 require conversion into registered shares, five require holding with a regulated financial institution or professional intermediary, two require controlling shareholders to notify the company which records their identity, one has a range of these options and five have none. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "45 jurisdictions either do not have bearer shares or bearer share warrants in circulation"</sub>

## Formal nominee shareholders and directors

- **descriptive / definition**: A nominee shareholder is the registered owner of shares held for another person's benefit; a nominee director is appointed to a company board to represent the interests of the appointer. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A nominee shareholder is the registered owner of shares held for the benefit of another person."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Legally nominees are responsible for operating the company and accept the legal obligations of directorship or ownership in the country of incorporation, but some nominees hold the position in name only for someone else, possibly governed by a trust or civil contract with the actual director or shareholder. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a nominee may hold the position of director or shareholder in name only on behalf of someone else"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Nominee shareholders and directors are common in most countries, and some countries formally recognise permitted nominee scenarios (such as publicly traded companies); uses include shielding the nominator from public disclosure or meeting legal requirements such as a domestically resident director. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to shield the nominator from public disclosure requirements or to meet legal requirements"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Formal nominee services are offered by legal and accounting professionals, TCSPs and professional nominees (people who rent their identification information to companies for nominee purposes only, with no other services); vulnerabilities of nominee provision by lawyers, accountants and TCSPs are covered in Section 3. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "people who rent their identification information to companies for nominee purposes only"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 81 (New Zealand) is cited as showing a TCSP providing nominee directorship services for over 1 000 New Zealand companies on behalf of foreign clients; authorities suspect at least 73 facilitated crimes abroad including smuggling of illegal goods, arms smuggling, tax fraud, investment fraud and money laundering. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "provided nominee directorship services for over 1 000 companies registered in New Zealand on behalf of foreign clients"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Although nominee use is lawful or at least not unlawful in most jurisdictions, nominees have been used to disguise ownership and control or circumvent laws on foreign business ownership and trade; FIUs and law enforcement report known criminals and individuals prohibited from serving as directors using nominee services, so formal nominee services are vulnerable to exploitation. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "individuals who have been prohibited from serving as a director of a company due to previous malfeasance"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Just under half of the analysed cases involved formal nominees; nominee directors and shareholders in company records can delay law enforcement identification of the beneficial owner or create false links between companies sharing nominees. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "just under half of the cases involved formal nominees"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: The report says FATF Recommendation 24 states that countries should take measures to prevent the misuse of nominee shares and nominee directors. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "countries should take measures to prevent the misuse of nominee shares and nominee directors"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 81 (New Zealand): companies registered in New Zealand by a Vanuatu-based TCSP run by New Zealand citizens were suspected shell companies facilitating foreign crime; the TCSP acted as nominee shareholders and supplied nominee directors resident in Vanuatu, Panama and the Seychelles, plus a New Zealand-based nominee director to meet the resident-director and address requirement. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to satisfy the legal requirement to have a New Zealand resident director and address"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 81 (continued): the employee recruited as director of Company A likely knew nothing of the activities; by 2010 the TCSP had registered about 2 000 New Zealand companies for foreign clients, mostly using an Auckland registered office, and authorities suspect at least 73 facilitated crimes abroad (smuggling, arms smuggling, tax fraud, investment fraud, money laundering). (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the TCSP had registered approximately 2 000 companies in New Zealand on behalf of clients in foreign jurisdictions"</sub>

## Informal Nominee Shareholders and Directors

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Informal nominees perform the same function as formal nominees but usually have a personal rather than professional connection to the true owner; law enforcement commonly identifies spouses, children, extended family, controlled business associates and other personal associates, and reports foreign students and tourists being convinced or coerced to establish companies for third parties, sometimes for nominal payments, and then rarely involved in operations. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "foreign students and tourists have been convinced or coerced into establishing companies on behalf of third parties"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Just under half of the analysed cases involved informal nominees. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Of the case studies analysed for this report, just under half involved informal nominees."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Informal nominee arrangements are rarely governed by contract; whereas formal nominees insulate themselves from company activities, informal nominees are more likely to profess to be the beneficial owner to maintain the fiction, hence being called 'straw' or 'front' men. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "informal nominees are more likely to profess to be the beneficial owner of the legal person or arrangement"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 87 (Russia) is cited: ownership of companies used to defraud a government contract passed from suspect Mr. X to several straw men, including his daughter; at least one received a salary but was unaware of the company's activities, and the purpose was to distance the companies from Mr. X, who was related to the man responsible for the project in the public department. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the ownership of companies used to facilitate fraud against a government contract was passed from the suspect"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Acting as an informal nominee carries significant risk: they are legally responsible for the company's activities, often lack resources to distance themselves, and cannot use protections such as client confidentiality or legal professional privilege available to some formal providers, so are more susceptible to investigation; yet informal nominees with no prior law enforcement attention or only an indirect link to the owner are often hard for financial institutions and some authorities to identify. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "informal nominees are unable to utilise protections such as client confidentiality or legal professional privilege"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Some law enforcement agencies report use of stolen identities to establish legal persons (the victim is ostensibly an informal nominee without knowledge or consent) and companies registered to informal nominees who sold their identification details to a third party, often through financial hardship, and who have no visibility of the company. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the use of stolen identities to establish legal persons"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 80 (New Zealand) is cited for bank accounts in students' names used to receive laundered foreign funds to buy properties, and Case Study 77 for lower-income individuals manipulated into selling identification information to professional money launderers who use it to set up companies and bank accounts. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "lower-income individuals can be manipulated into selling their identification information to professional money launderers"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Cases showed an approximately equal split between formal and informal nominees, but law enforcement and FIU experience indicates criminals, particularly those with limited resources, favour informal nominees, often family members (especially spouses) who are frequently complicit, possibly because the beneficial owner can easily control them. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "approximately equal distribution between the use of formal and informal nominees"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 77 (New Zealand): a shell company set up by a Vanuatu-based New Zealand TCSP for an unknown overseas client used nominees; its business was not apparent from its name, its registered address was the TCSP's virtual office in Auckland, the nominee director lived in Seychelles, and the nominee shareholder was a TCSP-owned nominee shareholding company, itself essentially a shell used for hundreds of other shells. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The address listed on the companies’ register was the same virtual office in Auckland as the TCSP."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 77 (continued): news reports said a power of attorney transferred directorship to a Russian national who had sold his passport details, with a bank account in Latvia; he told OCCRP journalists he knew nothing of it. A former Russian tax police officer said hundreds of law firms specialise in ready-made shell companies, usually relying on disadvantaged individuals who sell passport details for approximately USD 100-300. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "rely on disadvantaged individuals who sell them passport details for approximately"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 77 (continued): trade transactions were conducted with Ukrainian companies including a state-owned weapons trader, contracts were cancelled after funds were transferred and refunds went to different third-party international companies; three other New Zealand shells from the same TCSP with the same nominee director, shareholder and address were also used, and news reports said all four laundered USD 40 million for the Sinaloa drug cartel. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "all four shell companies had been involved in laundering USD 40 million for the Sinaloa drug cartel"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 80 (New Zealand): shell companies in Panama, Belize and the UK with nominee shareholders and directors opened Latvian bank accounts for hundreds of millions of dollars in international payments, mostly on behalf of Vietnamese entities for imported goods or to Vietnamese expats, suggesting control from within Vietnam; New Zealand accounts held by students or fruit wholesalers and exporters received funds from Latvia, Cambodia and China. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This distinct Vietnamese connection indicated the accounts might have been controlled or administered from within Vietnam."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 80 (continued): more than 15 New Zealand properties were bought with the funds, all through New Zealand law firms; the Latvian accounts were also topped up by other shell company accounts abroad, indicating a coordinated layering process. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "More than 15 New Zealand properties were purchased with the funds"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 87 (Russia): a state customer contracted Contractors #1 and #2 for research and software; neither did research but passed budget funds to subcontractors. Most of Contractor #1's funds went through a subcontractor to layers of shell companies and were withdrawn in cash; most of Contractor #2's went to a real estate company that invested in its business, bought luxury cars and gave zero-interest loans to individuals. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The majority of funds from Contractor #1 was sent to its subcontractor"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 87 (continued): ownership data, address registry, air ticket booking database, transactions and law enforcement data showed Contractor #2 and the real estate company were formerly owned by Mr. X (the latter transferred to his daughter) and Contractor #1 was owned by straw men on a 'salary' following Mr. X's instructions; the director of the state department responsible for research was Mr. X's brother, and his daughter and a woman who flew with Mr. X bought expensive real estate with cash deposited in advance. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The director of the state customer’s department responsible for research activities was a brother of Mr. X."</sub>

## Declaring Numerous Beneficiaries

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Declaring numerous beneficiaries on one account can confuse financial institutions and conceal the nature of transactions; FIUs and financial institutions report large numbers of customers declared as beneficiaries on one account so the bank cannot tell which transaction was for which beneficiary, and it is unclear whether the transaction controller was listed; co-mingling many beneficiaries challenges determining the ultimate (excerpt ends mid-sentence). (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "large numbers of customers have been declared as beneficiaries on a single bank account"</sub>

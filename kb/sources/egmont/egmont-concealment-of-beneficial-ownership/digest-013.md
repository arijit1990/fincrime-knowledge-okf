## Low-Tax Jurisdictions

- **descriptive / context**: Many OFCs actively take part in global efforts against ML and tax evasion via the FATF, Egmont Group and OECD Global Forum; many OECD Global Forum members signed the EOIR and AEOI standards, and some OFCs began automatic exchange in 2017 while others were expected to begin by September 2018. (p. 82)[^original]

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 43 (Italy): an investigation into a transnational criminal organisation was triggered by STRs on flows from a British Virgin Islands company via a Swiss bank to an Italian legal person for refurbishing a EUR 9 million property; a chartered accountant was charged with money laundering, a search seized documents on many offshore vehicles set up for wealthy national clients, and around EUR 800 million was found moved between Italy and international accounts. (p. 82)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "around EUR 800 million had been moved between Italy and international accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 68 (Netherlands): Dutch suspects were investigated for incorrect tax returns, money laundering and forgery; funds went through a numbered Swiss account in the name of a Panamanian financial service provider, and very similar amounts were soon debited under a false description to the credit of the suspects; the provider helped conceal the cash flows, with invoices for its services paid via the Swiss account. (p. 82)[^original]

## Online and Virtual Services

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Disguising beneficial ownership is exacerbated by online and virtual services; many providers and financial institutions have reduced or removed face-to-face interaction, and such services will likely become more prevalent. (p. 84)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The ability to disguise beneficial ownership is exacerbated by the provision of online and virtual services"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Establishing companies, business banking relationships and moving money without face-to-face contact can facilitate identity fraud, smurfing, cuckoo smurfing and concealment of beneficial ownership; despite verification measures (document verification services, formal virtual identities), reliance on customer-provided documents can enable fraudulent documents or informal nominees acting as representatives without the knowledge of professionals or institutions. (p. 84)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "reliance on documentation provided by a customer in the absence of face-to-face engagement can enable the use of fraudulent documentation"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 70 defines smurfing as using multiple individuals or accounts to perform transactions to avoid suspicion or currency reporting requirements; footnote 71 describes cuckoo smurfing as seeking to transfer wealth through the bank accounts of innocent third parties, named after the cuckoo bird that lays eggs in other birds' nests (AUSTRAC). (p. 84)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "using multiple individuals or accounts to perform transactions so as to avoid suspicion or currency reporting requirements"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Financial institutions and professional service providers need to be conscious of individuals and intermediaries that may be manipulating online and virtual facilities. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "need to be conscious of individuals and intermediaries that may be manipulating these facilities"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Internet banking lets unknown individuals control bank accounts anonymously; FIUs report criminals coercing 'straw men' to open accounts, then handing over account details including internet banking logins and passwords after CDD is complete, disguising the beneficial owner and circumventing CDD. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "criminals will often coerce “straw men” to establish bank accounts for use by the criminal at a later time"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Israeli case (Case Study 38) shows online services letting a suspect set up foreign companies and accounts using straw men's identification, and foreign online banking letting an unknown third party control accounts of unrelated individuals; authentic documents such as a genuine passport can be used by third parties without face-to-face contact, because document verification checks only authenticity, not whether the document belongs to the person opening the account. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "document verification controls are only designed to verify the authenticity of the document"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Some financial institutions and RegTech firms have implemented or are developing virtual CDD measures: capturing metadata from client interactions such as IP addresses and geolocation data; using built-in cameras of phones, tablets, laptops and ATMs to capture the customer's image (with knowledge and consent) for verification against identity documents; and using biometric identifiers including facial recognition and fingerprint scanning. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "using in-built cameras from mobile phones, tablets, laptops, and automatic teller machines (ATMs)"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: These technologies could significantly reduce online vulnerabilities, but their expense and sophistication will likely limit short-term implementation, and most professional service providers and smaller financial institutions will remain vulnerable to the challenges of identifying beneficial ownership virtually. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the expense and sophistication of these CDD systems is likely to limit their implementation in the short term"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 38 (Israel, repeated): the suspect, owner of a registered MSB who also ran an unregistered MSB, used contacts in East Asia to engage local company service providers; local straw men were registered as shareholders using passports supplied by the contacts, with East Asian addresses and bank accounts. (p. 86)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Local straw men were registered as the shareholders of the new international companies established for the scheme."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 38 (continued): immediately after accounts were opened the suspect received the sole means of control, an electronic token with online passwords; some accounts were run at low volume to build credibility and credit history while others carried high volume, and bank queries were passed by the straw men to the suspect and returned to the bank by them. (p. 86)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the suspect received the sole means to control them, i.e. an electronic token with the passwords for online activities"</sub>

## Use of Third-Party CDD and Identity Verification

- **descriptive / context**: Third-party providers of identity verification and CDD support (sanctions lists, adverse information, company ownership information) can be an important part of a robust CDD program, but responsibility for CDD remains with the financial institution or DNFBP in outsourcing or agency relationships, in accordance with FATF Recommendation 17. (p. 86)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "responsibility for CDD measures remains with the financial institution or DNFBP in context of outsourcing or agency relationships"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Some major financial institutions reported via the Wolfsberg Group that third-party provider information can be out of date or incomplete, frustrating CDD including beneficial ownership verification; they detected this only through their own CDD and financial intelligence capabilities, whereas smaller institutions without established CDD mechanisms may not realise inaccuracies exist. (p. 86)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the information provided by third-party service providers can be out-of-date or incomplete"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Because in-house CDD and financial intelligence capabilities are expensive, most institutions and professionals will keep relying heavily on third-party services; why third-party data is sometimes deficient is not understood and may reflect the difficulty of collecting current information globally, and virtual identities may improve this in future. (p. 87)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "most financial institutions and professional service providers will continue to rely heavily on the services provided by third parties"</sub>

## Reliance on introduced business

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Financial institutions and DNFBPs may rely on other regulated institutions and DNFBPs for CDD in circumstances set out in Recommendation 17 (often a bank relying on a lawyer or TCSP forming a company and opening accounts for it); if Recommendation 17's reliance requirements are not properly applied, a negligent or complicit DNFBP can compromise the institution's CDD and its ability to identify beneficial ownership or suspicious activity. (p. 87)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "If the requirements for reliance set out in Recommendation 17 are not properly applied"</sub>

## SECTION 5 — CONCLUSIONS AND ISSUES FOR CONSIDERATION

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Schemes obscuring beneficial ownership often use a 'hide in-plain-sight' strategy using global trade and commercial infrastructure; tools meant to encourage business growth, such as limited liability corporations and nominee directorship services, are now exploited for ML, and globalisation has increased the threat, leaving countries to enforce national laws in a borderless commercial environment. (p. 88)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "many of the tools that were designed to encourage business growth and development"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report drew on open-source research, public intelligence reports, classified intelligence holdings and public and private sector experience; its findings largely confirm principles in existing literature, suggesting vulnerabilities are enduring or increasing despite ongoing AML/CFT efforts. (p. 88)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the vulnerabilities associated with the concealment of beneficial ownership are enduring, or increasing"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Implementing the FATF recommendations on beneficial ownership has proven challenging, so the FATF developed Guidance on Transparency and Beneficial Ownership to help countries implement Recommendations 24 and 25, and Recommendation 1 as it relates to understanding ML/TF risks of legal persons and arrangements; the issues for consideration may support effective implementation of the two Recommendations. (p. 88)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Implementation of the FATF recommendations on beneficial ownership has proven challenging for countries."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report concludes limited liability companies (and similar companies elsewhere) are more vulnerable to misuse for concealing beneficial ownership than other legal persons, owing to ease of establishment and use in complex ownership structures; availability and use of formal and informal nominee directors and shareholders appear to exacerbate risks despite FATF Standards requiring measures to prevent misuse, and nominees are a central enabler of indirect ownership chains. (p. 88)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "limited liability companies (and similar companies in various jurisdictions) are more vulnerable to misuse"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Paragraph 228 says the FATF Recommendations require that competent authorities can obtain adequate, accurate and timely information about who owns and controls legal persons, i.e. their beneficial ownership and control (Recommendation 24). (p. 88)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF Recommendations require competent authorities to have access to adequate, accurate and timely information on the beneficial ownership"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Paragraph 228 adds that countries must act to stop legal arrangements being misused for money laundering and terrorist financing, in particular by ensuring adequate, accurate and timely information is available on express trusts (Recommendation 25). (p. 88)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In addition, countries must take measures to prevent the misuse of legal arrangements"</sub>

## Client advocacy and brokerage services

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Professionals also introduce clients to mortgage brokers and financial advisers and sometimes seek specialist services on a client's behalf, especially legal professionals with contacts abroad; this poses the same risks as acting as client advocate or intermediary, and several intermediaries in the cases performed services for another intermediary on behalf of a third-party client. (p. 69)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a number of professional intermediaries performed services for another intermediary on behalf of a third-party client"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Professionals receiving facilitation requests from international professionals acting for international clients are at heightened risk of facilitating ML and obscuring beneficial ownership because verifying the identity and motives of the client and beneficial owner is hard; in Case Study 39 (Israel) law firms contacted foreign TCSPs to set up companies and bank accounts, and layers of intermediaries inhibited CDD by the foreign bank and TCSP. (p. 69)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "heightened risk of facilitating money laundering and obscuring beneficial ownership information"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: An unwitting professional may be unable to judge the complicity of a corrupt international professional and may naively trust a request based on their own ethics, risking unwittingly committing a domestic crime for an international criminal syndicate and damaging their reputation and professional standing. (p. 69)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may naively trust the legitimacy of the request based on their own professional ethics and morality"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 39 (Israel): a scheme hid proceeds of fraud via foreign exchange and binary options trades; local companies posed as legitimate trading platforms, and Israeli companies, representatives of foreign banks and law firms contacted TCSPs abroad that set up shell companies and opened bank accounts for them; the TCSPs then took no part in management, and suspects used the companies to launder money or sold them to third parties for profit. (p. 69)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "After the companies were established, the TCSPs were not involved in their management nor in any related activity."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 101 (United States): the defendant ran a mortgage broker business and several real estate companies, concealing ownership with nominee accounts and shell corporations; properties were bought from entities he controlled through an employee, financed by loans. (p. 69)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "He used nominee accounts, shell corporations and other schemes to conceal his ownership."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 101 (continued): loan applications contained fraudulent financial information and appraisals overstating collateral value; he 'sold' commercial property between entities he controlled at highly elevated prices and altered invoices to inflate the cost of work so improvements to collateral properties falsely appeared to have been made. (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the defendant altered invoices directed to one of the entities by inflating the cost of the work listed"</sub>

## Providing services to clients and intermediaries domiciled internationally

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Because most professionals specialise in structures within their own country, international clients and intermediaries commonly seek their services; the majority of intermediaries in the cases served clients in another country, relationships may be short-term transactional or ongoing (especially TCSPs where resident directors or trustees are required), and most of those serving overseas clients also provided directorship, trustee, nominee or virtual office services. (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the majority of professional intermediaries were providing services to clients based in another country"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Professionals servicing international clients are vulnerable to deception and fraud by criminal clients, complicit foreign professionals or unwitting intermediaries; the report says professional intermediaries and financial institutions require sophisticated CDD capabilities to accurately identify beneficial ownership, particularly without face-to-face engagement. Most intermediaries serving overseas clients were assessed as unwittingly or negligently involved. (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "require sophisticated CDD capabilities to accurately identify beneficial ownership"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 85 (Panama) is cited: a smaller TCSP failed to do enhanced due diligence on an overseas client, relying on the referring financial institution's due diligence, and the trust it managed collected proceeds of corruption and illicit enrichment. (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a smaller TCSP failed to conduct enhanced due diligence on their overseas client"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Some professionals, especially in countries with strict DNFBP regulation, have developed international networks of trusted intermediaries for CDD; this likely mitigates some vulnerabilities but relies on the intermediary's capability and honesty, and as the professional has limited control over them yet retains the risk, the vulnerability remains. (p. 71)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they rely on the trusted intermediary having the capabilities necessary to perform accurate CDD"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 36 (Guernsey): a Guernsey TCSP was exploited by a foreign client to administer a company used for market manipulation; over five years it was unaware of the fraud and raised no suspicions with Guernsey authorities. In a CFTC investigation (2014-2016) of UK national Mr. X Doe, who made about GBP 32 million from purported futures dealing, TCSP B (formerly administered by a Cayman company), licensed for AML/CFT, identified he was under investigation and co-operated with Guernsey authorities. (p. 71)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Over a five-year-period Mr. X Doe made approximately GBP 32 million"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 85 (Panama): a purported real estate development scheme funded by small investors and illicit funds (corruption, illicit enrichment) involved a BVI company with nominee directors controlled by a PEP who was a bank client; the TCSP set up a real estate trust, the assets were used as warranty for a bank financing 60% of the project, and the ultimate beneficial owner was the PEP's son. (p. 71)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The ultimate beneficial owner of the real estate project was the son of the PEP."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 85 (continued): the trustee did not do extensive due diligence and relied on the referring bank's, since both client and trustee had a relationship with the bank. (p. 72)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "relied on the due diligence performed by the bank that referred the client"</sub>

## Providing advice on tax compliance

- **descriptive / context**: A key role of many providers, particularly accountants and lawyers, is advising on maximising profits and minimising costs, often including lawful minimisation of tax liabilities. (p. 72)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "advising clients on lawful means of minimising their taxable liabilities"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Tax advice expertise is vulnerable to exploitation by those seeking to disguise beneficial ownership to evade tax, though professionals' tax law knowledge reduces the risk of innocently or unwittingly advising on or facilitating tax evasion. (p. 72)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the risk of innocently or unwittingly providing advice on, or facilitating of tax evasion schemes is reduced"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Authorities report high professional involvement in tax evasion schemes, often with complex transnational structures, fraudulent trade and false invoicing and phoenixing; many cases had tax evasion as predicate offence, most involved professionals (principally legal or accounting), the majority assessed as complicit, and criminals target complicit professionals paying lucrative fees. (p. 72)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Criminals actively target complicit professionals to assist with tax evasion"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Almost all complicit intermediaries in tax evasion cases were also scheme designers and/or promoters, so beneficial owners may be unaware the scheme is illegal, a vulnerability for unwitting beneficial owners and the sectors' reputation; no case specifically identified unwitting beneficial owners, but several focused on the professionals' own corruption. (p. 72)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "almost all complicit intermediaries involved in tax evasion cases were also assessed as being the designer and/or promoter"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 5 (Australia): a 'round robin' scheme moved funds from suspects' companies to New Zealand companies and accounts controlled by a Vanuatu-based accountant (a bank account signatory), falsely described as 'management and consultancy fees' with matching false invoices and no evidence of consulting work. (p. 72)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The payments were falsely described in the companies’ records as “management and consultancy fees,”"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 5 (continued): the false expenses were claimed as deductions in companies X, Y and Z's tax returns; the accountant then moved the funds via round-robin international transactions through accounts of companies he owned, including an overseas company separate from the New Zealand recipients, into the suspects' personal accounts in Australia, using false international loan agreements with a foreign lender so the receipts were not assessed as income. (p. 73)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "false documents were created purporting to be international loan agreements with a foreign lender"</sub>

## Legal professional privilege and client confidentiality

- **descriptive / context**: Legal professionals' ethical obligations, which vary by country but follow a core set, include independence from the State, honesty, integrity and fairness, duty to act in the client's interest and maintaining client confidentiality and LPP; some law societies consider these rules prevent knowing involvement in ML/TF, but some obligations are vulnerable to criminal exploitation, and FIUs report LPP and confidentiality being used to protect the identity of the beneficial owner, frustrating investigations. (p. 73)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIUs and other competent authorities have reported the use of LPP and client confidentiality to protect the disclosure"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: LPP generally does not extend to all lawyer-client communications and often stops short of commercial advice (varying by country); communications outside the national LPP definition are protected by legal confidentiality, which is not absolute and is limited in certain key areas. (p. 73)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "LPP generally does not extend to all communications between a lawyer and their client"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The initial application of LPP and confidentiality often rests with the legal professional rather than an independent third party, so there is no clear, consistent interpretation among professionals despite case law and guidance. (p. 73)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the initial application of these protections often rests with the legal professional rather than an independent third party"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: LPP belongs to the client and can only be waived at the client's direction or if the legal professional is being used to commit a criminal offence; breaching LPP is an offence in many countries (professional sanctions or criminal charge), so legal professionals can take a conservative approach. (p. 74)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "LPP is considered to belong to the client, and can only be waived at the direction of the client"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Complicit legal professionals can exploit LPP and confidentiality to delay investigations, and because of general caution any legal professional can unwittingly conceal criminality through it. A Dutch investigation of a TCSP and civil-law notary involved structures designed by an international law firm; multiple legal professionals in several countries asserted privilege, and Dutch authorities had to verify their rights through time-consuming mutual legal assistance requests. (p. 74)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "have asserted privilege in order to delay or frustrate the investigation"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Law enforcement and FIUs report LPP is regularly exploited by complicit legal professionals to hinder investigations; privilege claims must be reviewed before being overturned even if questionable, the subjective nature of LPP will remain a challenge, and related FATF-reported evidence-gathering challenges may explain fewer legal-professional cases in the sample and little evidence of complicity. (p. 74)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "claims of privilege need to be reviewed prior to being overturned, even if the grounds for LPP are questionable"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 57 (citing FATF 2013): evidence-gathering challenges include obtaining DNFBP records, uncertainty over the scope of privilege, difficult and time-consuming processes for seizing legal documents and lack of access to client account information; law enforcement are required by law to have strong evidence from the outset to show LPP/secrecy should be removed, and privilege claims can impede and delay investigations. (p. 74)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Law enforcement agencies are required by law to have strong evidence from the outset"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Client confidentiality among accountants generally does not prohibit disclosure permitted or required by law, so it is less vulnerable; however, in some countries accountants afford clients LPP or a close equivalent, some hold legal qualifications and work as solicitors, and those working at lawyers' behest may be subject to LPP, facing similar vulnerabilities to legal counterparts. (p. 74)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Accounting professionals who are subject to LPP obligations face similar vulnerabilities"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 193 reports that, during consultation, some private sector representatives said LPP training for legal professionals can often be inadequate unless the lawyer specialises in litigation, where LPP is frequently considered; legal professionals in tax, private client, corporate or estate planning work are likely to rarely need to consider or use LPP. (p. 74)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "LPP training offered to legal professionals can often be inadequate unless the legal professional specialises in litigation"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 193 says it has been suggested that a low level of training, together with a lack of practical application by some lawyers, leads to broad and conservative approaches to LPP. (p. 74)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the low level of training, coupled with a lack of practical application by some lawyers"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Paragraph 193 states that enhanced training and guidance on LPP may help reduce this vulnerability over time. (p. 74)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Enhanced training and guidance in this area may assist to reduce this vulnerability over time."</sub>

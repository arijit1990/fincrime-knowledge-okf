## Trust and Company Service Providers

- **descriptive / definition**: For this report, the terms 'TCSP' and 'TCSP sector' exclude professionals operating in the legal and accounting sectors. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the terms “TCSP” and “TCSP sector” exclude professionals operating in the legal and accounting sectors"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: GIFCS input shows that in countries with a highly active, well-established TCSP sector the market is dominated by many small businesses with no large dominant players, and a relatively small proportion of TCSPs are accounting or legal firms or their subsidiaries. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the market is dominated by a large number of small businesses with no significantly large players"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: TCSPs are particularly well established in low-tax jurisdictions, such as GIFCS members, where they play a far more active role in company establishment and management. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "particularly well established and defined in low-tax jurisdictions, such as those that are members of GIFCS"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The majority of GIFCS members require TCSPs and their shareholder controllers and key persons (director, partner, MLRO, compliance officer) to be fit and proper, assessing integrity, competence (including mandatory relevant professional qualification and continuous personal development for key persons in executive roles) and financial soundness; other members strongly encourage executives to hold qualifications. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to be fit and proper. In determining this, authorities consider integrity, competence"</sub>

## Role in the Establishment and Management of Legal Persons and Arrangements

- **descriptive / finding or result**: In most countries TCSPs' role is limited to incorporation and registration, not strategic business or financial advice; TCSPs appeared in over one-third of case studies, were the largest proportion of intermediaries and were more likely in cases with multiple intermediaries, though the count likely includes lawyers and accountants broadly called TCSPs. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "TCSPs were identified in over one-third of the case studies analysed for this report"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: TCSPs give a low-cost way to engage in international business, useful to small and medium businesses owing to lower fees; the sector shows low globalisation with most providing services only in their own country, and the majority of TCSPs in the cases served overseas customers and established legal persons/arrangements locally. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the lower fees associated with TCSPs make them a useful resource for small to medium-sized businesses"</sub>

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 140 says that, in GIFCS jurisdictions, the requirement for a TCSP's key persons to hold a professional qualification does not generally extend to a shareholder controller unless that person holds a director, manager or compliance role in the TCSP, although the shareholder controller remains subject to all other aspects of the fit and proper test. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "does not generally apply to a shareholder controller unless they are occupying a director, manager or compliance role"</sub>

## Role in the Establishment and Management of Legal Persons and Arrangements

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Some TCSPs offer complete company packages including incorporation, registration and a bank account in the country of incorporation; more than half opened bank accounts for clients (mostly overseas), acting as intermediary and facilitating CDD. Most also offer trustee, protector, directorship and virtual/registered office services, and almost all TCSPs in the cases provided directorship, trustee, nominee or virtual office services. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "More than half of TCSPs were assessed as having opened bank accounts on behalf of their clients"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: TCSPs increasingly serve clients virtually without face-to-face engagement; some require meeting an intermediary in the client's country for CDD but many rely only on documents provided virtually, which makes AML/CFT effectiveness, particularly CDD to identify the ultimate beneficial owner, more challenging. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "many others rely only on documentation provided virtually by the client"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: One-third of case studies referred to TCSPs serving other professional intermediaries (lawyers, accountants) for third-party clients; approximately half of TCSPs involved were unwitting, suggesting their role is more transactional and they are less likely to be masterminds, and complicit TCSPs were more likely wilfully blind than fully complicit or may have been misclassified. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "approximately half of the TCSPs involved were unwitting in their involvement"</sub>

## Other Intermediaries

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Law enforcement and private sector representatives reported 'full service' real estate firms that create shell and shelf companies, provide corporate officers, close transactions with lawyers and identify properties, reportedly working with developers to enable fraud where strong early sales are essential to financing; detailed analysis was not possible, though real estate professionals providing the covered services would face similar vulnerabilities. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "“full service” real estate firms, which provide a full range of intermediary functions"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: FIUs and law enforcement identified non-professional 'business finders' who are outside Recommendation 22 services but help clients set up complex structures by finding other professionals willing to establish the legal persons; because they are not actively involved in formation, they fall outside the regulated population under Recommendation 22. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "are often responsible for finding other professional intermediaries capable of (and willing to) establish the legal persons"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The role of business finders is not well understood; law enforcement experience relates mainly to those serving criminal clients (professional money laundering facilitators), and whether they play any legitimate corporate role is unknown but unlikely or questionable. Approximately 20% of cases involved a professional money launderer performing tasks like a professional intermediary (Case Study 38). (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "approximately 20% were assessed as having involved a professional money launderer"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The report did not assess vulnerabilities of these other intermediaries for lack of information, but assesses that non-professional intermediaries pose a vulnerability to the professional intermediaries they engage, heightened where they can create legal persons without a professional's involvement. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these non-professional intermediaries pose a vulnerability to other professional intermediaries"</sub>

## OVERVIEW OF VULNERABILITIES

- **descriptive / context**: Key vulnerabilities assessed from case studies and the experience of FIUs, law enforcement and regulators: establishing legal persons and arrangements; establishing and selling shelf companies; providing directorship, trustee, virtual office and mailbox services; facilitating transactions through trust or client accounts; facilitating purchase or sale of real property; client advocacy and brokerage services; services to internationally domiciled clients and intermediaries; advice on tax compliance and tax minimisation; legal professional privilege and client confidentiality; and limited AML/CFT obligations or insufficient awareness and compliance. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "limited AML/CFT obligations or insufficient awareness and compliance."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The list of vulnerabilities is not intended to be exhaustive and represents the more commonly exploited vulnerabilities. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is not intended to be exhaustive, and represents the more commonly exploited vulnerabilities"</sub>

## Establishing legal persons and arrangements

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Advice from intermediaries on company formation, structures and asset management often centres on protecting assets from high-risk activity and lawfully minimising tax; criminals actively seek complicit and unwitting professionals to protect illicit assets and evade tax through concealment of beneficial ownership. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "known to actively seek the advice of complicit and unwitting professionals"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Some countries require legal professionals (principally notaries) to incorporate and register companies, but many allow direct engagement with the public authority; where legal representation is unnecessary, intermediaries are employed to advise on the most appropriate structure, explain or facilitate the process, and enhance respectability and perceived legitimacy and trustworthiness. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "enhance respectability and perceptions of legitimacy and trustworthiness"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Case Study 100 shows a legal professional's services exploited to enhance apparent legitimacy of a corporate structure used in a loan-fraud pyramid scheme; the legal representative was likely complicit. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the services of a legal professional were exploited to enhance the apparent legitimacy of a corporate structure"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Because of their expertise and role in establishing companies, intermediaries are vulnerable to knowingly or unwittingly facilitating complex ML schemes; the majority of cases with companies and legal persons were facilitated by a professional intermediary, and providers in major global trade and finance hubs are vulnerable to international clients seeking company establishment. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The majority of cases that involved companies and other legal persons were facilitated by a professional intermediary."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Almost all cases involving legal arrangements involved professional intermediaries; since trusts can use legal persons as trustees, a provider may never engage directly with the ultimate beneficial owner, and together with strict confidentiality laws applicable to trusts this makes trust-establishing providers vulnerable to exploitation. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the professional service provider never engages directly with the ultimate beneficial owner of the assets held in trust"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 100 (United States): an individual ran a loan-fraud pyramid scheme falsely inflating his company's sales and revenues, with his wife and son; defendants created numerous legal entities (trusts, corporations, LLCs) to open bank accounts and hide ownership, using an attorney to create entities, and loans were diverted to private benefit including gems and jewellery. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an individual organised a loan-fraud pyramid scheme to falsely inflate the sales and revenues of his company"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 100 (continued): the attorney set up trusts for the individual and family and helped sell jewellery held in them using false documents showing the jewellery as a gift into the trust; USD 2.8 million from the sale was wired into the trust's brokerage account, and USD 200 000 was later moved to another trust's account using the address of the attorney, by then deceased. (p. 62)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "USD 2.8 million from the sale of the jewellery was wired into the trust’s brokerage account"</sub>

## Establishing and selling shelf companies

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Corporate law practices and TCSPs occasionally establish and hold shelf companies in anticipation of need, with the intermediary or employees recorded as nominee directors or shareholders; easy incorporation has reduced the need for legal and accounting professionals to do so but TCSPs (especially online and in major hubs) still sell them, and shelf companies can be used for any purpose including large complex structures. (p. 62)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the professional intermediary or its employees are recorded as nominee directors or shareholders of the company"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Many TCSPs also set up bank accounts registered to the shelf company that are retained after sale, which can complicate financial institutions' CDD; approximately one-third of intermediaries in the cases opened bank accounts for clients, mostly in foreign jurisdictions. (p. 62)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This practice can complicate CDD activities performed by the financial institutions."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Case Study 104 discussion says criminals specifically targeted shelf companies, likely desiring their corporate history to lend legitimacy, and shelf companies were sold by nominee directors with pre-established bank accounts. (p. 62)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "criminals specifically targeted shelf companies to facilitate their fraudulent scheme"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 104 (United States): the defendants conspired to sell fraudulent renewable energy credits using shell and shelf companies in the US, in order to fraudulently obtain US government renewable energy tax credits for renewable fuels that were never produced and to launder the proceeds, which bought real estate, boats, cars, watches and gold. Law enforcement determined that the defendant directed a network of his professional contacts to buy shelf companies across the US to serve as purported purchasers of renewable fuel and purported sellers of feedstock.

## Legal arrangements

- **descriptive / definition**: One way to translate a fiduciary relationship into a legal agreement, especially in common law countries, is the settlement of a trust; there is no universal definition, but functionally a trust separates legal property, administration and economic benefit of an asset. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a trust can be said to separate the legal property, administration, and economic benefit of an asset23"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Trusts can serve objectives including: transferring administration of an asset to a third party to organise an inheritance; protecting assets for children, family classes or vulnerable adults; managing an asset in common for a pool of corporations (e.g. syndicated loans); financing charity through an intermediary gathering funds; and investing to finance a future expense such as education fees or retirement. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Trusts can be used to achieve varying objectives, including:"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Trusts have a long history in common law and are a more ambiguous concept in civil law countries, but similar 'trust-like' arrangements exist in some civil law countries (such as the 'fiducie', which cannot be used to facilitate a legacy); civil law countries not recognising trusts have often used other mechanisms for the same functions, for example life insurance contracts in a European perspective. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "similar “trust-like” legal arrangements exist in some civil law countries"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 23: the FATF Recommendations use Article 2 of the Hague Trust Convention to define a trust; key characteristics are separation of assets from the trustee's estate, title in the name of the trustee or another person on the trustee's behalf, and the trustee's power to manage assets according to the trust's terms. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the separation of the assets from the trustee’s estate"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Horizontal Study found 60% of responding jurisdictions provide for creating trusts or similar arrangements under domestic law; 21% give some recognition to foreign arrangements and permit them to be created or administered by gatekeepers within their jurisdiction (e.g. under the Hague Trust Convention); 19% do not recognise any legal arrangements, domestic or foreign. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "60% of responding jurisdictions provided for the creation of trusts or other similar legal arrangements"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Apart from the intent to separate legal and beneficial ownership, it is unclear why criminals exploit trusts; they may exploit secrecy provisions to stop competent authorities unravelling ownership, particularly with foreign trusts, and foreign trusts may carry risks owing to differing tax treatment and lack of coordination between tax authorities. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is not clear precisely why criminals exploit trusts in money laundering schemes"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In the analysed cases, criminals used foreign jurisdictions in broadly the same proportions when establishing legal persons and legal arrangements. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "criminals used foreign jurisdictions in broadly the same proportions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The complexity and expense of establishing legal arrangements may limit their use compared with legal persons; legal arrangements appeared in approximately one-quarter of cases, possibly because many cases involved sophisticated predicate offences with significant proceeds that warranted the investment. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The relative frequency of the use of legal arrangements in the cases analysed for this report"</sub>

## SECTION 2 — TECHNIQUES USED TO OBSCURE BENEFICIAL OWNERSHIP

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Additional techniques not explored in the FATF's previous guidance are shelf companies, front companies, misleading naming conventions, false loans and invoices, and declaring numerous beneficiaries. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "use of shelf companies28 and front companies29, misleading naming conventions, false loans and invoices"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnotes 27-29 define shell companies (incorporated but no independent operations, significant assets, ongoing business or employees), shelf companies (incorporated, inactive shareholders, directors and secretary, left dormant for longer) and front companies (fully functioning with all characteristics of a legitimate business, usually cash intensive). (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a “front company” is considered to be a fully functioning company with all characteristics of a legitimate business"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Paragraph 52 reports that, according to the 2014 FATF Guidance on Transparency and Beneficial Ownership, beneficial ownership information is commonly hidden by means of: shell companies (especially where foreign ownership is spread across jurisdictions); complex ownership and control structures; bearer shares and bearer share warrants; legal persons being freely allowed to act as directors; formal nominee shareholders and directors where the nominator's identity is not disclosed; informal nominee shareholders and directors such as close associates and family; trusts and other legal arrangements that allow legal and beneficial ownership of assets to be separated; and intermediaries, including professional intermediaries, involved in forming legal persons. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "beneficial ownership information is commonly obscured through the use of:"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Three broad methods of obscuring beneficial ownership: (1) generating complex ownership and control structures using legal persons and arrangements, particularly across multiple jurisdictions; (2) using individuals and financial instruments (bearer shares, nominees, professional intermediaries) to obscure the relationship between beneficial owner and asset; (3) falsifying activities through false loans, false invoices and misleading naming conventions. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "falsifying activities through the use of false loans, false invoices, and misleading naming conventions"</sub>

## Generating complex ownership and control structures

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: A key method is using legal persons and arrangements to distance the beneficial owner from an asset through complex chains of ownership; adding numerous layers across different jurisdictions and using different types of legal structures can prevent detection and frustrate investigations. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Adding numerous layers of ownership between an asset and the beneficial owner in different jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: More than half of the case studies used complicated ownership structures combining direct and indirect control, often with chains of legal persons and arrangements across multiple countries; only a small number of cases had the beneficial owner retaining legal ownership through a complicated structure without an intermediary. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "More than half of the case studies submitted in support of this report made use of complicated ownership structures"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: There are few restrictions on establishing chains of ownership within and across jurisdictions: legal persons may own shares in companies in any country and many countries allow legal persons to be registered as directors. Shell and front companies feature prominently in most complex structures identified, while trusts and other arrangements are less frequently identified. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There are few restrictions on the establishment of chains of ownership within and across jurisdictions."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Complex ownership and control structures are not in themselves unlawful and often serve legitimate purposes; globalisation, communications technology and ease of travel are making global finance and business centres accessible beyond large corporations and high net worth individuals. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Complex ownership and control structures are not, in and of themselves, unlawful."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Complex structures can simplify business for companies trading transnationally, serving international clients or operating abroad, and are often used by family businesses, government-owned ventures and publicly traded companies; in these cases a financial institution or professional can readily ascertain beneficial ownership, and such structures are generally transparent to authorities with minimal vulnerability. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "will be in a position to readily ascertain the beneficial ownership of the structure"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Complex structures can be used to obscure beneficial ownership, avoid taxation, conceal wealth, launder proceeds, and in fraudulent investment schemes, phoenix activity, false invoicing and other fraud; the majority of cases with tax evasion, fraudulent investment schemes and fraud as predicate offences also used complex structures. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The majority of case studies that involved tax evasion, fraudulent investment schemes and fraud as predicate offences"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 31 defines illegal phoenix activity as creating a new company to continue the business of a company deliberately liquidated to avoid paying debts, including taxes, creditors and employee entitlements. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Illegal phoenix activity is the creation of a new company to continue the business of a company"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Using numerous legal persons or arrangements in one structure, numerous bank accounts and nominee directors can significantly impair FIUs, authorities and financial institutions in identifying and verifying the beneficial owner, especially when structures span jurisdictions; mutual legal assistance and other information requests are often slow or complicated by legal hurdles, and results often show the company is owned by another legal person or arrangement abroad. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "mutual legal assistance and other forms of bilateral or multilateral information requests are often slow to action"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Horizontal Study (Question 3) showed considerable challenges in ensuring accurate, up-to-date information on legal persons in many jurisdictions; the more companies and countries in a structure, the greater the challenge of timely discovery of the ultimate beneficial owner. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the greater the number of companies and countries involved in a corporate structure"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 88 (Russia): RUB 300 million (USD 11 million) of embezzled public funds moved from Company K to Company R, a Delaware corporation owned and managed by the Russian wife of a state official suspect; the same day Company R lent USD 11 million to Company A (BVI) at a Cypriot bank, which sent over USD 11 million to Company D (US) to buy French real estate, and Company D sent over USD 12 million to a French notaries bureau. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Company R, a Delaware corporation, was owned and managed by the Russian wife of the suspect, a state official"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 88 (continued): Luxembourg FIU information showed a US bank acted as guarantor for the suspect's wife in buying shares of a French company holding the real estate, via an S.S. company (French subsidiary of a Luxembourg S.D. SA, incorporated and owned by the same individual); analysis showed the two chains were interrelated and the property was bought with embezzled funds for the wife's benefit. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Analysis showed that these two chains were interrelated and the real estate was purchased with the proceeds"</sub>

## Shell and Shelf Companies

- **descriptive / definition**: The 2014 FATF Guidance defined shell companies as companies incorporated but with no significant operations or related assets; the FATF 2013 report on legal professionals used a similar definition describing shell companies as a technique to place or layer illicit funds. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "companies that are incorporated but which have no significant operations or related assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Shell companies can serve legitimate purposes, such as acting as a transaction vehicle for company mergers or protecting a corporate name from use by another party. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "serving as a transaction vehicle for company mergers or protecting a corporate name"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Shell companies are the most common type of legal person used in schemes to obscure beneficial ownership; more than half of analysed cases specifically referred to them (likely an undercount, as many countries referred to legal persons generally), and of those, the majority included a corporation in a foreign jurisdiction. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "shell companies are the most common type of legal person used in schemes and structures"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: In illicit structures, money may flow through multiple layers of shell companies before being withdrawn in cash or transferred internationally to its final destination. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "money may flow through multiple layers of shell companies before finally being withdrawn in cash"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Indicators that a company may be a shell include use of only a post-box address, a lack of personnel (or a single staff member), and a lack of tax and/or social benefit payments; shell companies are difficult to detect because incorporation is often no different from other companies. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the use of only a post-box address, a lack of personnel (or only a single person as a staff member)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Shell companies in complex structures are a consistent and enduring technique of criminal groups, corrupt individuals and complicit professionals; increased availability of shell companies to foreign nationals, enabled by global communications and converging trade markets, has exacerbated the issue. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is a consistent and enduring technique used by criminal groups, corrupt individuals, and complicit professionals"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Shelf companies serve legitimate purposes: they let investors secure a company structure within hours for time-sensitive needs; a shelf company operating for years lets the new owner use its history to secure business relationships or credit, and some already have customer relationships with financial institutions, easing access to the international financial system. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "shelf companies allow investors, or people planning a new undertaking, the possibility of securing a company structure within hours"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: When a shelf company is sold, inactive shareholders transfer shares to the purchaser and directors resign; the purchaser may receive the credit history; directors occasionally continue as nominees, particularly when a TCSP established and sold the company, so the only apparent change is ownership. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the inactive shareholders transfer their shares to the purchaser, and the directors submit their resignations"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: A change of shelf company ownership is apparent only if properly recorded in company registries, which is often 'overlooked' where shelf companies are used to disguise beneficial ownership (excerpt ends mid-sentence with law enforcement and FIU reports). (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "However, the change of ownership will only be apparent if it is properly recorded in company registries."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Paragraph 63 says many shell companies have no physical presence and are geographically anchored through TCSPs and nominee directors whose role in managing and directing the shell company is limited. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "geographically anchored through the use of TCSPs and nominee directors"</sub>
- **expected / finding or result**: Paragraph 63 describes this as a particular problem with shell companies and a meaningful vulnerability that should be considered when doing business with companies that show characteristics of being a shell company. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "presents a meaningful vulnerability that should be considered when doing business with companies"</sub>

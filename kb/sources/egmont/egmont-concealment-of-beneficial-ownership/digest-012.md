## Limited AML/CFT obligations or insufficient awareness and compliance

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Internationally, financial institutions' effective application of risk-based measures has generally increased, raising detection risk for those exploiting them, whereas implementation of AML/CFT obligations for DNFBPs has been slower, with many jurisdictions yet to fully implement Recommendations 22 and 23. (p. 75)[^original]

- **descriptive / finding or result**: FIUs, law enforcement and competent authorities report the primary environmental vulnerability affecting concealment of beneficial ownership is the global lack of regulatory obligations to collect, disclose and make available beneficial ownership information. (p. 75)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the lack of regulatory obligations to collect, disclose, and make available information regarding beneficial ownership"</sub>

## Limited AML/CFT obligations or insufficient awareness and compliance

- **descriptive / finding or result**: A significant Horizontal Study finding is that 17% of responding jurisdictions impose no AML obligations or supervision on professional intermediaries, despite this being a requirement of FATF Recommendations 22, 23 and 28; causes include resistance from sectors (lobbying against or constitutionally challenging laws) or an 'unfinished' aspect of the AML/CFT system. (p. 76)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "17% of jurisdictions that responded do not impose any AML obligations or AML supervision on professional intermediaries whatsoever"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Professionals not subject to AML/CFT obligations are more vulnerable to exploitation owing to lower awareness of ML/TF threats; less than 10% of intermediaries in the cases identified and reported a suspicious matter to a supervisory body, all in countries regulating DNFBPs, suggesting DNFBP supervision effectiveness needs improvement. (p. 76)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "less than 10% of intermediaries involved in these schemes identified and reported a suspicious matter"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In many countries only expressly regulated businesses and professionals may submit suspicious transaction reports, so unregulated professionals cannot voluntarily report to the FIU or self-regulating body (SRB), an additional vulnerability limiting their response to a suspicious request. (p. 76)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the inability for unregulated professionals to voluntarily report a suspicious matter to the FIU"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The vulnerability from reduced AML/CFT obligations is greater for small firms and firms not operating internationally; larger multinational firms are more likely to be attuned to ML vulnerabilities and have robust measures, particularly if regulated in one or more operating countries. (p. 76)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The vulnerability posed by reduced AML/CFT obligations is greater for small professional firms"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Where DNFBPs are covered by AML/CFT law, FIUs and supervisors are concerned about compliance standards and reporting levels that sometimes appear low relative to sector size; Case Study 71 shows systematic non-compliance by a Dutch TCSP exploited by foreign clients for tax evasion. Whether legal professionals' compliance reflects unwillingness or limited understanding has not been assessed. (p. 76)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the level of reporting, which sometimes appears low in comparison to the size and activities of the sector"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 61 defines a self-regulatory body as a body that represents a profession (for example lawyers, notaries, other independent legal professionals or accountants) and is made up of members of that profession; it also plays a role in regulating who is qualified to enter and practise in the profession and performs certain supervisory or monitoring functions. (p. 76)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A self-regulatory body is a body that represents a profession"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Footnote 61 adds that self-regulatory bodies should enforce rules so that high ethical and moral standards are maintained by those practising in the profession. (p. 76)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Such bodies should enforce rules to ensure that high ethical and moral standards are maintained"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In the Dutch cases (71 and 66), professionals managing companies and promoting investment schemes failed to identify indicators of criminality or do sufficient due diligence, not for lack of regulatory obligations but through insufficient awareness of risks and/or inadequate measures to detect high-risk activity; the effectiveness of DNFBP supervision and application of obligations has been a consistent challenge in the current FATF Mutual Evaluation round. (p. 77)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These failures were not due to a lack of regulatory obligations"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 66 (Netherlands): funds from extortion were laundered through legal constructs of parent companies registered in a low-tax jurisdiction with few or no obligations to keep administrative and accounting records, a coded Swiss bank account, and TCSP-managed companies; the public prosecutor said the persons and trust companies should have noticed what was going on but no one flagged concerns. (p. 77)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The suspect used a coded bank account in Switzerland to further conceal the money laundering activity."</sub>

- **descriptive / context**: Environmental vulnerabilities affecting the risk of legal structures and their service providers include jurisdiction-specific vulnerabilities (such as AML/CFT regulations and trade and commercial trends) and vulnerable business practices, including online client interactions. (p. 78)[^original]

## Jurisdictional vulnerabilities

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Availability of beneficial ownership information varies significantly; despite renewed focus by FATF, Egmont Group, OECD Global Forum, G20 and the UK Anti-Corruption Summit, many countries have not taken sufficient steps to implement the FATF Standards, and fourth-round mutual evaluations show most countries assessed had low or moderate effectiveness and technical compliance against key beneficial ownership recommendations, raising the difficulty and cost of due diligence, particularly for small businesses. (p. 78)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "most countries assessed at the time of the drafting of this report had demonstrated low or moderate levels of effectiveness"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Horizontal Study showed that even where intermediaries are subject to AML/CFT requirements, supervisory mechanisms remain weak due to capacity issues and lack of a consistent approach across professions, and enforcement actions are rare. (p. 78)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supervisory mechanisms remain weak due to capacity issues and the lack of a consistent approach"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Beyond regulatory effectiveness, consideration should be given to whether the country where a legal person or arrangement is established, or holds active bank accounts, is a common international trade or financial centre and/or a low-tax jurisdiction. (p. 78)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "consideration should also be given to whether the country in which the legal person or arrangement is established"</sub>

## Trade and Financial Centres

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: A primary benefit of legal persons is disguising transactions as legitimate business and trade; they can facilitate trade-based money laundering (TBML) typologies, including those with no actual movement of goods or purporting to provide or acquire services from other international businesses. Case Study 40 (Israel) shows international companies, including one in a major South-East Asian trading hub, used for TBML via false invoicing. (p. 79)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "legal persons can facilitate trade-based money laundering (TBML) typologies"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Trade and financial centres are loosely defined as any city that is an epicentre of regional or international trade, accommodates regional headquarters of major international businesses, consultancies and/or financial institutions, or hosts a cluster of nationally and internationally significant financial services providers such as banks, investment managers or stock exchanges; criminals often establish legal persons and bank accounts in such cities. (p. 79)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is home to a cluster of national and internationally significant financial services providers"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Establishing legal persons in trade and financial centres serves to legitimise the legal person as a seemingly high-functioning business, legitimise transactions between legal persons as lawful trade, and conceal unlawful transactions behind the vast number and value of genuine transactions on the same channel. (p. 79)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "conceal the unlawful transactions made by, or to, the legal persons behind the vast number and value of genuine transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Legal persons incorporated in global and regional trade and financial centres likely represent a greater vulnerability than those elsewhere; the vulnerability is unique to each country, based on its trade and finance corridors. In Case Study 3 (Australia) the accounting firm used companies in Hong Kong, the US and Canada, major hubs in the Australian context. (p. 79)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These countries represent major trading and finance hubs in the Australian context."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The report lists no highly vulnerable cities or countries; FIUs, competent authorities and financial institutions are best placed to identify high-risk ML corridors specific to their economy and should use this to assess the vulnerability of legal persons along those corridors; major trade and finance centres should be aware of their vulnerabilities as jurisdictions of choice for international criminals. (p. 79)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should use this information to assess the vulnerability posed by legal persons operating or transacting along these corridors"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 3 (Australia, repeated): university managers and construction company directors ran a fraudulent invoice scheme; profits bought racehorses and property; accounting firms sent funds to New Zealand, Canada, Hong Kong and the USA, largely to horse-racing-linked companies, and received similar-value transfers mostly from Hong Kong, leading authorities to suspect a professional money laundering syndicate. (p. 80)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Managers at a university and directors of construction companies were complicit in a fraudulent invoice scheme."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 40 (Israel): suspects used a TCSP to register and operate two international shell companies (A and B) that traded only with each other, creating a false appearance that international trade revenues did not belong to their local Israeli company; Company A sent large funds to local company C as a 'consulted fee'/'service commission', and only this commission, less than half of real income, was reported to Israeli tax authorities. (p. 81)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Only this commission, which was less than half of the real income, was reported to the tax authority in Israel."</sub>

## Low-Tax Jurisdictions

- **descriptive / context**: Many jurisdictions offer favourable tax conditions (very low or nil corporate or income tax, other incentives) to attract foreign investors, characteristics associated with many offshore financial centres (OFCs); research shows even marginal concessions can stimulate investment, and low-tax jurisdictions attract investment both for low local rates and because they facilitate avoiding taxes owed elsewhere. (p. 81)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "very low or even nil corporate or income tax rates, or other tax incentives designed to appeal to foreign investors"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Authorities regularly identify criminals using legal persons and bank accounts in low-tax jurisdictions; over half of the cases involved fund transfers via companies or accounts in low-tax jurisdictions, though many cases referred only to 'offshore jurisdictions' and the prevalence may also reflect selection bias (excerpt ends mid-sentence). (p. 81)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "over half of the cases analysed involved a transfer of funds via companies or accounts held in low-tax jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 67 defines offshore financial centres as jurisdictions whose financial centres contain institutions dealing primarily with non-residents, in foreign currency, on a scale out of proportion to the host economy, where non-resident owned or controlled institutions play a significant role and may gain tax benefits unavailable outside the centre (OECD Glossary on Statistics). (p. 81)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "financial institutions that deal primarily with non-residents, in foreign currency on a scale out of proportion"</sub>

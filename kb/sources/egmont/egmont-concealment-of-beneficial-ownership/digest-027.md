## Covert surveillance

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 21: most law enforcement and intelligence agencies can use covert surveillance, including telecommunication interception and physical surveillance, to identify connections between associates and control over assets or companies. (p. 178)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "including telecommunication interception and physical surveillance"</sub>

## Informants and witnesses

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 22: some intelligence and law enforcement agencies can coerce witnesses to give information or documents. Often this can be used only in limited circumstances and the information may be usable for intelligence purposes only (not evidence), but it can be highly valuable for dissecting complicated corporate structures designed to conceal beneficial ownership and frustrate investigations. (p. 178)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the information gathered from these witnesses can often be used for intelligence purposes only"</sub>

## Search Warrants

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 23: search warrants are a standard but overt and intrusive law enforcement capability that immediately announces interest in a person or company, so they are often used towards the end of an investigation. They are valuable for evidence gathering and for proving beneficial ownership in court but may have limited value in identifying beneficial ownership early on. (p. 178)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they are an overt and intrusive capability that immediately announces law enforcement"</sub>

## Multi-agency task forces

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 24: rarely does a single agency hold all the information and capabilities needed to unravel and prosecute complicated laundering schemes obscuring beneficial ownership; law enforcement, intelligence agencies, taxation authorities, securities regulators and other competent authorities are often all needed. Multi-agency task forces coordinate investigative efforts, share information and reduce duplication, and a standing taskforce allows quick deployment of resources and capabilities against emerging threats and opportunities. (p. 178)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is rare that any single agency has all of the information and capabilities required"</sub>

## Tools to identify potential efforts to obscure beneficial ownership

- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 25: law enforcement and the private sector have identified novel approaches to collecting information relevant to risk indicators. In most cases these tools are not suitable for discovering true beneficial ownership and control, but they may reveal anomalous activities and indices that could help recognise high-risk individuals and companies. (p. 178)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these tools are not suitable to discovering true beneficial ownership and control"</sub>

## IP addresses

- **descriptive / method**: Paragraph 26 (IP addresses): many financial institutions and law enforcement agencies have begun collecting and analysing the IP address of customers involved in a transaction, since most financial transactions are now online. The report says careful analysis could identify control exerted by an unknown third party, control shifting from one person to another, control of a domestic account exerted by a foreign influence, or a person trying to hide their IP via a VPN. (p. 179)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "careful analysis of IP address information could identify situations where control is being exerted by an unknown third party"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Paragraph 27: analysis of IP addresses collected by a financial institution may reveal commonalities and control nexuses where a single IP address is responsible for transaction requests for multiple accounts, customers and beneficial owners. Repeat IP addresses across numerous accounts may indicate a professional nominee, professional intermediary or professional money launderer, and these accounts may deserve close monitoring. (p. 179)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Instances of repeat IP addresses across numerous accounts may be indicative of a professional nominee"</sub>

## Online maps and street-level images

- **descriptive / method**: Paragraph 28 (online maps and street-level images): such images are available for a significant proportion of countries and can be used to verify and analyse customer addresses. Previously service providers and financial institutions were often limited in critically analysing addresses, especially for foreign customers; today a simple search of a company address has significant analytical potential. (p. 179)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Online maps and street-level images (such as those developed by Google and other search engines)"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Paragraph 29: analysing a customer's address location and, where images exist, its street appearance can reveal anomalies indicative of a shell company or an attempt to conceal true identity. The listed anomalies are: location inconsistent with the customer's financial profile; location inconsistent with the company's business profile; physical appearance of the address inconsistent with the size and nature of the company; and the address being a post box. (p. 179)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the physical appearance of the address is inconsistent with the size and nature of the company"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Paragraph 30: addresses that appear anomalous may warrant enhanced due diligence and closer monitoring. (p. 179)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Addresses that appear anomalous may warrant enhanced due diligence and closer monitoring"</sub>

## Media reporting

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 31: a number of cases involved financial institutions and professional intermediaries that identified suspicious transactions through media reporting, which is useful for identifying potential corruption, high-value government contracts and high-profile corporate activities. (p. 179)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "involved financial institutions and professional intermediaries that identified suspicious transactions as a result of media reporting"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 31 continues that media reporting is not an indication of suspicious activity, though it may help identify anomalous or high-risk activities. (p. 180)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "While media reporting is not an indication of suspicious activities"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 32: global journalist consortiums such as the International Consortium of Investigative Journalists have investigated corruption, tax evasion and money laundering. In two key instances (the 2015 Mossack Fonseca leak and the 2017 Appleby leak, per footnote 79) they released leaked documents on law firms setting up complex structures and companies in low-tax jurisdictions for high-wealth individuals. Such leaks are not evidence of criminality or wrong-doing but may indicate risk and may warrant close consideration in risk analysis. (p. 180)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these leaked documents are not evidence of criminality or wrong-doing, they may be indicative of risk"</sub>

## Techniques to identify potential efforts to obscure beneficial ownership

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 34 says a broad range of analytical techniques exist to identify activities and trends indicative of concealed beneficial ownership and of money laundering more broadly. The report does not list them all but includes key techniques identified by FIUs, competent authorities and private sector representatives. (p. 180)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This report will not attempt to list all such techniques"</sub>

## Identifying the beneficial owners of legal arrangements

- **descriptive / method**: Paragraph 35: identifying beneficial ownership of legal arrangements is challenging because of the number of actors who can exercise control or benefit. For a trust, three key questions may help financial institutions and professional intermediaries: who is the real settlor and what is the real source of funds; who are the real beneficiaries, i.e. for whose benefit the trust assets are managed; and what is the trust's governance system and who are the real 'natural persons exercising effective control'. (p. 180)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Who is the real settlor and what is the real source of funds?"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Paragraph 36: seeking copies or extracts of tax or legal advice on forming the trust, or an explanation from current advisers of the purpose behind its formation, will help answer these questions. Where such advice is unavailable, inferences may be drawn from background information, although this can be less reliable. (p. 180)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Seeking copies or extracts of tax or legal advice on the formation of the trust"</sub>

## In the absence of the ability to identify BO, identify senior management personnel

- **descriptive / method**: Paragraph 37: beneficial ownership must involve some level of ultimate control, direct or indirect. Even where the beneficial owner is not visible, the management structure is generally easier to ascertain; analysing directors and senior management may show whether one is the ultimate beneficial owner, while analysing management's activities and financial dealings may reveal a third party exerting control from outside the company. (p. 181)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "While the beneficial owner of a company may not be visible, the management structure is generally easier to ascertain"</sub>

## In the absence of the ability to identify BO, identify individuals with control over transaction accounts/power of attorney

- **descriptive / method**: Paragraph 38: identifying individuals with control over transaction accounts or power of attorney over the company may help identify the beneficial owner, though this is more difficult to discern. Many are legitimately employed in finance and legal areas of larger companies, but those with no apparent connection to the company, or seemingly employed in unrelated areas, could potentially be the beneficial owner. (p. 181)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "those with no apparent connection to the company, or who are seemingly employed in unrelated areas of the company"</sub>

## Search existing records for the same addresses or telephone numbers

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Paragraph 39: numerous professional intermediaries, particularly TCSPs, provide directorship and company management services, and a key indicator is use of a mailbox service for multiple clients. Large numbers of shell companies, particularly with foreign beneficial owners, are therefore registered to the same address and telephone number; identifying commonly used addresses and numbers can reveal companies using a directorship or management service, and may indicate professional nominees and a shell company. (p. 181)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A key indicator of this activity is the use of a mailbox service for multiple clients"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Paragraph 40: companies established and managed by TCSPs often share the same bulk address, and TCSPs often set up their clients' banking relationships at the same financial institutions. Analysis of customer databases by those institutions is likely to identify commonly used addresses and telephone numbers indicative of bulk company incorporation and management, and these customers may warrant enhanced due diligence to ensure beneficial ownership and control details are recorded correctly. (p. 181)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Analysis of customer databases by these financial institutions is likely to identify commonly used addresses and telephone numbers"</sub>

## Meet high-risk clients face-to-face

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 41: one finding of the report is that growing use of internet communications and fewer face-to-face client interactions have worsened the challenges of identifying and proving beneficial ownership and control, because individuals can more easily conceal identity. Despite government and FinTech investment in digital identification (document verification systems, digital identities), lack of face-to-face interaction will continue to be a vulnerability for CDD and KYC processes. (p. 181)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the lack of face-to-face interactions will continue to pose a vulnerability to CDD and KYC processes"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: Paragraph 42 offers as one solution increasing face-to-face interactions with high-risk clients or customers, including via publicly available video-conference facilities. Meeting the client lets the financial institution verify identity against photographic identification and better understand the control the client exerts over the company or assets, and even a brief discussion is likely to reveal indicators of nominee directors and indirect control by a third party. (p. 182)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "One solution is to increase face-to-face interactions with high-risk clients or customers"</sub>

## Analysis of cross-border wire transfers

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 43: regular and proactive analysis of cross-border wire transfers is often instrumental in identifying true ownership and control structures. FIUs that receive cross-border wire transfer reports stress their value in tracing money flows and identifying likely beneficial ownership. Financial institutions have direct and unfettered access to this information and are ideally placed to identify anomalous money flows globally. Indicators of suspicious activity suggesting concealment of beneficial ownership are in Annex E. (p. 182)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial institutions have direct and unfettered access to cross-border wire transfer information"</sub>

## Additional resources

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 44 points readers wanting more examples and ideas on technology to verify beneficial ownership to the Tax Justice Network report 'Technology and Online Beneficial Ownership Registries: Easier to create companies and better at preventing financial crimes' (Knobel, 2017) and the FATF 2014 guidance on Transparency and Beneficial Ownership. (p. 182)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "For more examples of, and ideas on, the use of technology to verify beneficial ownership"</sub>

## ANNEX E. INDICATORS OF CONCEALED BENEFICIAL OWNERSHIP

- **descriptive / context**: Annex E states that 106 case studies were submitted by FATF and Egmont Group members during development of the report; analysis of them, plus discussions with FIUs, competent authorities and the private sector, identified a range of indicators of concealed beneficial ownership. The list is not exhaustive and other indicators may be identified. (p. 183)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "106 case studies were submitted by the FATF and Egmont Group members"</sub>

## Indicators about the client or customer

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Annex E indicator 1 about the client: the client is reluctant to provide personal information. (p. 183)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The client is reluctant to provide personal information"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Annex E indicator 3 (individuals or connected persons), first group: they insist on using an intermediary (professional or informal) in all interactions without sufficient justification; actively avoid personal contact without sufficient justification; are foreign nationals with no significant dealings in the country where they procure professional or financial services; and refuse to co-operate or provide information, data and documents usually required to facilitate a transaction. (p. 183)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "insist on the use of an intermediary (either professional or informal) in all interactions without sufficient justification"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Annex E indicator 3, second group: individuals or connected persons who are PEPs or have familial or professional associations with a PEP; conduct transactions that appear strange given their age (particularly relevant for underage customers); have previously been convicted of fraud, tax evasion or serious crimes; or are under investigation or have known connections with criminals. (p. 183)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "are conducting transactions which appear strange given an individual"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Annex E indicator 3, third group: individuals or connected persons who have previously been prohibited from holding a directorship role in a company or operating a Trust and company service provider (TCSP); and who are signatories to company accounts without sufficient explanation. (p. 183)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "have previously been prohibited from holding a directorship role in a company or operating a Trust"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Annex E indicator 2: the client is reluctant or unable to give an account of any of the following: their business activities and corporate history; who the beneficial owner is; their source of wealth or funds; why they carry out their activities in a particular way; who they are transacting with; and the nature of their dealings with third parties, particularly third parties in foreign jurisdictions. (p. 183)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The client is reluctant or unable to explain:"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Annex E indicator 3, final items: individuals or connected persons who conduct financial activities and transactions inconsistent with their customer profile, and who have declared income inconsistent with their assets, transactions or lifestyle. (p. 184)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "have declared income which is inconsistent with their assets, transactions, or lifestyle"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Annex E indicator 4 (legal persons or legal arrangements), first group: a long period of inactivity after incorporation followed by a sudden unexplained increase in financial activity; describing themselves as a commercial business but not findable on the internet or social business network platforms (such as LinkedIn, XING); and a registered name that does not indicate the company's activity, that indicates activities or services it does not provide, or that appears to mimic other companies, particularly high-profile multinationals. (p. 184)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "describe themselves as a commercial business but cannot be found on the internet or social business network platforms"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Annex E indicator 4, second group: use of an email address with an unusual domain (such as Hotmail, Gmail, Yahoo); registration at an address that does not match the company's profile; at an address that cannot be located on internet mapping services (such as Google Maps); or at an address also listed against numerous other companies or legal arrangements, indicating a mailbox service. (p. 184)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "are registered at an address that is also listed against numerous other companies or legal arrangements"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Annex E indicator 4, final items in this excerpt: an unusually large number of declared beneficiaries and other controlling interests; numerous authorised signatories without sufficient explanation or business justification; incorporation/formation in a jurisdiction considered to pose a high money laundering or terrorism financing risk; incorporation/formation in a low-tax jurisdiction or international trade or finance centre; and regularly sending money to low-tax jurisdictions or international trade or finance centre (the list is cut off here). (p. 184)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "have declared an unusually large number of beneficiaries and other controlling interests"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Annex E indicator 4 (legal persons or legal arrangements), third group: the director or controlling shareholder(s) cannot be located or contacted; the director or controlling shareholder(s) seem to play no active role in the company; or the director, controlling shareholder(s) or beneficial owner(s) are listed against the accounts of other legal persons or arrangements, which is said to point to the use of professional nominees. (p. 184)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "where the director or controlling shareholder(s) do not appear to have an active role in the company"</sub>

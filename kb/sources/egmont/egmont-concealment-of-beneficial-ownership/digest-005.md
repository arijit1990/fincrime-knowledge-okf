## Shell and Shelf Companies

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Only two analysed case studies specifically referred to shelf companies, so their prevalence in schemes obscuring beneficial ownership is unknown; the true figure may be higher because some shelf companies were probably called 'shell companies'. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "only two of the case studies analysed for this report included specific references to the use of shelf companies"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The value of shelf companies likely lies predominantly in the pre-existence of nominee directors and shareholders; many TCSPs offer nominee services to newly established shell companies, making shelf companies less necessary. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "many TCSPs will offer nominee services to newly established shell companies, making shelf companies less necessary"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 19 (Ecuador): public officials, relatives and individuals connected to law firms created shelf companies in several countries to receive bribes; payments came via individuals linked to suppliers to a public oil-sector institution, and the suppliers created companies in Panama, Hong Kong, British Virgin Islands, Bahamas, Uruguay and the US to hide the real beneficiaries. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "created a series of shelf companies in several countries for the purpose of receiving bribe payments"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 26 (Egypt): the accused created six British Virgin Island shell companies, all with a nominee shareholder, and used their bank accounts to launder proceeds of more than EGP 1 billion; the predicate offence was 'illegal earning'. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The six shell companies all had a nominee shareholder."</sub>

## Front company

- **descriptive / definition**: A front company is a fully functioning company with assets, income, expenses and other characteristics of a legitimate business; any functioning company can be one, but the most common form operates in customer service industries (such as a restaurant, night club or salon) because they commonly handle cash. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A “front company” is a fully functioning company, with assets, income, expenses."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Front companies launder proceeds by integrating illegitimate funds with legitimate income, often disguising them as cash sales; the funds are deposited and used by the beneficial owner or false expenses are paid to transfer money to the true beneficial owner. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "often by disguising the illegitimate funds as cash sales made during the course of business"</sub>

## Front company

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Unlike many launderers, criminals using front companies will occasionally pay company tax on the illicit income to further legitimise the wealth. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "will occasionally pay company tax on the illicit income to further legitimise the wealth"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Front companies conceal beneficial ownership of illicit wealth at the placement stage: company records show a transfer as originating from a customer interaction, concealing that the 'customer' is often the business owner or a close associate. Over a quarter of case studies involved front companies. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they also conceal the beneficial ownership of that wealth at the placement stage of the laundering process"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Front companies are not always cash-intensive; in a digitised transnational economy they can be anything expected to generate income from multiple sources, and can be set up to commit fraud (appearing to offer a service they do not provide, to defraud investors and embezzle public funds) or to obscure the beneficial owner within a complex structure. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Front companies are not always cash-intensive businesses."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Financial institutions have identified informal nominees solicited by crime groups to establish front companies to circumvent due diligence, money laundering controls or sanctions: a crime group operating a company has an employee set up an apparently legitimate company in another jurisdiction, which due diligence would not typically link to the original company. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "informal nominees are solicited by crime groups to establish front companies as a means of circumventing due diligence"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Front companies were less prevalent than shell companies in the cases but appear a popular technique; their steady stream of legitimate income conceals the beneficiary of the income, so criminals will continue to exploit them. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "criminals will continue to exploit front companies to conceal beneficial ownership and integrate illicit wealth"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 2 (Australia): a drug syndicate laundered more than AUD 1 million of cannabis proceeds using trust accounts, a front company, high-value goods and real estate, and misused two professional facilitators (an accountant and solicitor). (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An Australian drug syndicate used multiple money laundering methods to launder more than AUD 1 million"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 2 (continued): one of four methods paid illicit wealth to members as apparent wages; members deposited cash proceeds into a new transport company's account, funds moved to a wage processing company which paid members, each an annual wage of around AUD 100 000. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Syndicate members were paid an annual wage of around AUD 100 000."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 99 (United States): US authorities identified front companies concealing ownership of US assets by Bank Melli (previously designated for services to entities in Iran's nuclear and ballistic missile program; also subject to a call for enhanced vigilance in UNSCR 1803); the DOJ obtained forfeiture of assets including a 36-story Manhattan office tower at 650 5th Avenue appraised at over USD 500 million, other properties and several million dollars in cash. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Department of Justice (DOJ) obtained the forfeiture of substantial assets controlled by the Government of Iran."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 99 (continued): the office tower was owned 40% by Bank Melli and 60% by the Alavi Foundation, which provided services to the Iranian government such as transferring funds from the tower to Bank Melli. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "split between Bank Melli (40%) and the Alavi Foundation (60%)"</sub>

## Splitting company incorporation and asset administration over different countries

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The ability of legal persons to establish and administer banking relationships in different countries is a commonly exploited vulnerability; keeping foreign accounts is legitimate, but banks often find robust customer due diligence on foreign companies difficult, and splitting assets and incorporation impedes investigation of the business objective, ownership and control structure, transaction purpose and especially the beneficial owner. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is often difficult for banks to conduct robust customer due diligence on foreign companies"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A large number of cases involved splitting incorporation and asset administration across countries; in most, shell companies were used to open foreign bank accounts, and sometimes several accounts in different countries enabled rapid movement of funds across numerous frontiers, impeding tracing. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In most cases, shell companies were"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 76 (Netherlands): a Dutch-headquartered international company paid corruption funds to a government employee via letter box companies, one registered with the employee as beneficial owner but with nominee shareholders and directors; payments went from a Dutch subsidiary account to Estonia, via a Hong Kong enterprise, to bank accounts in a foreign jurisdiction and then to a Luxembourg account; bribes also went to charities tied to government employees and false invoices were entered in accounting records. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "false invoices were entered in the accounting records"</sub>

## Trusts and other legal arrangements

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Trusts and other arrangements enhance anonymity by adding complexity through separation of legal and beneficial ownership: legal title and control are separated from equitable interests, so different persons may own, benefit from and control the trust depending on trust law and the trust deed; in some countries settlor and beneficiary (sometimes even the trustee) can be the same person. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the legal title and control of an asset are separated from the equitable interests in the asset"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Trusts and other legal arrangements appeared in approximately one-quarter of the analysed cases; most were common law express trusts, with two using a civil law fiducie. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Most of the examples involved common law express trusts, with two making use of a civil law fiducie."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The enhanced anonymity of trusts can benefit criminal operations and challenge financial transparency, but also poses risks to criminals because they must relinquish legal ownership and control to a trustee, who may be a vulnerability if not complicit or if control over the trustee is not guaranteed. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Legal arrangements require the criminal to relinquish legal ownership and control of the asset to a trustee"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Paragraph 75 says trust deeds can contain provisions affecting where ultimate control over trust assets lies, including clauses by which the settlor keeps certain powers such as revoking the trust and having its assets returned (possibly the initial intention of the corrupt individual in the Cayman Islands case referred to as Case Study 14). It names further vulnerable features: directed trust arrangements, general or special powers of appointment that the settlor can exercise, and loans to the trust that are repayable on demand (made by the settlor or others). (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Other vulnerable features include directed trust arrangements, general or special powers of appointment"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Complex structures with multiple trusts appear relatively rare (Case Study 42 is one rare example), whereas a trust interacting with at least one company appears more frequently; almost all cases with a legal arrangement also involved a company or other legal person, suggesting trusts are rarely used in isolation and that trust-only schemes may be harder for authorities to identify. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Almost all of the cases that involved the use of a legal arrangement also involved a company"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Horizontal Study (Questions 2 and 3) shows information on legal arrangements is rarely available or faces significant relevance and accuracy challenges; Case Study 13 (Cayman Islands) illustrates complexity generated through transfers between a company and a trust. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "information on legal arrangements is rarely available, or is subject to significant challenges"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In the cases, legal arrangements rarely held actual proceeds of crime; they added layers of complexity and anonymised transactions. In multi-level structures trusts appear to enter a company's shareholder register in place of the beneficial owner, and approximately half of cases with a legal arrangement also involved shares, proportionally higher than the whole sample. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "legal arrangements were rarely found to hold the actual proceeds of crime"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 2 (Australia) again: the crime syndicate created bank accounts held in trust and investment companies, and instructed an accountant to use cannabis cash proceeds to buy shares in the names of the trust accounts and investment companies, to distance the shares from the beneficial owners. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The purpose of the trust in this arrangement was to further distance the assets (the shares) from the beneficial owners."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Use of trusts is not insignificant though less common than legal persons; the report suggests complexity and expense of establishing and managing a legal arrangement may make them less attractive, or that they may make beneficial owners harder to identify, explaining low prevalence in the sample. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the frequency of the use of trusts and other legal arrangements is not insignificant"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 42 (Italy): Nucleo Polizia of Milan preventively seized EUR 1.3 billion held in the Channel Islands traceable to a single family, concealed in a complex network of trusts hiding beneficiaries of public debt securities and cash; between 1996 and 2006 assets were placed in Dutch and Luxembourg companies and moved to Channel Islands trusts, then legally repatriated via a tax amnesty in December 2009; chartered accountants facilitated the concealment. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The assets were concealed through a complex network of trusts."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 13 (Cayman Islands): Mr. A set up a Cayman revocable trust with himself as settlor and a local TCSP as trustee, plus Company B with the TCSP as registered office. Over two years the TCSP saw numerous transfers from questionable sources into the trust and company, became aware of allegations of an oil and gas contract scam involving foreign government members, and filed an STR. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Mr. A established a Cayman Islands revocable trust, with himself as settlor and a local TCSP acting as trustee."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 13 (continued): analysis of the trust accounts showed outgoing funds to individuals named in media reports on the kickback scandal, and the foreign jurisdiction confirmed Mr. A was being investigated for money laundering and corruption of government officials. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the foreign jurisdiction confirmed that Mr. A was being investigated for money laundering and corruption of government officials"</sub>

## Using individuals and financial instruments to obscure the relationship between the beneficial owner and the asset

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Obscuring the relationship between beneficial owner and asset differs from complex structures in that it creates a false or misleading picture of true ownership and control rather than distance via legal complexity; techniques most often used are formal and informal nominees and professional intermediaries, with bearer shares and declaring numerous beneficiaries identified but less common. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it attempts to create a false or misleading picture of the true ownership and control structure"</sub>

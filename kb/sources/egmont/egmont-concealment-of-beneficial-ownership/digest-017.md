## National supervisor and one or more SRBs

- **descriptive / finding or result**: In 29% of participating jurisdictions (18 of 64), AML supervision of gatekeepers is split between a government agency and one or more self-regulating bodies (SRBs). Under this model, 61% of jurisdictions (11 of 18) report no supervisory or enforcement action (paragraph 32). (p. 107)[^original]

## SRBs only for all gatekeepers

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Where all gatekeeper sectors are supervised by SRBs with no national authority for AML oversight, such jurisdictions make up 13% of the sample; five of the eight (63%) report no supervision or enforcement action (paragraph 33). (p. 107)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Five of the eight jurisdictions in this category (63%) do not report any supervision or enforcement action"</sub>

## Supervision by SRBs

- **descriptive / finding or result**: In the 26 jurisdictions where SRBs supervise AML/CFT obligations, lawyers are supervised only by SRBs in every jurisdiction but one. In 16 of the 26 (64%) no enforcement actions were reported, and seven jurisdictions reported active SRB supervision of lawyers (paragraph 34). (p. 107)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "lawyers are supervised only by SRBs in every jurisdiction but one"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: No discernible pattern could be identified in how SRBs perform supervision, owing to a wide variety of approaches, and without proper assessment it is even harder to say which approach is more effective. Only general observations are offered (paragraph 35). (p. 107)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "no discernible patterns could be identified with regard to how this supervision is performed"</sub>

## Supervision by SRBs

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Observation on SRB supervision: there is no consistent approach when different types of professional intermediaries performing similar functions (e.g. company formation) are supervised by different bodies; the approach often depends on the profession rather than on the operations performed. Forums for co-operation and risk awareness among SRBs and competent authorities (especially FIUs) exist in many jurisdictions but do not seem to produce a coherent supervisory approach. (p. 108)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supervisory approach is often based on what type of profession intermediaries belong to"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Observation on SRB independence: most SRBs, especially those covering lawyers and notaries, are independent and do not seem to be supervised or monitored by a competent authority (as the FATF Glossary definition of 'Supervisors' contemplates), though in some cases competent authorities have a role, e.g. appointing employees. Two jurisdictions place SRBs under direct supervision of competent authorities, in one jurisdiction an SRB is legally a governmental body, and another is creating an umbrella organisation to oversee and facilitate SRB activities. (p. 108)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "do not seem to be subject to supervision/monitoring by a competent authority"</sub>

## Box 8. Switzerland’s national oversight of SRBs

- **descriptive / case example**: Switzerland: the legislator gave SRBs responsibility for AML/CFT supervision and FINMA (national supervisor) supervises implementation. SRBs must be recognised by FINMA, which requires that they issue FINMA-approved regulations specifying the due diligence obligations of affiliates, oversee compliance with those rules, and ensure the persons and bodies they instruct to carry out controls are independent and professionally qualified. If an SRB fails these conditions FINMA can issue a warning and then withdraw recognition. (p. 108)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "SRBs are structures which must be recognised by FINMA"</sub>

## Supervision by SRBs

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Observation on SRB resources (paragraph 36): resources for inspections are limited. Models used: eight SRBs hire independent experts with appropriate professional background who work solely for the SRB; two SRBs rely on peer members' staff to supervise each other; three SRBs outsource inspections to established auditing companies; combinations are possible. (p. 108)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "eight SRBs indicated that they hire independent experts with appropriate professional background"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Seven SRBs take a proactive approach to identifying compliance breaches (during on-site inspections rather than after a complaint or law enforcement investigation), but this appears related to overall obligations rather than AML/CFT or beneficial ownership in particular. (p. 108)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Seven SRBs take proactive approach with regard to identifying breaches of compliance"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Application of a risk-based approach (RBA) to professional intermediaries is not widespread and is not always based on ML/TF risk factors. One jurisdiction indicated that all lawyers and notaries are inspected annually and auditors at least every 6 years. (p. 108)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "One jurisdiction indicated that all lawyers and notaries undergo an inspection on an annual basis"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Supervisory actions by SRBs are very rare, although most SRBs have appropriate tools available (warnings, monetary penalties, disqualifications). (p. 109)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisory actions are very rare (as highlighted below), although most SRBs have the appropriate tools"</sub>

## Question 6: Cases of supervisory and enforcement actions.

- **descriptive / context**: Question 6 asked whether beneficial ownership obligations are enforced by administrative supervisory action or by law enforcement authorities, hoping to identify best practices; the information provided was not sufficient for that, though some emerging issues may be considered for further targeted information gathering (paragraph 37). Figure 2 (Enforcement Models) has only labels legible in the extracted text: Administrative Proceedings Only, Law Enforcement Proceedings Only, Administrative and Law Enforcement Proceedings, No Enforcement Action Reported; no values are legible. (p. 109)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "but the information provided is not sufficient for that purpose"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: No supervisory or enforcement actions were reported in 56% of responding jurisdictions; in three of these it is attributed to newly enacted legislation not yet implemented. Some jurisdictions reported AML enforcement but none specific to beneficial ownership obligations. The 17% of respondents with no AML obligations or supervision for gatekeepers could have no enforcement to report, and others may have found the questionnaire format hard for giving meaningful information (paragraph 38). (p. 109)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "No supervisory or enforcement actions were reported in 56% of responding jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Among responses that gave enforcement information, the most common is administrative enforcement by AML supervisors: 18 of the 28 jurisdictions reporting enforcement action (64%) rely on supervisors to enforce beneficial ownership requirements. Many responses included sanitized case studies (paragraph 39). (p. 109)[^original]

## Box 10. Jersey’s FSC and Registry

- **descriptive / case example**: Jersey: the Jersey Financial Services Commission (JFSC) reports enforcement tools including formal remediation plans with regular monitoring and reporting by the TCSP; directions to safeguard assets, prevent taking on new business or transferring existing business; and appointing independent co-signatories to review and approve certain activities and transactions. It also uses supervisory powers for public statements and to ban individuals from working in the financial services industry. (p. 110)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "formal remediation plans with regular monitoring and reporting by the TCSP"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Jersey: the Companies Registry will not incorporate or register an entity lacking sufficient information; applications are placed on hold until information is provided. Failure to provide information is noted and shared by the Companies Registry with the supervision and enforcement units of the JFSC, so supervisor and Registry perform complementary functions. (p. 110)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Companies Registry will not incorporate or register an entity if it does not have sufficient information"</sub>

## Question 6: Cases of supervisory and enforcement actions.

- **descriptive / finding or result**: In ten of the 28 jurisdictions reporting enforcement action, law enforcement proceedings may be used to enforce beneficial ownership requirements. In four of the ten they are the only available remedy; in the other six, authorities may use administrative or law enforcement proceedings (paragraph 40). (p. 110)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In four of the ten jurisdictions, law enforcement proceedings are the only available remedy"</sub>

## Box 11. Liechtenstein and Croatia

- **descriptive / case example**: Liechtenstein and Croatia: the AML supervisor starts legal proceedings when weaknesses are found in compliance inspections. In Liechtenstein the supervisor found weaknesses in establishing and corroborating the beneficial owner's source of wealth and the source of funds held by the legal person or arrangement and referred the matter to the courts, which in some instances fined the responsible senior management member. In Croatia the supervisor filed misdemeanour proceedings for violation of beneficial ownership, CDD and risk assessment obligations. (p. 110)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the AML supervisor initiates legal proceedings when weaknesses are identified during compliance inspections"</sub>

## Box 12. Latvia

- **descriptive / case example**: Latvia: from 2013 to 2015 five criminal cases were initiated for non-provision of information and provision of false information regarding ownership of resources and the true beneficiary. Two were submitted for prosecution and one is under review by the court; no outcome information was given. (p. 111)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "five criminal cases were initiated on grounds of non-provision of information"</sub>

## Box 13. Spain and the US

- **descriptive / case example**: Spain and the US described cases where police followed illicit financial flows to gatekeepers complicit in setting up networks of shell companies to launder proceeds of drug trafficking, political corruption, fraud and tax evasion. (p. 111)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "police followed illicit financial flows to gatekeepers who were complicit in setting up networks of shell companies"</sub>

## Question 6: Cases of supervisory and enforcement actions.

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Although the sample is small, a pattern appears: some jurisdictions have AML supervisors who start court proceedings to penalise weaknesses found in compliance inspections, while others, like Spain and the US, do not use criminal proceedings to enforce preventative measures such as beneficial ownership obligations; legal proceedings there are limited to complicit actors actively engaged in money laundering (paragraph 41). (p. 111)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "do not use criminal proceedings to enforce preventative measures like beneficial ownership obligations"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: Paragraph 42 says some enforcement approaches may potentially help address challenges in preventing misuse of legal persons, and that they could be reviewed in more depth as a basis for the final Horizontal Study: exercise of administrative supervisory powers and its impact on compliance; Jersey's use of both the AML supervisor and the Companies Registry to enforce beneficial ownership obligations; and the role of law enforcement in enforcing preventative measures. (p. 111)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These could be reviewed in more depth, as a basis for more detailed description and analysis"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 42 says the lack of reported information on enforcement raises questions that should also be considered for further information gathering. (p. 111)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The lack of reported information on enforcement gives rise to questions that should also be considered"</sub>

## ANNEX C. CASE SUMMARIES

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 1 (Argentina): Company G, 95% owned by Mr. A and 5% by Mr. B, bought a power generator from Company K, owned by Company R in the Cayman Islands. Company R was linked to Panamanian Foundation P, whose beneficiaries were Mr. A and his spouse. Company G leased the generator to Company E and received amounts cleared by Company L; funds drawn on Company K's bank account, and Company G paid K to settle a debt, with funds credited to accounts of Companies S, T and R. The simulated commercial operations put funds of dubious origin into the financial system and hid the true beneficiary. (p. 112)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The simulation of commercial operations introduced funds of dubious origin to the financial system"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Case Study 1 (Argentina) indicators: declared income inconsistent with assets, transactions or lifestyle; entity formed in a low-tax jurisdiction or international trade/financial centre; regularly sends money to such jurisdictions or centres; transacts with international companies without sufficient corporate or trade justification; activities and transactions inconsistent with corporate profile; false invoices for services not carried out; falsified paper trail; family members with no role in running the business listed as beneficial owners; complex structures that do not legitimately require that complexity or make commercial sense; finance from a lender other than a known credit institution with no logical explanation or commercial justification; loans from private third parties without supporting loan agreements, collateral or regular interest repayments; transaction between connected parties without apparent business or trade rationale; complicated transaction routings without sufficient explanation or trade records; funds sent to or received from a foreign country with no apparent connection to the client; funds sent to or received from a low-tax jurisdiction or international trade/finance centre. (p. 112)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Declared income which is inconsistent with their assets, transactions or lifestyle"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 2 (Australia): a drug syndicate laundered more than AUD1 million of cannabis proceeds using trust accounts, a front company, high-value goods and real estate, and misused two professional facilitators (an accountant and a solicitor). It bought cannabis in bulk in one state and sold it in another, and set up an apparent transport company, buying a truck and renting a warehouse in the company's name to traffic the cannabis interstate. (p. 113)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An Australian drug syndicate used multiple money laundering methods to launder more than AUD1 million"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Case Study 2 (Australia) indicators: no real business activities; exclusively facilitates transit transactions and does not appear to generate wealth or income; transaction from a business account involving a large sum of cash (deposit or withdrawal) that is anomalous or inconsistent with the company's profile; professional intermediary involved without due cause or apparent justification; multiple large cash payments used to pay down a loan or mortgage. (p. 113)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Exclusively facilitates transit transactions and does not appear to generate wealth or income"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 3 (Australia): university managers and directors of construction companies were complicit in a fraudulent invoice scheme; managers approved inflated maintenance invoices and invoices for work never done. Profits bought racehorses and property; managers were repaid with kickbacks or direct shares in racehorses. Accounting firms made international transfers for the suspects to many countries, including New Zealand, Canada, Hong Kong and the US, largely to horse-racing-linked companies, and received transfers of similar value from overseas entities, mostly from Hong Kong. Authorities suspected the accounting firms were laundering as part of a professional money laundering syndicate. (p. 113)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Managers at a university and directors of construction companies were complicit in a fraudulent invoice scheme"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Case Study 3 (Australia) indicators as far as this excerpt goes: financial activities and transactions inconsistent with the customer profile; declared income inconsistent with assets, transactions or lifestyle (the list continues in the next excerpt). (p. 113)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial activities and transactions inconsistent with their customer profile"</sub>

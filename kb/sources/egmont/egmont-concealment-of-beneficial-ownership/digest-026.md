- **descriptive / case example**: In Case Study 101, loan applications contained fraudulent information on the borrower's or purchaser's financial position, fraudulent appraisals overstating collateral value and other documents with material misrepresentations. The subject 'sold' commercial property held by one entity he controlled to another entity he controlled at highly elevated prices, financed through fraudulent loan applications and documents. ## Case Study 103 – United States

## OVERVIEW

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 2 states that concealing beneficial ownership information is the cornerstone of many money laundering and terrorism financing schemes, and that proving beneficial ownership is one of the greatest challenges for financial institutions and competent authorities. Some simple tools exist to help identify high-risk or suspicious customers and activities, outlined in this Annex. (p. 174)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "proving beneficial ownership presents one of the greatest challenges for financial institutions and competent authorities"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 3 notes that the FATF 2014 guidance paper on Transparency and Beneficial Ownership outlined mechanisms and sources for obtaining beneficial ownership information on legal persons: company registries, financial institutions, DNFBPs, the legal person itself, and other national authorities such as tax authorities or stock exchange commissions. (p. 174)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "company registries, financial institutions, DNFBPs, the legal person itself, and other national authorities"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 3 adds that the FATF guidance focuses on implementing policy initiatives to improve beneficial ownership transparency rather than investigative techniques, and may therefore be of limited value to financial institutions and competent authorities. (p. 174)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may therefore be of limited value to financial institutions and competent authorities"</sub>

## SOURCE OF INFORMATION TO ASSIST IN THE IDENTIFICATION OF BENEFICIAL OWNERSHIP

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 5: banks were the most common source of information that competent authorities used to identify beneficial ownership, being involved in over half of the investigations analysed; financial institutions are a key information source for FIUs and competent authorities. (p. 174)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Banks were the most common source of information used by competent authorities to identify beneficial ownership"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 5 also says financial institutions have limited ability to leverage information held by other financial institutions, and bank-held information depends on the quality of information provided by the client. This matters particularly for sharing suspicions and risk profiles between banks or within multi-national banks; further global work is under way to improve private-private and public-private information sharing. (p. 175)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is limited ability for financial institutions to leverage the information held by other financial institutions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 6 (professional intermediaries): in approximately one-third of cases information was provided by DNFBPs. Their information can be extensive, but in countries where DNFBPs are not obliged to conduct CDD it may not be reliable, and legal professional privilege (LPP) and client confidentiality can inhibit efforts to obtain information from intermediaries. (p. 175)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In approximately one-third of cases, information was provided by DNFBPs"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 7 (companies and company registries): beneficial ownership information held by companies and in company registries was used in only one-quarter of cases. In many cases the registries were general property or corporate registries rather than beneficial ownership registries, though many sample cases predate global work in this area. (p. 175)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "were used in only one-quarter of cases"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 8 (traditional law enforcement techniques): in one-quarter of cases beneficial ownership was not known, its source was not disclosed, or it was not discovered using information held under Recommendations 24 and 25. Often in these cases it was determined through surveillance. (p. 175)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "beneficial ownership was determined through surveillance"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 6: in approximately one-third of cases DNFBPs provided information. Information held by professional intermediaries can be extensive, but where DNFBPs are not obliged to conduct CDD the information may not be reliable, and LPP and client confidentiality can inhibit efforts to obtain it. The page also carries a Figure 3 titled "Sources of beneficial ownership information", whose extracted text shows only the category labels "Professional intermediaries" and "Companies and company registries" and axis ticks from 0% to 60%, without bar values. (p. 175)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Information held by professional intermediaries can be extensive; however, in countries where DNFBPs"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 9: the case study analysis showed that competent authorities and law enforcement can more easily obtain accurate beneficial ownership information from financial institutions than from DNFBPs. Banks appeared in 90% of cases and were a source of beneficial ownership in over half of them. Some type of DNFBP appeared in 76% of cases, but only in one third did DNFBPs act as a source of beneficial ownership information for authorities, which the report says may be due to complicity, legal professional privilege, or a lack of implementation of beneficial ownership record-keeping requirements in those sectors. (p. 175)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Banks featured in 90% of cases, and were a source of beneficial ownership in over half the case studies"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 10 introduces further sources of beneficial ownership information: registers of beneficial ownership, shareholder registers, commercial databases and professional nominees (paragraphs 11-14). (p. 176)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Other sources of beneficial ownership include:"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 11 (registers of beneficial ownership): after the 2016 Anti-Corruption Summit in London, many countries implemented or began implementing such registers, which contain useful information for determining true beneficial ownership and control where in place. (p. 176)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "particularly after the Anti-Corruption Summit held in London in 2016"</sub>
- **expected / limitation**: Paragraph 11 says care should be taken to analyse the veracity of information on beneficial ownership registers, as it is often self-reported and rarely vetted by a central administrating body. (p. 176)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "care should be taken to analyse the veracity of the information held on registers of beneficial ownership"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 12 (shareholder register): particularly in jurisdictions requiring companies to actively collect such information up front, shareholder registers can contain enough information to identify controlling interests. Many criminals seek to limit exposure by concealing share ownership, in which case the register may show other controlling persons (natural or legal) who may act on behalf of, or be controlled by, the beneficial owner. (p. 176)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "many criminals will seek to limit their exposure by concealing their share ownership"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 13 (commercial databases): many commercial databases are available to law enforcement for developing investigations and to financial institutions for identifying risk, giving a quick way to obtain varied information and leads. A lack of information on commercial databases can indicate use of a shell or shelf corporation, and together with other techniques can help unravel an entity's legal arrangement. (p. 176)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A lack of information on commercial databases can be an indicator of the use of a shell"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 14 (professional nominees): some countries require domestic legal persons to keep a physical presence there and sometimes a domestic national as director or controlling shareholder; many professional intermediaries, particularly TCSPs, offer nominee directorship and company management services to foreign clients to meet this. These nominees often keep customer records which may not prove true beneficial ownership and control but help trace and unravel the wider control structure. (p. 176)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Many professional intermediaries, particularly TCSPs, offer nominee directorship and company management services to foreign clients"</sub>

## MECHANISMS AVAILABLE TO COMPETENT AUTHORITIES

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 15 says the reliability of some beneficial ownership information sources is often questionable, so to fully unravel complicated ownership structures and prove ownership and control (and thereby criminality where relevant), law enforcement and competent authorities require access to a broader range of intelligence and evidence collection capabilities; key capabilities are outlined below. (p. 176)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the reliability of some of these sources is often questionable"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 16 (mutual legal assistance): MLA is the cornerstone of most major investigations involving transnational corporate structures or international financial flows. However, practitioners report that delays in MLA requests are one of the key inhibitors, so it is not necessarily a quick or easy solution to unravelling opaque transnational ownership structures, though the information is often invaluable. (p. 176)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Mutual legal assistance is the cornerstone of most major investigations that involve a transnational corporate structure"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 17 (intelligence disclosures and sharing): besides MLA, which is often used for evidentiary exchange, FIUs and competent authorities regularly exchange information with international partners for intelligence purposes only, spontaneously or on request, which can greatly help FIUs understand the ownership and control of complex international structures or their financial activities. (p. 177)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIUs and competent authorities will regularly exchange information with international partners for intelligence purposes only"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 18 (taxation databases): comparing previous tax assessments with bank statements, financial transactions, assets and an individual's lifestyle can reveal anomalous financial activity, and further investigation often uncovers dubious control structures or corporate dealings designed to conceal beneficial ownership. (p. 177)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "By comparing previous tax assessments to bank statements, financial transactions, assets, and the lifestyle of an individual"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 19 (asset disclosure databases): many countries require public officials to disclose assets on publicly accessible databases, which help gauge officials' wealth and identify anomalous activity. Absence from the registry of an asset clearly controlled by the official, their family or their corporate interests may indicate efforts to conceal ownership. (p. 177)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Many countries require public officials to disclose their assets on publicly accessible databases"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 20 (subpoenas): subpoenas are often coercive and generally used to compel the recipient to provide information, but can also offer the recipient protections and indemnities. For this reason they are often used where a competent authority and a financial institution are collaborating on an investigation, even though the institution is a willing party. (p. 177)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "subpoenas are often used in situations where a competent authority and financial institution are working collaboratively"</sub>

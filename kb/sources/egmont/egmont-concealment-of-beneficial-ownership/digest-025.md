## Case Study 89 – Serbia

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 89 (Serbia): four transfers totalling about EUR 200 000 went from the account of agricultural cooperative 'U.B.' to legal person 'P.I.P.H'. EUR 178 630 of it was converted to foreign currency straight after deposit and sent to Delaware Company M, whose account was at a bank in Cyprus. The stated purpose was payment for trade in goods. (p. 163)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Funds in foreign currency totalling EUR 178 630 were purchased from this money immediately after it was deposited"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In Case Study 89, 'P.I.P.H' also transferred EUR 75 175 to Delaware Company S, which held an account with a bank in a foreign country, again stated as payment for trade in goods. Investigation established black market trade. The funds went to accounts of six Serbian legal persons suspected to be front companies, then to legal persons abroad, and then to accounts of numerous Chinese citizens assumed to be the real beneficial owners of the goods sold in Serbia. (p. 163)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Investigation established that this was the case of black market trade"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Indicators listed for Case Study 89: legal person or arrangement formed in a low-tax jurisdiction or international trade or financial centre; a corporation with a bank balance close to zero despite frequent incoming and outgoing transactions; questionable connections between parties that the client cannot sufficiently explain; transaction involving transfer of shares in an off-market sale; and funds sent to or received from a foreign country with no apparent connection to the client. (p. 163)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Corporation maintains a bank balance of close to zero, despite frequent incoming and outgoing transactions"</sub>

## Case Study 90 – Serbia

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 90 (Serbia): an organized crime group devised a scheme using Serbian banks to legalize drug trafficking proceeds by purchasing a company's shares. Transactions were structured to avoid reporting to the FIU: the group found 42 individuals who agreed to pay deposits into their own accounts, each below the EUR 15 000 threshold, declaring them as savings. (p. 163)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The organized crime group found 42 individuals, who agreed to pay deposits into their own accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In Case Study 90, the 42 persons later stated that they agreed to let their money be used to acquire a company providing hospitality services. At the same time the group took over profitable, well-performing Serbian private companies with large capital turnover through accounts, whose owners were willing to sell. (p. 163)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "took over profitable private companies in Serbia, with large capital turnover through accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Indicators listed for Case Study 90: persons under investigation or with known connections with criminals; falsified records or counterfeited documentation; finance provided by a lender (natural or legal person) other than a known credit institution with no logical explanation or commercial justification; and purchase of high-value goods in cash. (p. 164)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Finance is provided by a lender, including either a natural or a legal person, other than a known credit institution"</sub>

## Case Study 91 – Slovenia

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 91 (Slovenia): EUR 4 million was moved from a Slovenian company to a Liechtenstein TCSP's account at a Liechtenstein bank under the label 'construction consulting'. MLA was used to identify the TCSP's beneficial owner, and the FIU found that another Liechtenstein TCSP with the same trustee had opened an account at a Slovenian bank, with the trustee declaring himself beneficial owner. (p. 164)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "MLA was used to identify the beneficial owner of TCSP"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In Case Study 91, a bank statement let authorities identify the beneficial owner as named by the trustee when the account was opened. A deal was made with three suspects, authorities retrieved the embezzled assets and levied a penalty of more than EUR 1 million. (p. 164)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A deal was made with three suspects and authorities retrieved the embezzled assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Indicators listed for Case Study 91: director, controlling shareholder(s) and/or beneficial owner(s) listed against the accounts of other legal persons or arrangements; legal person or arrangement formed in a low-tax jurisdiction or international trade or financial centre; and questionable connections between the parties that the client cannot sufficiently explain. (p. 164)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Director, controlling shareholder(s) and/or beneficial owner(s) are listed against the accounts of other legal persons or arrangements"</sub>

## Case Study 92 – Switzerland

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 92 (Switzerland): a lawyer already convicted of document falsification and misappropriation hid stolen bearer shares in accounts opened in the names of offshore companies. The bearer shares were sold, registered shares of the same company were bought with the proceeds and moved to accounts in other jurisdictions. (p. 164)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "hid stolen bearer shares in accounts opened in the name of offshore companies"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In Case Study 92, effective domestic and international co-operation led to the suspect's arrest and extradition to Switzerland, where he is in prison, and assets of more than CHE 50 million (Swiss Francs) were blocked in all five countries. (p. 164)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the suspect was arrested and extradited to Switzerland and is now in prison"</sub>

## Case Study 93 – Switzerland

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 93 (Switzerland): an operational coal mining company paid EUR 800 million over four years to its owner, a Dutch NV. A Swiss financial intermediary learned of an ongoing prosecution of the Dutch NV and its owner in a third country and filed an STR for misappropriation of funds. Its documentation showed the NV was owned by Mr. A, a citizen of another European country. (p. 165)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An operational coal mining company paid out EUR 800 million to their owner, a Dutch NV"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In Case Study 93, over 10 years CHF 3.5 billion moved through a large, complicated structure of 32 companies in different countries including the British Virgin Islands and the Netherlands. The Swiss intermediary's documentation identified Mr. A as beneficial owner of almost all the companies. (p. 165)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "CHF 3.5 billion was transferred through a large and complicated structure of 32 companies in different countries"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Indicators listed for Case Study 93: foreign nationals with no significant dealings in the country where they obtain professional or financial services; under investigation or known connections with criminals; legal person or arrangement formed in a low-tax jurisdiction or international trade or finance centre; regularly sending money to such jurisdictions or centres; transactions with international companies without sufficient corporate or trade justification; complex corporate structures with no legitimate need for that complexity or commercial sense; questionable connections between parties that the client cannot sufficiently explain; two legal persons with similar or identical directors, shareholders or beneficial owners; complicated transaction routings without sufficient explanation or trade records; and funds sent to or received from a low-tax jurisdiction or international trade or finance centre. (p. 165)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Transaction involves two legal persons with similar or identical directors, shareholders, or beneficial owners"</sub>

## Case Study 94 – Switzerland

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 94 (Switzerland): a Swiss financial intermediary filed a SAR after Company B, a wholly owned subsidiary of C Holding, deposited USD 2 million into the account of Company A. Company A's beneficial owner, Mr. X, justified the funds as payment for services under a contract between A and B, described as providing business contacts, acquiring potential clients and negotiating terms and conditions. (p. 165)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A Swiss financial intermediary filed a SAR after a deposit of USD 2 million was made"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In Case Study 94, shortly after the deposit two transfers of USD 1 million each went to two other companies beneficially owned by Mr. X and Mr. Y, both high-ranking executives of Dutch company C Holding. The Dutch company's annual report gave no information on compensation to them, so the intermediary suspected money laundering and dishonest business management to the disadvantage of C Holding's shareholders. (p. 166)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The annual report of the Dutch company did not include any information about compensation to Mr. X and Mr. Y"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Indicators listed for Case Study 94: questionable connections between the parties that the client cannot sufficiently explain; two legal persons with similar or identical directors, shareholders or beneficial owners; and complicated transaction routings without sufficient explanation or trade records. (p. 166)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Transaction involves complicated transaction routings without sufficient explanation or trade records"</sub>

## Case Study 95 – Trinidad and Tobago

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 95 (Trinidad and Tobago): a US citizen ('Blackjack') built a complex scheme, involving gatekeepers, multiple individuals and legal structures and money remitters, to avoid tax on income from a Trinidad and Tobago Private Members' Club (similar to a casino). He earned millions of dollars over 2009-2011. (p. 166)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The scheme included the involvement of gatekeepers, multiple individuals and legal structures and use of money remitters"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In Case Study 95, Blackjack concealed income and assets from the IRS by: depositing personal income into unreported Trinidad and Tobago bank accounts; receiving casino income in US accounts in the names of his New Jersey business entities; using those entities to pay personal expenses; transferring casino income directly to US vendors for personal expenses; and directing casino employees to wire cash to individuals in New Jersey who collected it for him. (p. 166)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "using unreported bank accounts in Trinidad and Tobago to deposit personal income"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Indicators listed for Case Study 95: activities and transactions inconsistent with the corporate profile; focus on aggressive tax minimisation strategies; correct documents not filed with the tax authority; questionable connections between parties the client cannot sufficiently explain; transactions from a business account that appear to fund personal purchases (assets or recreational activities) inconsistent with the company's profile; and funds sent to or received from a jurisdiction considered high money laundering or terrorism financing risk. (p. 166)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Focused on aggressive tax minimisation strategies"</sub>

## Case Study 96 – Turkey

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 96 (Turkey): fuel from fuel smuggling was sold through petrol stations controlled by organised crime. Person A, the beneficial owner and leader of the organisation, disguised his ownership by transferring control of the stations to close associates and by using cash transactions and straw men. (p. 167)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "disguised his ownership by transferring control of the petrol stations to close associates"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Indicators listed for Case Study 96: client reluctant or unable to explain the identity of the beneficial owner; director or controlling shareholder(s) cannot be located or contacted; bank balance close to zero despite frequent incoming and outgoing transactions; falsified paper trail; and nominee owners and directors including informal nominees such as children, spouses, relatives or associates not apparently involved in running the enterprise. (p. 167)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Client is reluctant or unable to explain the identity of the beneficial owner"</sub>

## Case Study 97 – Turkey

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 97 (Turkey): a group ran websites offering illegal internet betting and hid their identity by using natural persons and shell companies to open bank accounts and withdraw or transfer deposited funds. The natural persons were aged around 30, not registered as taxpayers, without social security records, living in different cities and generally unemployed, housewives or minimum wage workers; they were paid for the use of their accounts. (p. 167)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The straw men are paid a certain amount of money for the use of their accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In Case Study 97, intermediary accounts were changed constantly. Sums collected were withdrawn in cash from banks or ATMs, transferred to accounts of persons or companies set up for the purpose, or sent to an offshore corporation. (p. 167)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The intermediary accounts are changed constantly"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Indicators listed for Case Study 97: activities and transactions inconsistent with the customer profile; correct documents not filed with the tax authority; nominee owners and directors including informal nominees such as children, spouses, relatives or associates not apparently involved in running the enterprise; and a business-account transaction involving a large cash deposit or withdrawal that is anomalous or inconsistent with the company's profile. (p. 167)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Transaction is executed from a business account and involves a large sum of cash, either as a deposit or withdrawal"</sub>

## Case Study 98 – Ukraine

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 98 (Ukraine): former high-ranking Ukrainian officials laundered money through Ukrainian and foreign banks. Non-resident companies, mostly registered in Panama, Cyprus, BVI, UK and Belize and linked by constituent-officials and business relations, invested considerable funds in Ukraine by buying internal government bonds, placing funds in deposit accounts and contributing to the authorised capital of Ukrainian enterprises. (p. 168)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A number of non- resident companies (mostly registered in Panama, Cyprus, BVI, UK and Belize)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In Case Study 98, analysis of the IP addresses used to access the business accounts showed that all the investments were managed from one management centre. (p. 168)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "all the investments were managed from one management centre"</sub>

## Case Study 99 – United States

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 99 (United States): US authorities identified front companies used to conceal ownership of certain US assets by Bank Melli, previously designated by the US for providing financial services to entities involved in Iran's nuclear and ballistic missile program and also subject to a call for enhanced vigilance in UNSCR 1803. (p. 168)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "US authorities identified front companies used to conceal the ownership of certain US assets by Bank Melli"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In Case Study 99, the DOJ obtained forfeiture of substantial assets controlled by the Government of Iran, including a 36-story Manhattan office tower at 650 5th Avenue appraised at more than USD 500 million, other properties and several million dollars in cash. The tower was owned 40% by Bank Melli and 60% by the Alavi Foundation, which provided services to the Iranian government such as transferring funds from the tower to Bank Melli. (p. 168)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The ownership the office tower was split between Bank Melli (40%) and the Alavi Foundation (60%)"</sub>

## Case Study 100 – United States

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 100 (United States): an individual ran a loan-fraud pyramid scheme to falsely inflate his company's sales and revenues, with his company as a front and his wife and son involved. The defendants created numerous entities (trusts, corporations/LLCs) to open bank accounts, manage illicit funds and conceal ownership, with an attorney's help, and diverted loans to private benefit including gems and jewellery. (p. 169)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "organised a loan-fraud pyramid scheme to falsely inflate the sales and revenues of his company"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In Case Study 100, the attorney helped sell the jewellery (a trust asset), and the address of the attorney, then deceased, was used to move money from two different accounts. (p. 169)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The address of the attorney (then deceased) was used to move money from two different accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In Case Study 100, investigators obtained legitimate financial records from third parties by subpoena because the organisation's own corporate records were fabricated. Assets were identified by interviewing third parties about true ownership, and a tax return preparer was also interviewed. Standard financial investigative techniques identified several trusts/trustees and legal persons. (p. 169)[^original]

## Directorship, trustee, virtual office, and mailbox services

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Directorship, virtual office and mailbox services (offered by many providers, especially TCSPs) let a legal person keep a physical footprint in a country and distance it from the beneficial owner's other assets, so they are vulnerable to exploitation to disguise true controllers; nominee directors and virtual offices are common in complex structures tied to ML, tax evasion and investment fraud. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Nominee directors and virtual offices are common features in many complex legal structures"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Approximately half of the professional intermediaries in the cases provided directorship services; TCSPs were the large majority and were often assessed as serving other professional intermediaries on behalf of third-party clients. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "approximately half of the professional intermediaries provided directorship services to their clients"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Nominee directors can be formal or informal; criminals recruit people with no criminal history or who agree to have their details recorded, and identity theft for nominee director roles has been identified, but this is risky for criminal groups so professional providers are an attractive low-risk alternative. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "criminals have been known to recruit people with no criminal history to perform these roles"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Directorship and virtual office providers may unwittingly facilitate ML and deal in proceeds and instruments of crime; even when passive (often the case) the nominee director risks prosecution or penalty for the entity's crimes, and a majority of providers of directorship services in the cases were assessed as unwittingly involved. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the nominee director is at risk of prosecution or other penalty as a result of crimes"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Some countries require legal persons to keep an active presence (a resident director, a physical presence, or both), making providers there more vulnerable to overseas clients; a large majority of directorship providers in the cases served clients overseas. Case Study 78 shows a foreign client of a TCSP appointing a domestic nominee director who knew little of the companies' activities. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A large majority of professional intermediaries who provided directorship services in the assessed cases"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Some providers offer trustee services for trusts under domestic law; in some countries professional rules prohibit legal professionals from acting as trustee, limiting their role to advising on the underlying contract. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "professional rules prohibit legal professionals from acting as a trustee"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In most countries trustees need not register the existence or beneficiary of a trust, and in others they are expressly prohibited by law from doing so; trustees must act in beneficiaries' interests and consider them over their own. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In most countries trustees are not required to register the existence of, or beneficiary of, a trust arrangement"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Providers of trustee services risk becoming the effective legal owner of criminal assets and dealing with proceeds of crime; the report says only strict due diligence on the settlor, the beneficiary and the asset in trust can help professionals avoid this. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Only strict due diligence measures, for the settlor, the beneficiary, and the asset in trust"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 78 (New Zealand): a New Zealand law firm was linked to clients implicated, arrested or convicted of offences including embezzlement, bribery, corruption, tax evasion and money laundering; it used its New Zealand tax, trust and company law knowledge for overseas clients and routinely used employees as nominee directors and shareholders, keeping beneficial owners (sometimes offenders and associates) unnamed. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "routinely used its employees as nominee directors and shareholders"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 78 (continued): chains of companies (one the shareholder of another) added complexity, sometimes a New Zealand shell company acted as trustee, and the companies were usually shell companies with nominee directors, shareholders and addresses; partnerships and trusts formed complex structures that can hide wealth. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "often a chain of companies was established, with one company the shareholder of another"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 78 (continued): the firm's business associates abroad added entities to the structures, increasing complexity and reducing detection ability, leaving a convoluted audit trail; strong indications criminals had structures set up and used them to hide assets. A NZ-based employee was named director to meet the resident-director requirement, and the beneficial owner was not identified in every instance. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the beneficial owner of the company was not identified in every instance"</sub>

## Facilitating transactions through trust accounts or client accounts

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Law firms and larger accounting firms often operate trust accounts for client transactions, escrow or fees; in most countries these are highly regulated against misappropriation but oversight often falls short of AML/CFT considerations, so professional trust accounts remain a global money laundering vulnerability. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this oversight often falls short of AML/CFT considerations"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Professionals facilitating client transactions, including cash, attract criminals seeking to obscure the beneficiary because it puts the burden of integrating cash into the regulated sector on the legal professional via the trust or client account, with a dual effect: leveraging the profession's credibility to reduce suspicion of cash deposits, and distancing the client and associates from financial sector AML/CFT controls. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "distancing the client and any associates or third parties from the AML/CFT controls of the financial sector"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: An intermediary in a transaction can disrupt a chain of transactions and obscure the relationship between parties, making the beneficial owner of funds in trust or client accounts hard to ascertain, especially with clustering/structuring or protracted timing; the vulnerability increases when a lawyer lets funds into the firm's account when no legal services are performed or expected. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "when no legal services are performed or expected to be performed"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 102 (United States): Individual 1, a US citizen resident in Belize, incorporated more than 5 000 shell companies in Belize and the West Indies for securities and tax fraud schemes; Individual 2, secret owner of a broker-dealer and investment management company in Panama City and Belize City, ran three interrelated schemes: fraudulent stock promotion and price manipulation, circumventing capital gains taxes under FATCA, and laundering more than USD 250 million through unidentifiable debit cards and attorney escrow accounts. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "incorporated more than 5 000 shell companies in Belize and the West Indies"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 102 (continued): Individual 2 used a US-based lawyer to launder over USD 250 million of stock manipulation proceeds, directing them to five law firm accounts and back to scheme members and co-conspirators, also enabling evasion of reporting requirements to tax authorities. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "directing the fraud proceeds to five law firm accounts and transmitting them back to members of the scheme"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 3 (Australia): university managers and construction company directors ran a fraudulent invoice scheme (inflated invoices and invoices for work never done), with profits used to buy racehorses and property and managers repaid with kickbacks or racehorse shares; accounting firms making international transfers for the suspects sent money to many countries, largely to horse-racing-linked companies, and received similar-value transfers back, mostly from Hong Kong, so authorities suspected a professional money laundering syndicate. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The authorities suspected that the accounting firms were laundering the funds on behalf of the suspects"</sub>

## Facilitating the purchase or sale of real property

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Real estate is highly attractive for laundering: the market has shown resilience and generally appreciated in dense cities, is a relatively safe store of illicit wealth, offers a legitimate rationale for receiving large sums on sale, and provides an excuse for mortgage loans (including from private lenders) and for ongoing payments disguised as rental income. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Real estate property is a highly attractive medium for laundering the proceeds of crime."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Approximately one-third of the case studies involved acquiring real estate, and most of those used a professional intermediary for the purchase. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Approximately one-third of the case studies analysed for this report involved the acquisition of real estate"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In some jurisdictions legal professionals must facilitate real estate transactions; elsewhere providers are commonly used for conveyancing as a precaution, and they often identify and report vendors and purchasers to authorities, which makes them vulnerable to those disguising the real estate's beneficial owner. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "professionals will often be responsible for identifying and reporting the vendor and/or purchaser"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Methods in the cases for concealing the beneficial owner of real estate: purchase through intermediaries such as companies, trusts, family members, associates or complicit third parties with no criminal record (Case Study 2); and use of a false name or fraudulent identification information (Case Study 101). (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "use of a false name or fraudulent identification information (Case Study 101)"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Sometimes the beneficial owner is not involved in the purchase and directs a third party to buy; this is difficult to detect and requires the professional to be vigilant and aware of ML/TF risks. In Case Study 2 a suspect bought a property in a family member's name and used crime proceeds to pay down the mortgage, with the solicitor doing conveyancing, registering the purchase and acting as intermediary for loan repayments. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "purchased a property in the name of a family member and used the proceeds of crime to pay down"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 2 (Australia, real estate method): a syndicate member bought a property worth more than AUD 700 000 in a family member's name with a mortgage, then over two months made 16 cash deposits totalling more than AUD 320 000 to the solicitor to pay off the mortgage; the cash was crime proceeds. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "more than AUD320 000 in 16 cash deposits to their solicitor"</sub>

## Client advocacy and brokerage services

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Professionals often offer client advocacy and brokerage (introducing clients to banks, opening accounts, seeking loans), becoming the intermediary to the regulated sector and responsible for giving banks the information for CDD; many intermediaries in the cases facilitated banking relationships for clients. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "takes on the responsibility of providing banks with the requisite information to meet their CDD obligations"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Professionals occasionally control some or all of clients' bank accounts as signatories to manage affairs and bookkeeping; this is standard for in-house accountants and lawyers and for outsourced professionals serving small and medium businesses regularly, but not common for occasional services. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "professionals are listed as signatories to their clients’ accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Managing a client's accounts raises the professional's risk of facilitating ML/TF but presents lower risk of obscuring beneficial ownership, provided the transaction is not through an account in the name of the professional or firm and appropriate CDD is done by the professional and then the financial institution. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "presents a lower risk of obscuring beneficial ownership"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Professional service providers also introduce clients to other providers, including lawyers, accountants, TCSPs and real estate agents (excerpt ends mid-list). (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "providers, including other lawyers, accountants, TCSPs, real estate agents,"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Paragraph 174 says that in countries where financial institutions are permitted to rely on third parties to perform CDD on the customer (footnote 54 points to Recommendation 17 of the FATF Standards), professional intermediaries are vulnerable to exploitation to disguise beneficial ownership and control. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "professional intermediaries are vulnerable to exploitation for the purposes of disguising beneficial ownership and control"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 174 states that the ultimate responsibility for conducting CDD should remain with the financial institution during a third-party reliance arrangement. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the ultimate responsibility for conducting CDD should remain with a financial institution during a third-party reliance arrangement"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Paragraph 174 adds that criminals will still try to use the reputation of professional intermediaries to convince the financial institution that a false or misleading identity or ownership and control structure is legitimate. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "criminals will still seek to use the reputation of professional intermediaries to convince the financial institution"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Paragraph 174 points to one US case study (Case Study 101) in which a complicit professional used their role as an intermediary to frustrate and overcome financial institutions' CDD in order to obtain fraudulent loans. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a complicit professional used their role as a professional intermediary to frustrate and overcome the CDD activities of financial institutions"</sub>

## Online registry systems

- **descriptive / context**: Some jurisdictions let residents use different forms of digital identity to incorporate companies directly online with no intermediaries (paragraph 11). (p. 101)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Some jurisdictions permit their residents to use different forms of digital identity to incorporate companies directly online"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Digital identity modalities vary (tokens, passwords, SMS, biometric authentication). In-person identification happens only once, through a government authority or an authorised agent such as a bank or post office, using valid ID papers and/or biometric data. The digital identity is then stored centrally and can be used to access services of many public and private entities. (p. 101)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an in-person identification takes place only once, either through a government authority or an authorised agent"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Digital identity information may in some cases not be updated after initial issuance. The person may be held responsible for keeping access details confidential and bears responsibility for how the identity is used; use of another person's identity can be a criminal offence in itself. (p. 101)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "bears responsibility for how it is used"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Digital identity systems have advantages (simplified formalities, more security, since a digital ID is almost impossible to falsify) but raise concerns about higher risks of identity theft and misuse by straw men, particularly where safeguards are insufficient. (p. 101)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is almost impossible to falsify a digital ID"</sub>

## Question 2: Describe the legal requirements for the formation of legal arrangements (whether under domestic or foreign law).

- **descriptive / context**: In February 2017 the FATF decided to expand the project scope to include trusts, so Question 2 sought information on formation of trusts and similar arrangements under domestic or foreign law (paragraph 12). (p. 101)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In February 2017, the FATF decided that the scope of this project should be expanded to include trusts"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Of responses to Question 2: 60% came from jurisdictions whose domestic law provides for trusts or similar arrangements; 21% from jurisdictions that are not a source of law for legal arrangements but recognise foreign ones and permit them to be created or administered by gatekeepers or others (e.g. under the Hague Trust Convention); 19% do not recognise any legal arrangements, domestic or foreign (e.g. in courts or tax system). (p. 101)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "60% of responses were from jurisdictions whose domestic law provides for the creation of trusts"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Among the 52 jurisdictions allowing creation of trusts or similar arrangements under domestic or foreign law, almost 54% gave no information on whether registration is required and 46% gave no information on implementing beneficial ownership obligations. The text says the information may show some general patterns but a sample this small may not support conclusions on best practices (paragraph 13). (p. 101)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "almost 54% did not provide any information as to whether registration is required"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Of the 24 jurisdictions that gave information on registration of legal arrangements, 29% require registration of trusts, 29% do not, and the largest share, 42%, require it only when certain criteria are met: generation of taxable income, making taxable distributions, real property as a trust asset, or the trust being a foreign trust. Trusts may be registered as some other type of business entity where the jurisdiction does not allow trusts to be created (paragraph 14). (p. 101)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The largest percentage, 42% require registration of trusts only when certain criteria are met"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: On implementing beneficial ownership requirements for legal arrangements, 27 jurisdictions provided information: 52% impose obligations by applicable statute, 26% rely on a combination of common law and statute, and 22% rely solely on common law trustee obligations for availability of beneficiary information (paragraph 15). (p. 102)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "52% impose beneficial ownership obligations by applicable statute"</sub>

## Box 5. Jersey’s requirements for professional trustees

- **descriptive / case example**: Jersey: any person who by way of business, regardless of profession, acts as or arranges for another to act as trustee of an express trust conducts a regulated activity and is subject to AML obligations. The text calls this an activity-based approach, similar to the formation of legal persons in Singapore, that avoids naming a profession and avoids unintended AML obligations on professions whose day-to-day activities are not at risk of abuse. (p. 102)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "conducts a regulated activity and is subject to AML obligations"</sub>

## Box 6. New Zealand’ regime for foreign trusts

- **descriptive / case example**: New Zealand: since February 2017 a regime requires foreign trusts (a trust to which a settlor has never been resident in New Zealand) with a New Zealand resident trustee to register with Inland Revenue Department. Trustees must update changed details within 30 days of becoming aware, and file annual returns updating details, attaching financial statements and giving details of new settlors and beneficiaries who receive a distribution. (p. 102)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Trustees are required to update any changed details within 30 days of becoming aware of the change"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A New Zealand resident trustee who fails to comply may lose the tax exemption for foreign-sourced income and may be prosecuted. The regime responded to publications by international bodies and media reports identifying foreign trusts as misused in criminal schemes; authorities enhanced oversight of these entities. (p. 102)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may cease to receive the tax exemption for foreign sourced income and may be subject to prosecution"</sub>

## Legal Persons

Notification of changes follows at 37.5% (12 of 32). ## Legal Arrangements

- **descriptive / finding or result**: For legal arrangements other than those under common law obligations, there appear to be no or minimal maintenance requirements: only 9 of 23 responding jurisdictions impose any, three of which require notification of changes in beneficial ownership or control; 14 of 23 have no maintenance requirement at all (paragraph 21). (p. 104)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Only 9 of 23 responding jurisdictions impose any maintenance requirement for legal arrangements"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Based on these figures, availability of accurate and up-to-date information on legal arrangements depends almost entirely on gatekeepers and non-professional trustees (or equivalent), with little or no role for public-sector registries. Where gatekeepers are involved in forming legal arrangements, this underscores the importance of effective supervision to ensure CDD compliance. (p. 104)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "depends almost entirely on the gatekeepers and non-professional trustees"</sub>

## Ongoing Monitoring of Compliance with Maintenance Requirements

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Twenty-five jurisdictions described mechanisms to monitor compliance with maintenance requirements. The most common is registry oversight (10 of 25, 40%): some registries have automated systems monitoring filing deadlines, some cross-check with other authorities such as tax authorities, and some do sample testing or targeted audits. Nine of 25 report monitoring by the AML supervisor or prudential regulator in compliance inspections, and 24% report no compliance monitoring at all (paragraph 22). (p. 104)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the most common mechanism for ongoing monitoring of compliance with those requirements is oversight by the registry"</sub>

## Box 7. Belgium’s beneficial ownership registry

- **descriptive / case example**: Belgium is implementing a beneficial ownership registry expected to be operational by 2018, with two automated controls: one cross-checks 'obligated entities' against the entities that actually provide beneficial ownership information; the other cross-checks the beneficial ownership database against other government databases (primarily within the Ministry of Finance) to verify data quality. A dedicated data miner will monitor these checks and a special unit of the Treasury will enforce compliance. (p. 104)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "one will cross check “obligated entities” against the entities that actually provide beneficial ownership information"</sub>

## Question 4: Describe how agencies responsible for AML/CFT supervision of gatekeepers (whether government agency or SRB) assess compliance with beneficial ownership obligations.

- **descriptive / limitation**: There is insufficient information, from questionnaire responses or mutual evaluation reports, to give a general picture of how authorities or SRBs assess gatekeepers' compliance with beneficial ownership obligations. Some common elements, not necessarily present in every case, can be described (paragraph 25). (p. 105)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There is insufficient information - either from the questionnaire responses or from mutual evaluation reports"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In most cases supervision combines desk-based reviews and on-site inspections. Desk-based reviews analyse annual independent audit reports and other mandatory reports, identify risky intermediaries (by firm size, cross-border involvement or business sector), and use automated scrutiny of registers to detect missing beneficial ownership information and identify the responsible gatekeeper. On-site inspections review internal policies, controls and procedures, the gatekeeper's own risk assessments, spot-check CDD documents and supporting evidence, and sample-test reporting obligations. Some national supervisors and SRBs mandate independent auditors to perform on-site inspections on their behalf. (p. 105)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In most cases, the supervision combines both desk-based reviews and on-site inspections"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Delegations could consider whether more information is needed on how gatekeeper compliance with beneficial ownership obligations is assessed, for example in further projects after the Horizontal Study or as part of risk-based approach (RBA) guidance for gatekeeper professions (paragraph 26). (p. 105)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Delegations could consider whether this is an issue on which more information is needed"</sub>

## Question 5: How are the businesses or professions engaged in the formation and/or maintenance of legal persons or legal arrangements regulated and supervised?

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The most startling finding: 17% of responding jurisdictions impose no AML obligations or AML supervision whatsoever on gatekeepers, despite this being a requirement of R.22, R.23 and R.28. In some cases, such as the US and Canada, this results from resistance by the relevant sectors or professions (e.g. to prevent laws imposing such obligations or to mount constitutional challenges); in other cases it may be an 'unfinished' aspect of the AML/CFT system not yet implemented (paragraphs 27-28). (p. 105)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "do not impose upon their gatekeepers any AML obligations or AML supervision whatsoever"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Under Recommendation 28, the categories of DNFBPs acting as gatekeepers should be subject to effective systems for monitoring and ensuring compliance with AML/CFT requirements, i.e. effective supervision. Question 5 sought the types of supervisory regimes and supervisors' roles. (p. 105)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should be subject to effective systems for monitoring and ensuring compliance with AML/CFT requirements"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Where supervision is imposed, arrangements fall into four general categories: a national AML supervisor; multiple national AML supervisors; a national supervisor for one or more gatekeeping sectors with one or more self-regulating bodies (SRBs) for others; and SRBs only for all gatekeepers (paragraph 28).

## Types of businesses at risk of trade-based money laundering

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Types of businesses at risk of TBML are varied: small or medium-sized businesses featured in multiple schemes, but some investigations involved large multinational companies, often through overseas subsidiaries with more fluid trading relationships when distributing products into newer markets. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "some investigations involved large multinational companies, often through overseas subsidiaries"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Specific business indicators: (1) rapid growth of newly formed companies into existing markets; (2) consistent and significant cash payments, including to previously unknown third parties, and possibly unexplained third-party payments received; (3) unnecessarily complicated supply chains with multiple transhipments; (4) established companies in one sector unexpectedly pivoting to an unrelated sector (example: an IT company quickly establishing a foothold in acquiring and distributing bulk pharmaceuticals); (5) companies simultaneously involved in more than one unrelated sector. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Rapid growth of newly formed companies into existing markets;"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The sub-sections cover private sector entities in international trade that may be uniquely positioned to identify TBML (freight forwarders and customs brokers) or commonly used by criminals in TBML (shell and front companies); the role of FIs and DNFBPs is explained in Section 6. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "like freight forwarders and customs brokers, or are commonly used by criminals as a tool in TBML schemes"</sub>

## Shell and front companies

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Shell and front company exploitation is a key feature of many ML types and facilitates many predicate offences. They often intersect with TBML/TF but do not feature in all schemes, particularly those exploiting legitimate supply chains. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "has become a key feature of many different types of ML activity"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Some OCGs, PMLs and terrorist financiers build a TBML scheme around shell companies or use them in financial settlement, anonymising the ultimate beneficial owner; front companies offer opportunities to integrate physical cash into a business and use its banking relationships to move cash across jurisdictions. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "anonymising the ultimate beneficial owner to maximum effect"</sub>

## Freight forwarders and customs brokers

- **descriptive / context**: Freight forwarders help buyers and sellers navigate customs and shipping processes and act as experts in the most efficient transport method, which can combine multiple modes in a single shipment; they can therefore access documentation that may show TBML indications. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "can access and review relevant documentation that might contain indications of TBML"</sub>

- **descriptive / limitation**: The report cautions that a company meeting one or more of the listed business risk indicators is not necessarily being used in a TBML scheme. (p. 27)[^original]

## Freight forwarders and customs brokers

- **mandatory / data or feature**: The report says a commercial invoice, although it has no standardised format, must include information such as the parties to the transaction, the goods being transported and the harmonised commodity description and coding system; it can also include a statement certifying the invoice is true. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the goods being transported and the harmonised commodity description and coding system17"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: The bill of lading is issued by a carrier or its agent to acknowledge receipt of cargo for shipment and has three functions: it is a conclusive receipt that goods have been loaded; it contains or evidences the terms of the contract of carriage; and it is a document of title conferring title over the goods on the named consignee or lawful holder. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It serves as a document of title to the goods."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Customs brokers, affiliated with freight forwarders or independent, facilitate customs clearance, checking documentation or licences and duty and tax payment. Services can include: checking classification and valuation of goods and commodity codes; liaising with government agencies and customs authorities; advising on licences for restricted or hazardous goods; and helping arrange payment of import duties and VAT. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Checking the classification and valuation of goods, ensuring the right commodity codes are used."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Most jurisdictions do not impose AML/CFT obligations on freight forwarders and customs brokers, yet they can hold trade data complementing that of authorities and FIs. A key TBML challenge is disaggregation of data: no single stakeholder owns or accesses information that would help identify TBML. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Even though most jurisdictions do not impose AML/CFT obligations on freight forwarders and customs brokers"</sub>

## Common trade-based money laundering techniques

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Over- and under-invoicing of goods and services: the key element is misrepresenting the price of a good or service to transfer value; the critical enabler is that importer and exporter are complicit in the misrepresentation. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The key element of this technique is the misrepresentation of the price of the good or service"</sub>

## Freight forwarders and customs brokers

- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 17 explains that the harmonised system is an internationally standardised system of names and numbers used to classify traded products. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an internationally standardised system of names and numbers to classify traded products"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: The report says competent authorities should consider routine engagement with freight forwarders and customs brokers to share information and risk indicators and better educate them about TBML/TF schemes, which could extend to establishing or extending membership of TBML-focused PPPs to include them. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Competent authorities should consider routine engagement with these critical international trade facilitators"</sub>

## Common trade-based money laundering techniques

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Over- and under-shipment of goods and services: misrepresentation of the quantity of goods or services, including 'phantom shipments' where no product moves at all; it relies on collusion between importer and exporter. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "including ‘phantom shipments’ where no product is moved at all"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Multiple invoicing of goods and services: does not require price misrepresentation; reuses existing documentation to justify multiple payments for the same shipment or service, and criminals or terrorist financiers exploit it by reusing documents across multiple FIs, making it hard for one institution to identify. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "reuse of existing documentation to justify multiple payments for the same shipment of goods or delivery of services"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Falsely described goods and services: misrepresentation of the quality or type of a good or service, such as shipping a relatively inexpensive good described as a more expensive or entirely different item to justify value movement. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the misrepresentation of the quality or type of a good or service"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Though listed separately, criminals can mix techniques in one scheme; for example sophisticated networks may combine phantom shipping with multiple invoices, using one shipment of actual goods to create legitimacy or test customs compliance, then multiple invoices for phantom shipments as cover for fund transfers. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "more sophisticated ML networks may use phantom shipping in conjunction with multiple invoices"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Black Market Peso Exchange is a traditional TBML type used by Central and South American drug cartels to launder US-generated drug proceeds. A driver is currency restrictions limiting legitimate companies' ability to buy from foreign suppliers, so businesses rely on complicit money traders to exchange local currency for US dollars, and cartels use this chain to move illicit US dollars between jurisdictions. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "One of the drivers behind this scheme is currency restrictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: In typical BMPE schemes the broker receives dollars from the cartel's cash controllers and pays the US supplier, directly or by placing cash into multiple accounts via structured deposits and wiring funds to suppliers. The supplier exports goods to companies in the Central or South American country, which pay local currency to the money broker, who passes it to the cartel minus commission, so no cash moves between jurisdictions where LEAs might intercept it. Such schemes involve some importer and exporter knowledge of the illicit origin of funds. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This scheme ensures no cash is moved between the jurisdictions, where it may be identified and intercepted by LEAs."</sub>

## Box 2.9. Black Market Peso Exchange Scheme

- **descriptive / case example**: In January 2020 the US Justice Department indicted six Colombian nationals, with an Indian national, for an international ML scheme involving TBML and an unlicensed money transmission business, laundering drug proceeds using a BMPE-style process so US cash was not physically transferred. The Colombians allegedly acted as money brokers receiving proceeds from couriers across the US and incoming international wires; the cash was introduced into the US financial system and moved to the business account of the Indian national, an alleged complicit merchant. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the US Justice Department indicted six Colombian nationals, in co-operation with an Indian national"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The merchant exported consumer electronics worldwide including to importers in Colombia, exporting roughly equivalent value; the importers paid by delivering pesos to money brokers in Colombia, who passed them to the drug trafficking organisation, removing any need for cross-border cash movement. The case shows continued reliance on peso exchange and misuse of legitimate trading relationships by complicit exporters and importers (source: United States). (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This negated the need for the drug traffickers to attempt any movement of cash across borders"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A key finding across the FATF Global Network, FIUs and private sector is that these techniques are still prevalent, while other trends are emerging, including exploitation of legitimate supply chains operating without importer-exporter collusion, introduction of criminal cash into trade transactions, and growth of surrogate shopping. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these techniques are still prevalent today"</sub>

## Assessment of current trade-based money laundering risks

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Continued use of common techniques is complemented by consolidation of previous settlement methods (e.g. third-party invoice settlement) and growth of newer techniques supporting cash integration, some of which do not rely on product or trade document misrepresentation. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the consolidation of previous methods of financial settlement (e.g. third-party invoice settlement)"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Illicit cash integration: because TBML is prevalent for laundering proceeds of illegally smuggled commodities, OCGs and PMLs need ways to integrate illicit cash into the financial system, including exploiting other types of FI. A BMPE variation has OCGs or PMLs disposing of illicit cash cooperating with others seeking cash; other variations are surrogate shopping networks and infiltration of legitimate supply chains. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "OCGs or PMLs looking to dispose of illicit cash cooperate with other OCGs or PMLs looking for cash"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Third-party intermediaries facilitating invoice settlement were first identified in 2006 and remain constant in TBML, including schemes without importer-exporter complicity; the report revisits this to inform those who may be told to settle an invoice via an entirely unknown and unrelated third party. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "has remained a constant feature of TBML schemes, including those schemes not involving complicit relationships"</sub>

## Exploitation of other types of FIs

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: While much TBML cash integration involved banks, OCGs and PMLs also exploit other FIs such as money value transfer services (MVTS) or informal mechanisms like hawala; e.g. conspirators in a false invoicing scheme used MVTS to pay for goods rather than a bank, perceiving the MVTS sector to have less developed TBML understanding so it would not question a significant business payment. Footnote 18 says some PMLs operate or are closely linked to MSBs, keeping control of the payment process. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "OCGs and PMLs can also exploit other types of FIs, including money value transfer services (MVTS)"</sub>

## Offsetting schemes

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Offsetting or compensation is a BMPE variation in which OCGs or PMLs dispose of illicit cash by cooperating with OCGs or PMLs seeking cash; the case study shows a hybrid TBML and SBML scheme. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A variation of _Black Market Peso Exchange_ is called offsetting or compensation"</sub>

## Exploitation of other types of FIs

- **recommended / recommendation**: The report says supervisors and regulators should consider whether MVTS-type businesses need to establish specific TBML/TF risk mitigation policies and controls in meeting their AML/CFT requirements, reinforcing the FATF Recommendations' obligations on customer due diligence and MVTS. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervisors and regulators should consider whether these businesses need to establish specific TBML/TF risk mitigation policies"</sub>

## Box 2.10. Compensation scheme

- **descriptive / case example**: Belgian authorities have for several years noted Brazilian or Portuguese nationals setting up or taking over Belgian construction or cleaning companies, used as cover to employ non-declared workers, forming a network of similarly profiled companies existing for limited time for specific transactions and quickly replaceable by new companies with new managers. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Brazilian or Portuguese nationals are setting up or taking over Belgian companies in the construction or cleaning industry"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: These companies take criminal cash from other criminal networks, giving a veneer of legitimacy by invoicing for building maintenance services; funds are then transferred to wholesalers in Asia who are instructed to buy merchandise for Belgian retailers, which is imported and sold as part of a related TBML scheme (source: Belgium). (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "providing a veneer of legitimacy by invoicing for building maintenance services"</sub>

## Surrogate shopping networks

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Surrogate shopping networks involve shoppers buying desired goods for wealthier individuals, ostensibly to circumvent customs controls or tariff restrictions; some shop for OCGs to distance them from the asset, suggesting some knowledge. Like BMPE, OCGs or PMLs provide local currency (crime proceeds) to shoppers who pay for goods transported to another jurisdiction and passed to the OCG or PML. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Surrogate shopping networks involve individuals or networks of shoppers purchasing desired goods on behalf of wealthier individuals"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: An adaptation has the shopper pay by credit card and the OCG or PML settle the debt with crime proceeds; e.g. students made multiple purchases of smart phones and tablets, with card balances settled by electronic funds from companies suspected of laundering drug proceeds, and the electronics suspected of onward sale into grey markets in Asia and the Middle East. It can be combined with traditional techniques such as misrepresenting goods to increase the margins of proceeds transferred. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "students were making multiple purchases of portable electronics, such as smart phones and tablets"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Infiltration of legitimate supply chains: an OCG or PML buys a stake in a legitimate business (struggling or not) and uses its supply chain to integrate illicit cash, without changing business practices or necessarily using common TBML techniques; the aim is a slow, steady increase of illicit cash into the business while keeping existing supply chain relationships. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "their aim is a slow and steady increase of introducing illicit cash into the business"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: This method is challenging for authorities or FIs to detect, but as shown in the Box 2.6 case study it is still possible to investigate seizures of illicit cash and uncover a sophisticated TBML scheme. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it still is possible to investigate seizures of illicit cash and uncover a sophisticated TBML scheme"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Third-party intermediaries have been a constant in TBML since 2006, often in invoice settlement and associated with open account exploitation owing to limited FI oversight, and they can serve a dual purpose depending on where in the chain the OCG or PML is involved. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "are often associated with the exploitation of open account trading, because of the lack of oversight by FIs"</sub>

## Third-party intermediaries facilitating invoice settlement

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: When penetrating legitimate supply chains and sourcing goods without misrepresentation, the OCG may pay by involving a previously unknown third party (usually the company integrating criminal cash), which may be based in locations with beneficial ownership secrecy provisions. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "involving a previously unknown third-party (usually the company responsible for integrating criminal cash)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: FIs appear generally aware of the risk of third parties settling invoices, but unsuspecting firms receiving payment may not question why their trade relationship expanded to a previously unknown third party possibly in another jurisdiction. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "unsuspecting firms receiving payment may not question why their trade"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Although long-standing, third-party settlement is not systematically reported by DNFBPs such as auditors or accountants who may encounter such payments; greater awareness may increase reporting and improve intervention strategies. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is not systematically reported by DNFBPs, for example by auditors or accountants"</sub>

## Box 2.11. Third-party settlements

- **descriptive / case example**: The New Zealand FIU (NZFIU) received multiple suspicious activity reports on payments to NZ fruit export companies from Eastern European bank accounts registered to shell companies in high-risk jurisdictions; the NZ companies received approximately USD 1.5 million over 18 months. Enquiries showed the transfers paid for legitimate NZ fruit exports to a South-East Asian jurisdiction, and company representatives could not explain why payments came from shell accounts unrelated to the company receiving the goods. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "received approximately USD 1.5 million during an 18-month period from these overseas accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: NZ banks supplied invoices given as justification that were clearly fraudulent, depicting payments for export of 'ceramic tiles' from the NZ company to an Eastern European company, 'signed' by a purported NZ company manager who did not work there. NZFIU assessed the payments (potentially tens of millions of dollars given account activity volume) as part of a complex TBML scheme run from Eastern Europe, converting illicit funds to trade goods shipped for resale elsewhere, generating clean funds (source: New Zealand). (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these invoices were clearly fraudulent and depicted the transactions as payments for export of ‘ceramic tiles’"</sub>

## Trade-based terrorist financing

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Only a handful of respondents referenced TBTF, particularly when developing their NRA, recognising that what makes TBML attractive for moving value offers the same opportunities to terrorist financiers. TBTF schemes can and do rely on common TBML techniques and can feature legitimate firms and transactions through the supply chain until funds are diverted to terrorist organisations. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "TBTF schemes can and do rely on the common TBML techniques"</sub>

## Box 2.12. Misuse of an existing trade chain to move funds for terrorists

- **descriptive / case example**: Case example 1: an importer in country A buying from a supplier in country B in ongoing legitimate trade had the payment made by terrorist operatives in country C; after shipment the importer paid the goods' value in cash to operatives of the same organisation in country A. The organisation thus moved cash from C to A through legitimate trade using the commodity as currency; the goods were detected by the Israeli Customs Authority and confiscated. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the terrorist organisation was able to transfer cash from country C to country A through the legitimate trade system"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case example 2: a network of MVTS and hawala settled trade between importers in country A and exporters in country B (importers paid an MVTS in A, which transferred funds by hawala to an MVTS in B, which paid exporters through a bank; for settlement the A MVTS sent money by cash couriers to an MVTS in country C, which bank-transferred to the B MVTS). A terrorist financier in country D sent funds to the C MVTS, then to the B MVTS; the same sum was deducted from what the A MVTS owed for settlement and instead passed to a local terrorist organisation. The financier penetrated the trade chain using complicit MVTS, avoiding direct payments to the organisation (source: Israel). (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "using complicit MVTS to inject funds intended for TF purpose into the trade transactions"</sub>

## Box 2.13. Trade-based terrorist financing case

- **descriptive / case example**: Italian authorities spotted suspicious activity in personal and business accounts of two brothers linked to wholesale and retail motor vehicle trade firms. Vehicle purchases and movements looked legitimate, but suspicion arose from significant discrepancies in the underlying economic activity, e.g. significant cash deposits and withdrawals and personal accounts credited with money from other commercial entities. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "significant cash deposits and withdrawals, plus personal bank accounts credited with monies originating from other commercial entities"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Initial analysis suspected an ML network using false invoicing; further investigation, supplemented by open source reporting, identified a TF link. Key risk indicators were the jurisdictions linked to vehicle exports (with poor counter-terrorism financing safeguards) and economic sectors previously associated with TF. This was confirmed by subsequent STRs and police validation; profits from transnational car dealing went to Middle Eastern import/export companies and onwards to terrorist organisations (source: Italy). (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the fact these jurisdictions had poor counter-terrorism financing safeguards"</sub>

## Services-based money laundering

- **descriptive / definition**: Services-based money laundering (SBML) is not TBML and is included for reference as a recognised increasing risk; the fundamental difference is that SBML schemes exploit trade in services or other intangibles to disguise and justify movement of illicit proceeds. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Services-based money laundering (SBML) is not TBML"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: SBML schemes complicate detection: for services such as consultancy or advisory work it is hard to assess legitimacy of the buyer-provider relationship, and no physical commodity is traded that would normally create import or export data. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is no physical commodity traded, which would normally create import or export data"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The report lists gambling, particularly online gambling service providers, and software providers (including gaming software and business software such as electronic point of sale services) among the services and sectors identified as vulnerable to SBML. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Software providers, including gaming and business software, such as electronic point of sale services;"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The report also lists financial services (including virtual asset wealth management), consultancy and advisory services, and trademarks and similar intangible items such as intellectual property rights as vulnerable to SBML. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Trademarks and similar intangible items such as intellectual property rights."</sub>

## Section 3. Challenges to countering trade-based money laundering

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Despite significant attention, countering TBML remains challenging. The 2006, 2008 and 2012 reports and the WCO-Egmont Customs-FIU Cooperation Handbook stress TBML as particularly complex, causing difficulties at each stage of investigation and detection; together with international cooperation challenges and private sector difficulties, this has led to a relatively low number of successful TBML investigations globally. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "have led to a relatively low number of successful TBML investigations across the globe so far"</sub>

## Lack of understanding and awareness

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The 2006 and 2008 FATF publications put TBML on agencies' radar and NRAs improved understanding, and more publications by international bodies, academia and national authorities help public and private sectors. But as many respondents noted, some authorities have only a basic understanding and may be unaware of sophisticated aspects; those with advanced understanding struggle to keep pace as criminals constantly seek new ways to misuse the international trade system. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "some of the involved authorities still have only a basic understanding of TBML"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: TBML schemes are relatively complex and can exploit many sectors and commodities, from second-hand cars to flowers, so no scheme is like another. Law-abiding supply chain organisations such as production companies and traders see only a piece of the puzzle and not all are sufficiently aware of TBML signs, so they may overlook activity, making it hard for authorities to identify higher-risk sectors and prioritize (part of this passage is scrambled in the source text). (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "They can involve a multitude of sectors and commodities exploited as a means of moving value"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The report says growing online business opportunities have created additional challenges to understanding TBML methods, and the technologies public authorities use for oversight and analysis of trade transactions may not be keeping pace. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "technologies used by public authorities for oversight and analysis of trade transactions may not be keeping pace"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The report says new technologies and trade digitalisation increase the speed of trade operations, which requires authorities to adapt their strategies and develop knowledge of both criminals' modi operandi and the features of the modern trade system. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "requires authorities to adopt their strategies and develop knowledge not only about the"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The report says that, to identify suspicious trade and financial transactions among thousands of legitimate ones in a timely manner, public authorities need to digitalize the tools and techniques they use to analyse financial and trade data. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "public authorities also need to digitalize the tools and techniques they apply to analyse financial and trade data"</sub>

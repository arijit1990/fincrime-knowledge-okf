## FIU analytical approaches to trade-based money laundering

- **descriptive / context**: FIUs play a central role in both processing STRs and producing more sophisticated analysis of TBML schemes, combining intelligence from reporting entities, administrative and law enforcement authorities and international counterparts to build a fuller picture that can enable detection, substantiation or possible negation of a TBML case; such analysis can include comparing information on flows of goods with flows of funds to identify anomalies or sustain questionable variances. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the comparison of information on flows of goods and flows of funds, to initially identify anomalies"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The 'classic' operational analysis, which compares trade and financial data to detect possible anomalies, seems to be the most common way for FIUs to identify TBML cases. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "relying on comparisons between trade and financial data to detect possible anomalies"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Some FIUs use a 'reverse' approach that analyses corporate structures, registration details, alleged company purposes, corporate banking profiles and relationships between corporate networks (common representatives, overlapping ownership structures, identical registration addresses, joint bank accounts). If an 'international trading corporation' is shown with some certainty to be only a complex set of shell companies, FIUs may assume the trade transactions between these 'subsidiaries' are fictitious, and authorities can launch an investigation. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "common representatives, overlapping ownership structures, identical registration addresses, and joint bank accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The 'reverse' approach shows the importance of collecting and joining different pieces of financial intelligence and other data, and that analysis and detection of TBML does not necessarily require supporting trade documentation in all instances. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "analysis and detection of TBML does not necessarily require supporting trade documentation in all instances"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: FIUs highlight the importance of building capacity to process large data sets, including with analytics and visualization tools, and the increasingly critical role of matching technology (i.e., a thematic match filter) in their analysis. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "building the capacity to process large data sets, including through the use of analytics and visualization tools"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Because trade-related transactions are often complex and multijurisdictional, innovative IT solutions such as graph analytics and artificial intelligence and machine learning can be particularly helpful in FIU TBML analysis; they can analyse large data sets, fill in missing links in existing networks (for example identifying unknown criminal networks from known ones) and pinpoint interactions indicating fictitious trade activity. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "graph analytics and artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning, can be particularly helpful in TBML-related analysis"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Strategic analysis is a core FIU function, performed in different ways depending on resources and the structure of the domestic AML/CFT framework; it should ultimately increase understanding of risk for the FIU, other authorities, financial institutions and the public, and for TBML FIUs can give these groups insight into the potential size, scale and most commonly used methods. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the strategic analysis should lead to an increased understanding of the risk for the FIU"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The Italian FIU, with the Statistical Analysis Directorate of the Bank of Italy, developed an empirical analysis of bilateral statistics on Italy's foreign trade, based on discrepancies between statistics held by Italy and partner countries on goods traded over a period of four years. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The analysis is based on the evaluation of discrepancies between statistics held by Italy and partner countries"</sub>

## Box 4.8. Strategic analysis conducted by the Italian Financial Intelligence Unit

- **descriptive / definition**: The value of goods imported or exported as recorded in one country rarely matches the value of the corresponding exports or imports recorded by the trade partner; this misalignment is known as mirror statistics. Objective causes include insurance and freight costs, cultural and language differences, inefficient reporting systems and differences in goods classification criteria, as well as deliberate misreporting. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "registered in the trade partner country, also known as mirror statistics"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Following established economic literature on illicit financial flows, the Italian methodology controls for the main 'legal' sources of mirror-statistics discrepancies and identifies, with reasonable approximation, purposeful false declarations associated with illicit cross-border fund transfers; its final objective is to identify anomalous trade flows and define quantitative TBML risk indicators by country and product at sector level. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "define TBML risk quantitative indicators related to countries and products at sector level"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Based on the Italian FIU's operational analysis and exchanges with other national authorities, some findings of the mirror-statistics analysis have been linked to potentially illicit transactions. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "some of the findings have been linked to potentially illicit transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Other countries could replicate the Italian approach because the data used is produced for all countries by international organisations (United Nations Conference on Trade and Development, World Bank and Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development). Footnote 1 cites Gara, Giammatteo and Tosti (2019), 'Magic mirror in my hand... How trade mirror statistics can help us to detect illegal financial flows', The World Economy, 42: 3120-47. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Other countries could replicate this approach, since the relevant data used in the study is produced by international organisations"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Despite the potential of reporting to detect ML, in many jurisdictions FIs and DNFBPs still find TBML hard to identify, which often results in low quality STRs or a lack of reporting. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs and DNFBPs still experience challenges with identifying TBML"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Jurisdictions should take steps to ensure their FIs and DNFBPs have the necessary capabilities to identify suspicious transactions and promptly report them to the FIU. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions should take steps to ensure that their FIs and DNFBPs have the necessary capabilities"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Steps to build private-sector TBML detection capability may include communicating NRA findings to FIs and DNFBPs (including how criminals have misused or may misuse a sector for TBML), targeted training, and provision of risk indicators and FIU feedback. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "communicating NRA findings to FIs and DNFBPs, including how criminals have been misusing or may misuse"</sub>

## Role of customs in countering trade-based money laundering

- **descriptive / context**: Customs services are usually the primary enforcement authorities in the trade area, mandated to tackle crimes based on misuse of the international trade system including TBML; they have in-depth knowledge of international trade, goods flows and supply chains, often have sole access to international trade documents and data, and customs cargo analysis can detect TBML since anomalies in this data can indicate a scheme and other trade-related crimes. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Customs services also often have sole access to international trade documents and data, which is key to identifying TBML."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Customs services face challenges in identifying TBML: ever-increasing trade volumes and data; TBML shipments being a small fraction of overall legitimate trade; the need to balance cargo analysis and inspection against quick clearance and an efficient trade framework; and other priorities such as collecting duties and setting tariffs that need significant resources. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Shipments associated with TBML represent a small fraction of the overall legitimate trade, making TBML challenging to identify."</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: It is important to ensure customs services have sufficient capacity to examine shipping documentation and financial intelligence provided by the domestic FIU and LEAs; this can be addressed, for example, by establishing dedicated units or divisions within customs to focus on TBML. Footnote 23 notes that in some jurisdictions customs and LEAs may have direct access to financial intelligence developed by FIUs. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "establishing dedicated units or divisions with custom services to focus on this area"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Customs services regularly encounter AML methods used by organised criminal groups, especially in the placement and layering stages; in TBML, money is laundered through the international movement of commercial assets such as electronics, raw materials, clothing, jewellery and food stuffs, so customs officers have an essential role in analysing and identifying goods and shipments that could be used in TBML networks. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "money can be laundered through the international movement of commercial assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Close customs-FIU collaboration greatly enhances collective capacity to identify TBML by linking suspicious trade activity with suspicious financial activity; in TBML cases domestic predicate-offence investigations are often linked to international ML investigations as LEAs follow money and goods into the customs arena, especially for cash-intensive crime where OCGs convert illicit funds into commercial products for international shipment. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Close collaboration between customs agencies and FIUs greatly enhances the collective capacity to identify TBML"</sub>

## Role of customs in countering trade-based money laundering

- **expected / expectation**: It is particularly important that customs authorities, the FIU and LEAs synchronise their efforts in the AML/CFT area, especially in countries with elevated TBML risk. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "important that custom authorities, the FIU, and LEAs synchronise their efforts in the AML/CFT area"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The FIU of Peru provides in-house internships for Peruvian customs officials, letting a customs specialist build skills in financial analysis while preparing financial intelligence reports that are ultimately sent to the customs agency, so the expert understands how both agencies work. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FIU of Peru provides in-house internships for Peruvian customs authority officials."</sub>

(p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This collaboration should include swift information sharing between the abovementioned authorities"</sub>

## Box 4.10. German customs – FIU co-operation

- **descriptive / case example**: The Auditing Service of the German Customs Service has an information sheet, and an associated typology paper, on its involvement in the fight against ML and TF. When foreign trade, customs, fiscal, market organisation or tax audits reveal evidence of ML, the sheet sets out next steps, and in particular the Auditing Service is asked to send the ML suspicion directly to the FIU using the goAML web application. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Auditing Service is asked to send the suspicion of ML directly to the FIU using the goAML web application"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Combining FIU financial analysis expertise, customs international trade expertise and effective information sharing can increase AML/CFT efficiency and serve other customs objectives: information on high-risk sectors and TBML patterns, FIU strategic analysis outcomes and other strategic trade-shipment information may help customs prioritise cargo and shipment inspections; advanced analytical techniques in customs may increase identification of trade fraud and other trade-related crimes and help establish 'hot routes' and new schemes used by criminals and terrorists. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may help customs services improve their prioritization of cargo and shipment inspections"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: It is important for customs services to recognise counter-TBML efforts as a priority enforcement objective and to collaborate with each other bilaterally and multilaterally. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "recognize counter-TBML efforts as a priority enforcement objective and collaborate with each other on bilateral and multilateral bases"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: TBML schemes often generate a large volume of supporting documentation, part of which is meant to pass mandatory shipment clearance between customs jurisdictions and to justify payment for the goods. Criminals often falsify this documentation to earn illicit profits or move illicit value, so documentation in the importing jurisdiction can differ from that in the exporting jurisdiction; comparing inbound and outbound shipping documentation by the respective customs services may reveal trade anomalies, some possibly part of a TBML scheme. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the comparison of inbound and outbound shipping documentation conducted by the respective customs services"</sub>

## Box 4.11. CBSA: Trade Fraud & TBML Centre of Expertise

- **descriptive / case example**: Canada authorized the Trade Fraud and Trade-Based Money Laundering Centre of Expertise within the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA), operational from April 2020 and mandated to enhance CBSA capacity to identify, interdict and investigate complex trade fraud and to refer TBML files to the Royal Canadian Mountain Police. Its multi-disciplinary team of intelligence analysts, trade specialists and criminal investigators is positioned to identify and investigate anomalous trade transactions indicative of TBML and fill knowledge gaps on threat actors and modi operandi. ## Interagency groups and co-ordination bodies

- **expected / expectation**: Co-operation and co-ordination among competent authorities is a key factor in detecting and disrupting ML and TF; for TBML, effective collaboration among law enforcement, prosecutors, FIUs, customs and other authorities, with swift information sharing, is even more crucial given the complexity and variety of schemes. For trade-based terrorist financing (TBTF), effective co-operation and information sharing with intelligence agencies is also necessary. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "effective collaboration between law enforcement, prosecutors, FIUs, customs services, and other authorities"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: The report does not aim to set a required framework and says there is no 'silver-bullet' solution for TBML, but jurisdictions may want to take the factors that follow into account when establishing a new interagency group on TBML or expanding the mandate of an existing one. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions may want to take into account some of the factors below when establishing a new interagency group"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: TBML's multi-dimensional nature gives authorities extra opportunities to detect ML, since criminals combine TBML with other methods such as front companies and frontmen or complicit FIs, so identifying one element may lead to discovery of the whole scheme; which authority is best placed to detect TBML depends on the sector misused and technique applied. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identifying one element of the ML scheme may lead to the discovery of the whole scheme"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Identifying TBML schemes in full and tracing proceeds requires mapping multiple pieces of financial and trade information, which is only possible if authorities assist each other; authorities need mechanisms to communicate their TBML knowledge and expertise to LEAs in a timely manner and vice-versa, and because a TBML investigation can involve multiple agencies, a co-ordination mechanism or working group (under one agency or as a separate platform) can increase efficiency. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "authorities need to have mechanisms in place allowing them to communicate their knowledge and expertise of TBML"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Jurisdictions may structure collaboration in different models, such as a working group or 'fusion centre' exclusively focused on TBML or treating TBML as part of a broader ML mechanism, but it is important to ensure TBML is recognised as a priority proportionate to the risk it poses to national financial and trade systems; such prioritisation should safeguard authorities' ability to assign and efficiently use expertise and resources on trade, financial or other aspects of TBML. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is important to ensure that TBML is recognised as a priority proportionate to the risk it poses"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: As with other consensual crimes, TBML is hard to identify because there is likely no victim complaining, so authorities often rely on other means, some requiring comparison of large volumes of data; some professional money laundering networks using TBML rely on myriad transactions and trade operations and numerous frontmen and shell companies whose only purpose may be to disguise the illicit nature of the funds and confuse authorities. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there will likely be no victim making complaints to authorities"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: A mechanism allowing timely cross-comparison and matching of large volumes of data is important for detecting TBML and for identifying and tracing assets; wherever it is placed (within one agency or under an interagency group), its creators should ensure it can count on a wide range of information sources such as STRs, trade data, basic and beneficial ownership information, criminal records and property registries (land, cars, etc.). (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "its creators should ensure that it can count on a wide range of information sources"</sub>

## Public-private partnerships

- **descriptive / context**: The FATF Recommendations contemplate public-private co-operation on AML/CFT (for example competent authorities should provide guidance and feedback to FIs and DNFBPs in applying AML/CFT measures, per footnote 24) but do not explicitly require jurisdictions to establish a PPP; PPPs may nonetheless increase communication between sectors, which may be even more critical for TBML given the expertise needed from both authorities and the private sector. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they do not explicitly require jurisdictions to establish a PPP to meet this requirement"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: Jurisdictions creating or enhancing a PPP may choose different models given domestic risks and features; some have created a PPP specifically targeting TBML, others treat TBML as one among many ML issues, and regardless of model jurisdictions may want to consider the points that follow. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "others preferred to apply a PPP model in which TBML is treated one among many ML issues"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Even where a PPP is informal, without formal rules or procedures, it is important to reach a clear agreement among participants on the objectives of the collaboration and the division of roles, which should serve as the basis for trusted public-private relationships. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is important to reach a clear agreement among the participants on the objectives of such collaboration"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The number, level and sectors or authorities of PPP participants may vary with objectives and priorities; if TBML is among a PPP's priorities, it is important to ensure the PPP has relevant expertise, including trade, customs and trade finance. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is important to ensure that the PPP has the relevant expertise, including trade, customs, and trade finance"</sub>

(p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Jurisdictions may consider communicating the achieved outcomes, particularly related to risk identification and assessment"</sub>

(p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AFCA decided to create a private sector led task force focusing on TBML"</sub>

(p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "One way to ensure this is to identify short- and long-term goals before establishing the PPP."</sub>

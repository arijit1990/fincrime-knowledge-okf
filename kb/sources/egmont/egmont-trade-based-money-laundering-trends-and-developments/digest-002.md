## Trade process and financing

- **descriptive / context**: This section summarises common trade financing types identified in earlier FATF reports; it is by no means exhaustive and is provided to ensure a basic level of understanding. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is by no means an exhaustive list but is provided to ensure a basic level of understanding."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: International trade involves risks for the parties and uncertainty over the timing of payments between exporter and importer, creating tension along the supply chain with possible negative consequences for both; trade processes and financing have adapted to address this while supporting the global marketplace. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "uncertainty over the timing of payments between the exporter and importer"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: There are five primary payment methods for international transactions (Table 1.1), ranked by preference. For the exporter, from least to most preferable: consignment, open account, documentary collections, letters of credit, cash in advance. For the importer, from least to most preferable: cash in advance, letters of credit, documentary collections, open account, consignment. Cash in advance is thus least preferable for the importer but most preferable for the exporter. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "|Exporter|Consignment|Open Account|Documentarycollections|Letters of Credit|Cash in advance|"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 11 gives an example sub-typology of TBTF: procurement of items for use by terrorist groups, including typical goods such as firearms but also logistical equipment and weaponizable technology such as used vehicles or drones to conflict zones. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the procurement of items for use by terrorist groups"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Contributors noted open account and documentary collections as most prominent in their TBML analysis and investigations; the Wolfsberg Group notes approximately 80% of international trade processed by FIs is open account trading. Other trade finance types may also be exploited in TBML, but an importer routinely paying cash in advance as a method of systematically disguising crime proceeds is likely to arouse suspicion of authorities or FIs. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Wolfsberg Group notes approximately 80% of international trade processed by FIs is open account trading"</sub>

## Open account

- **descriptive / definition**: The UN Trade Facilitation Implementation Guide describes an open account transaction as a sale where goods are shipped and delivered before payment is due; payment is usually made within a set period, anywhere between 30 and 90 days after receipt of the good or service. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "open account transaction is a sale where the goods are shipped and delivered before payment is due"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: TBML schemes frequently involve open account because FIs have a reduced role, meaning less oversight than in documentary collections; FIs can struggle to assess accurately or consistently the legitimacy of the customer's operations, whether through automated or manual transaction monitoring. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "TBML schemes frequently involve this method because FIs have a reduced role"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The APG report described open account as creating a disconnect between the movement of the underlying trade and the money financing it; OCGs, PMLs or terrorist financiers exploit this using loopholes in a compartmentalised fashion to mitigate risk exposure, and may add complexity through third-party intermediaries in multiple jurisdictions. Footnote 12 says the 2012 report analysed open account facilities including factoring, forfeiting, pre- and post-shipment finance and buyer-supplier credit arrangements. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "creating a disconnect between the movement of the underlying trade and the money used to finance it"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Open account trading is a fundamental part of global trade, so increasing its regulation is not logistically or economically viable; later sections describe initiatives that increase opportunities to spot ML/TF exploitation, and online material sets out open account compliance procedures to increase TBML detection. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the potentially easy answer of increasing regulation of open account trading is not logistically or economically viable"</sub>

## Documentary collections

- **descriptive / definition**: In documentary collection the exporter requests payment by presenting shipping and collection documents to its FI, which forwards them to the importer's FI; the importer's FI transfers funds to the exporter's FI, which credits the exporter. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the exporter requests payment by presenting shipping and collection documents for the traded goods to its FI"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Despite a perceived larger FI role, it is limited because FIs do not necessarily verify the documents, and documents are not always standardised, increasing TBML risk through fictitious or false invoicing. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they do not necessarily verify the documents"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Where documents can be checked and assured, data points that can be used to spot TBML include: use of a personal email address instead of a legitimate business email; obvious recycling of previous documentation with few or no edits, even as basic as the date (subject to the FI's data storage capabilities); and a complete lack of any trading presence of the exporter after FI research, including residential rather than business premises for exporters supplying significant quantities of goods. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Use of a personal email address in lieu of a legitimate business email."</sub>

## Section 2. Trade-based money laundering risks and trends

- **descriptive / context**: Section 2 objectives include acknowledging existing FATF Recommendations to give jurisdictions confidence in using or enhancing their AML/CFT frameworks to identify and disrupt TBML/TF, and illustrating how jurisdictions and firms understand exposure to TBML and TBTF, including via national risk assessments or risk-specific threat assessments such as exploitation of a jurisdiction's corporate structures. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Acknowledges the requirements of the existing FATF Recommendations"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The section highlights the most common predicate offences involving TBML as a preferred or significant ML mechanism, without detailing them, to encourage competent authorities to revisit historic or ongoing predicate-offence investigations to determine if TBML is involved. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to encourage competent authorities to revisit historic or ongoing investigations into those predicate offences"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The section also summarises economic sectors or products vulnerable to TBML/TF (not definitive, to guide reviews by authorities or firms), describes common traditional TBML techniques and newer risks, and notes services-based money laundering (SBML), which shares similarities with TBML but is stressed as a totally different form of money laundering. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "while it shares similarities with TBML, it is a totally different form of money laundering"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Jurisdictions had difficulty indicatively quantifying their TBML/TF exposure. Main challenges: complexity and growth of international trade, customs authorities checking only a fraction of shipments, the cross-jurisdictional nature of TBML, and in some cases limited understanding by FIs and their customers of TBML/TF exposure. Footnote 13 says even access to invoices, shipping and customs documentation or physical spot checks does not guarantee detecting TBML, reflecting the need for cooperation with all trade chain participants. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the challenges customs authorities faced in checking more than a fraction of international trade shipments13"</sub>

## Risk-based approach to trade-based money laundering

- **descriptive / context**: Since the APG's 2012 report the FATF Recommendations and evaluation procedures were revised, including an effectiveness assessment methodology focused on practical implementation rather than just transposition into domestic law. For example, the Recommendations require a legal framework for prosecuting ML offences, while the effectiveness assessment determines how far those offences, including TBML, are investigated and prosecuted in line with the country's risk profile. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "focuses on the practical implementation of the AML/CFT measures, rather than just the transposition"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: In creating an NRA, a country assesses inputs including intelligence reports, suspicious transaction reports (STRs), threat assessments, investigation outcomes, and economic and social indicators, as well as the level of threat and existing vulnerabilities. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "intelligence reports, suspicious transaction reports (STRs), threat assessments, investigation outcomes, and economic and social indicators"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Public sector contributors' overarching theme was association of TBML with domestic and foreign predicate offences, including those involving smuggling of illicit or restricted commodities such as drug trafficking, arms dealing or tobacco smuggling, with OCGs and PMLs re-exploiting the smuggling supply chain to launder proceeds. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "association of TBML with a range of domestic and foreign predicate offences"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The report states that FATF Recommendation 1 requires countries to identify, assess and understand their ML/TF risks, which could include threats and vulnerabilities linked to TBML/TF, and to implement preventative and mitigating measures commensurate with the risks identified. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "requires countries to identify, assess, and understand their ML/TF risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report says countries often meet this Recommendation 1 requirement by developing national risk assessments (NRAs) for ML and TF, some publicly available in full or sanitised form, which are primarily driven by public sector bodies but can incorporate private sector feedback during development. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Countries often meet this requirement by developing NRA for ML and TF"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Others noted TBML tied to predicate offences not reliant on commodity smuggling, such as tax evasion; these schemes, typically associated with PMLs, need new supply chains and financial intermediaries as no commodity supply chain pre-exists, and were often multi-jurisdictional, also exploiting corporate services in other jurisdictions. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "require the development of new supply chains and financial intermediaries as there was no pre-existing commodity supply chain"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A handful of respondents referenced actual and potential TBTF abuse, but most did not see trade system abuse in moving funds to facilitate terrorist acts or on behalf of individual terrorists or groups. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "most did not see abuse of the trade system in moving funds to facilitate terrorist acts"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Every country is involved in trade, so TBML or TBTF can occur anywhere; enabling activities such as misuse of corporate structures can occur in a wide range of jurisdictions. OCGs, PMLs and terrorist financiers exploit loopholes, and the NRA's benefit is challenging countries to think of exposure in terms of threat and vulnerability. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Every country in the world is involved in trade. TBML or TBTF can therefore occur anywhere."</sub>

## Box 2.1. German national risk assessment and trade-based money laundering

- **descriptive / case example**: Germany launched its first NRA in December 2017, led by the Federal Ministry of Finance with 35 federal and local authorities. It highlighted TBML's significance given Germany's trade volume; typical methods: over- and under-invoicing of goods and services, multiple billing, fictitious trades, shell companies, and investing incriminated cash in high-priced goods (e.g. vehicles, watches, jewellery, gold, real estate, art). Private sector cooperation and obligated parties' reporting behaviour played a decisive role in understanding TBML risk. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A total of 35 federal and local authorities were involved in the assessment"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Germany's experience of integrating private sector knowledge, particularly from FIs, was to ensure FIs, beyond required documents, have sufficient knowledge of the underlying trade transaction and trading partners, so that FIs, particularly banks, can better detect TBML indications and submit an STR to the FIU. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "have sufficient knowledge of the underlying trade transaction and the trading partners"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Nearly all public sector respondents said they specifically referenced TBML in their NRAs or were aware of TBML exploitation risk through financial and trade systems and/or abuse of legal entities; several delegations referenced TBML as an elevated high risk. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Several delegations referenced TBML as an elevated high-risk"</sub>

## Box 2.2. U.S. national risk assessment and trade-based money laundering In the context of its national risk assessments, the United States classifies TBML as both a threat and vulnerability.

- **descriptive / case example**: The US classifies TBML as both a threat and a vulnerability in its NRAs: threats such as Mexican and European transnational criminal organizations (TCOs) and associated drug trafficking use TBML as its sophistication makes detection difficult; vulnerabilities include the US financial and trade sectors. The US highlighted TBML as early as 2005 in its National Money Laundering Threat Assessment. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "classifies TBML as both a threat and vulnerability"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The 2015 National Money Laundering Risk Assessment (NMLRA) found most TBML cases involved a complicit merchant or front company in the US accepting illicit proceeds in exchange for goods; US Treasury then did outreach with financial institutions. In 2018 the US issued its first National Illicit Finance Strategy and an updated NMLRA, noting TBML remained a key laundering method for drug trafficking and cartels (illicit proceeds used to buy goods for export) and increasing use by PMLs, which can break the link between predicate crime and ML. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "most cases involved a complicit merchant or front company in the U.S. that accepted illicit proceeds in exchange for goods"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The 2020 National Illicit Finance Strategy noted TCOs rely more on Asian PMLs who act as money brokers in traditional TBML schemes. TBML was also highlighted as a possible explanation for a steady decrease in US bulk cash seizures, which could indicate TCOs increasingly use more discreet methods such as TBML. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "TCOs are relying more on Asian PMLs who serve as money brokers in traditional TBML schemes"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A small number of delegations said their NRAs do not reflect TBML at all, primarily because they do not consider it a major risk; one still gave insight into its TBML experiences, and two others referenced TBML within general economic crime risks including legal entity exploitation and roles of intermediaries such as company formation agents or financial advisors. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A small number of delegations noted their NRAs does not reflect TBML at all"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Many private sector contributors ran internal risk assessments of TBML/TBTF exposure; all private sector respondents recognised their exposure to TBML, in terms of products or services offered and the extent to which customers may be actively or unwittingly facilitating TBML. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "all private sector respondents recognised their exposure to TBML"</sub>

## Box 2.3. Example of a private institution assessing its exposure to tradebased money laundering

- **descriptive / case example**: Singapore has identified TBML as a priority risk and the Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) has worked to raise industry awareness. A Singapore FI with international presence assessed its trade finance business as higher inherent ML risk based on geography, product and transaction risk, and plans an automated transaction monitoring system using data and network analytics to flag higher-risk customers for investigation, removing manual review of individual trade transactions. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "assessed its trade finance business to be of higher inherent ML risk based on geography, product, and transaction risk"</sub>

## Box 2.4. Strengthening dealers in precious metals and stones regulation

- **descriptive / case example**: After Germany's NRA and DNFBP supervision input, dealers in precious metals and stones were found highly susceptible to ML including TBML, with significant cash payments just under the EUR 10 000 due diligence threshold. In transposing the Fourth EU ML Directive, the cash threshold triggering due diligence for such dealers was lowered to EUR 2 000. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the cash threshold requiring dealers in precious metals and stones to conduct due diligence was lowered to EUR 2 000"</sub>

## Box 2.3. Example of a private institution assessing its exposure to tradebased money laundering

- **mandatory / requirement**: The report says that, once jurisdictions have identified and understood their risk exposure, the FATF Recommendations require them to use that insight to inform mitigating actions and to drive effective co-operation across their AML/CFT system. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FATF Recommendations require jurisdictions to use that insight to inform mitigating actions"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The report says jurisdictions should adopt a risk-based approach to supervision; in the TBML context this might mean FIs with large trade finance divisions or significant cross-border payment activity need additional oversight from supervisory bodies to ensure the effectiveness of any threat mitigation strategy. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions should adopt a risk-based approach to supervision"</sub>

## Economic sectors and products vulnerable to TBML activity

- **descriptive / context**: The listed sectors and products are not definitive but a snapshot. A private sector respondent noted criminals exploit sectors, products or businesses prone to gaps in, or inconsistent application of, CDD and KYC across jurisdictions, which can be exacerbated by nascent or limited understanding of TBML risk. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "prone to gaps in, or the inconsistent application of, customer due diligence and know your customer processes across jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Both high-value, low-volume products (such as precious metals) and low-value, high-volume products (such as second-hand textiles) can be exploited. Common themes conducive to TBML: goods with wide pricing margins; goods with extended trade cycles (e.g. shipping across multiple jurisdictions); goods difficult for customs authorities to examine. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Goods with extended trade cycles (i.e., shipping across multiple jurisdictions);"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Supply chains moving lower-value goods are most at risk of end-to-end ownership by a criminal organisation or PML network: set-up costs can be considerably lower and attract less scrutiny, and supplying multiple markets across jurisdictions helps avoid alerting authorities to market saturation (e.g. it is not necessarily suspicious that clothing is repeatedly shipped to the same destination). Footnote 16 says such products are usually in high demand, e.g. cheap clothing, giving a veneer of legitimacy. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supply chains moving lower-value goods are most at risk to end-to end ownership by a criminal organisation or PML network."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Examples of common technique use: a criminal organisation may execute legitimate cosmetic shipments to create enough valid documentation for later phantom shipments and misuse of that documentation. In other instances transactions remained entirely legitimate but the product shipped (e.g. second-hand textiles) had almost no saleable value and was dumped on arrival. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "creating enough valid documentation to allow for subsequent phantom shipments and misuse of that previous documentation"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: With higher-value products OCGs or PMLs more likely penetrate and misuse established supply chains, for example buying in as a 'silent partner' of an existing company struggling with cash flow and using its business and supply chain contacts to launder proceeds. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they buy their way in as a ‘silent partner’ and use the business and its supply chain contacts"</sub>

## Gold, precious metals, and minerals

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Gold and other precious metals and minerals are often a factor in TBML, including gold as an alternative form of value in the ML process, i.e. a proxy for cash as well as a commodity. Association with illegal mining adds issues such as systematic breaches of health and safety standards, worker exploitation and substantial environmental problems. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "not just a commodity to exploit in moving value, but also a proxy for cash"</sub>

## Box 2.5. Use of gold in a trade-based money laundering scheme

- **descriptive / case example**: In the US four Peruvian nationals were indicted for a multi-billion dollar international gold ML scheme: between 2013 and 2017 they conspired to buy billions of dollars of criminally derived gold from Latin America and the Caribbean (proceeds of illegal mining, foreign bribery and illicit smuggling), using a Florida precious metals dealer. The gold was sold to complicit US refineries, which completed the ML cycle by paying via wire transfers that outwardly appeared as legitimate wholesale gold purchases. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "four Peruvian nationals were indicted for their alleged involvement in a multi-billion dollar, international gold ML scheme"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Numerous TBML schemes exploited auto parts or vehicles, including second-hand or luxury cars. One scheme moved damaged cars between jurisdictions with a legitimate onward repaired-vehicle market: OCGs declared the right price at export but a considerably lower value at transhipment points, despite a relatively transparent market, and routed payments through a different network of companies in other jurisdictions. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "declaring a considerably lower value at transhipment points"</sub>

## Auto parts and vehicles

- **descriptive / case example**: A joint Spanish-Italian investigation found Italian nationals in Spain who built a company network to launder drug trafficking and tax fraud proceeds, with links to Mafia activity. The OCG exploited several sectors, including high-end vehicles and lower-value textiles, to diversify risk and extend the network across jurisdictions. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Italian nationals living in Spain who created a network of companies to launder the proceeds"</sub>

## Box 2.6. Use of vehicles in a trade-based money laundering scheme

- **descriptive / case example**: The OCG used criminal cash to buy luxury vehicles in Germany, registered legal entities and created a fake paper trail of sales and purchases to build value-added tax chains, disguising original proceeds and generating more; it convinced a legitimate Italian supplier to deliver large numbers of vehicles annually, increasing the apparent legitimacy of the laundering. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "established a fake paper trail for sales and purchases to create value-added tax chains"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The OCG also used controlled import/export companies to buy luxury watches (bought in Spain and Switzerland, supplied to drug traffickers in Morocco and the Netherlands) and shoes and fabric/clothing (bought in Hong Kong, China and China, exported to Colombia and Morocco). A 2017 intervention identified assets worth EUR 8 million in several European countries; 2018 follow-up identified and seized 11 properties, 6 vehicles, 32 bank accounts and shares of two companies (source: Europol). (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identified assets worth EUR 8 million across several European countries"</sub>

## Agricultural products and foodstuffs

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: TBML schemes also exploited agricultural products and food supply chains, including highly perishable items such as fresh fruit and vegetables, which are low-value, high-volume products not necessarily affected by market saturation given perishability. OCGs and PMLs penetrate these legitimate supply chains to introduce illicit cash into the financial system and move proceeds to other jurisdictions without using common TBML techniques. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "They do not use any of the common TBML techniques, instead they exploit these legitimate supply chains"</sub>

## Box 2.7. Use of agricultural products in a trade-based money laundering scheme

- **descriptive / case example**: France, Belgium and the Netherlands launched a multi-agency investigation in 2016 after a routine vehicle check found EUR 300 000, setting up a joint investigation team on laundering drug smuggling proceeds. A PML network active an estimated four years, suspected of laundering about EUR 400 million, used techniques including TBML and underground banking in France and Belgium; a Netherlands-based underground banker ran an import/export foodstuffs business trading with North African countries. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "suspected of laundering about €400 million"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Potatoes and onions were bought in the Netherlands and Germany and exported to North African companies, which were directed to pay invoices into bank accounts controlled by the drug traffickers (third-party invoice settlement). At the 2019 end of the investigation, sentences for ML and drug trafficking were handed down, including seizure of EUR 4.8 million of assets and over EUR 7 million in cash (source: Europol). (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These companies were directed to pay invoices into bank accounts controlled by the drug traffickers."</sub>

## Clothing and second-hand textiles

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Clothing and second-hand textiles are low-value, high-volume products enabling an extended supply chain, with extreme price variability that makes price mis-description attractive for laundering. Several FIs noted use of this sector, and one PPP produced an industry-wide alert on second-hand clothing and textile supply risks. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The extreme price variability also makes it attractive in terms of mis-description of the price"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Portable or handheld electronics are also attractive in TBML because they can be deliberately misrepresented and incorrectly valued, increasing the opportunity to move significant criminal proceeds. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Portable or handheld electronics are also attractive in TBML schemes"</sub>

## Box 2.8. Use of high-end electronics in a trade-based money laundering scheme

- **descriptive / case example**: In 2017 the Australian Border Force (ABF) began examining a TBML referral about small portable electronics. Using analytical techniques plus financial and criminal intelligence it assessed a criminal network and found more than AUD 500 million (EUR 303.6 million) had passed through Australian bank accounts since 2014. Proceeds from drug sales in North America went to South East Asian accounts, were layered through many Australian accounts, then remitted offshore or used to buy small high-end electronic devices exported to South East Asia and the Middle East; undervaluation of exported devices exaggerated the illicit value transferred. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "found more than AUD 500 million [EUR 303.6 million] had passed through Australian bank accounts since 2014"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The ABF used automated and manual trade data discrepancy analysis: export declarations from country A should match the corresponding imports into country B, and mismatches were treated as an indicator of trade mis-invoicing and potential TBML. Further investigation with partner agencies linked the OCG to the transactions (source: Australia). (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Declarations of goods on export from country A should match the corresponding import to country B"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: TBML exploitation also occurs in construction materials (lumber), plant machinery, scrap metal dealers, fuel and energy products, and alcoholic or soft drinks. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "construction materials (lumber), plant machinery, scrap metal dealers, fuel and energy products, and alcoholic or soft drinks"</sub>

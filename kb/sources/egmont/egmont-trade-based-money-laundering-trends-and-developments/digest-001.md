## Trade-Based Money Laundering Trends and Developments

- **descriptive / context**: This is the December 2020 joint FATF-Egmont Group report Trade-Based Money Laundering: Trends and Developments. The FATF is described as an independent inter-governmental body whose Recommendations are recognised as the global AML/CFT standard, and the Egmont Group's goal is to provide a forum for FIUs to improve cooperation against ML and TF and foster domestic programs. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF Recommendations are recognised as the global anti-money laundering (AML) and counter-terrorist financing (CFT) standard."</sub>

## Acronyms

- **descriptive / definition**: The acronym list defines among others BMPE (Black Market Peso Exchange), OCG (Organised Criminal Groups), PML (Professional Money Launderers), SBML (Services-based Money Laundering), TBML (Trade-based Money Laundering), TBTF (Trade-based Terrorist Financing), PPP (Public Private Partnership), MVTS (Money Value Transfer Service), ACIP (AML/CFT Industry Partnership), APG (Asia Pacific Group on Money Laundering) and NRA (National Risk Assessment). (p. 4)[^original]

## Executive summary

- **descriptive / context**: The report is a companion to earlier FATF and FSRB TBML work: the 2006 landmark study, the 2008 best practices paper and the 2012 APG report. It adds input from the Egmont Group, national and international private sector institutions and multilateral bodies, outlines how TBML remains a significant ML risk, notes consolidation of established techniques and newer developments in illicit cash integration, and covers trade-based terrorist financing (TBTF). (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "outlining the extent to which TBML remains a significant money laundering (ML) risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report reflects on progress since the APG report. It recognises significant challenges remain in achieving successful criminal prosecutions of TBML, but notes new initiatives, tools and capabilities improving detection and disruption, including advanced IT and risk-assessment systems and deeper, more systematic public-private cooperation. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there are still significant challenges in achieving successful criminal prosecutions of TBML"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The intended audience includes competent authorities tasked with identifying, investigating or prosecuting TBML/TF; FIs; DNFBPs that may be at risk from or identify aspects of TBML/TF without realising it; and other participants in regional or global supply chains, such as freight forwarders and customs brokers that hold relevant trade or financing data. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "such as freight forwarders and customs brokers that hold relevant and meaningful trade or financing data"</sub>

## Key findings

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Trade is inherently complex, reflecting interconnected global supply chains, and is exploited by Organised Criminal Groups (OCGs), Professional Money Launderers (PMLs) and TF networks to facilitate financial flows including laundering proceeds of crime such as drug trafficking, financing terrorism and evading sanctions. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "exploited by Organised Criminal Groups (OCGs), Professional Money Launderers (PMLs), and Terrorist Financing (TF) networks"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Contributors noted continued exploitation of TBML techniques first identified in the 2006 FATF study, which remain in use because they are highly flexible and adaptable despite changes in global trading patterns. Footnote 2 lists these techniques as under- or over-invoicing of goods, under- or overvaluation of goods, and phantom shipments where no goods move at all. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they are highly flexible and adaptable, despite changes in global trading patterns and the growth of new markets"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: These techniques are particularly effective when there is a complicit relationship between importer and exporter who actively misrepresent an aspect of the trade or the invoice settlement process; authorities can therefore have greater impact by disrupting these complicit actors, whether by criminal prosecution or another disruption such as removing their authority to trade. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "complicit relationship between the importer and exporter, who are actively misrepresenting an aspect of the trade"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Private sector contributors commonly noted exploitation of trade financing processes. The APG report stressed that public bodies should deepen understanding of these processes alongside their knowledge of predicate offences; this remains a key finding, as greater awareness of all aspects of the trade process, including how financing processes are managed, would likely increase opportunities to detect and disrupt TBML/TF. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "exploitation of trade financing processes was a common theme noted by private sector contributors"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report takes stock of current TBML risks including new or existing methods of introducing illicit cash into the financial system. Despite growth in technology-enabled payment methods, case studies show reliance on the Black Market Peso Exchange (BMPE), and the report also notes other illicit cash integration such as surrogate shopping and infiltration of legitimate supply chains. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "case studies highlight the reliance on Black Market Peso Exchange (BMPE)"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 3: surrogate shoppers can act for wealthy individuals who face restrictions buying higher-value goods because of stringent currency controls; an example is Daigou, where individuals or syndicated groups of exporters outside Asian countries buy mainly luxury goods for customers in those countries. Footnote 4: supply chain infiltration may not result in common TBML techniques; in some cases nothing about the trading relationship changes other than an increase in illicit cash integrated into the importing company. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "individuals or syndicated groups of exporters outside Asian countries purchase mainly luxury goods for customers in those countries"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Shell or front companies intersect significantly with TBML/TBTF schemes but do not feature in all of them; when used, they can support integration of funds and hide beneficial owners. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they do not feature in all TBML/TF schemes"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Third-party intermediaries, often part of financial settlement and linked to the OCG, PML or terrorist financier, continue to occur; they can integrate quickly into the transaction chain, creating distance between their activities and the TBML or TBTF scheme. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "can quickly integrate into the transaction chain, creating additional distance between their activities"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: All contributors noted challenges remain in routinely identifying and combating TBML/TF; issues flagged in the 2006 study and expanded in 2012 remain, such as challenges in ensuring systematic and consistent domestic and international cooperation, which can hamper detection and disruption. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "challenges in ensuring systematic and consistent co-operation, both domestically and internationally"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Relevant trade data is held across multiple stakeholders with restrictions on how far it is shared, operationally and in bulk. Newer challenges include growth in online businesses, which restricts scope for proactive compliance activity, and new technologies and digitalisation of trade processes, which increase the speed of trade operations. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Relevant trade data is held across multiple stakeholders with restrictions about the extent to which this data is shared"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Numerous countries have established Public Private Partnership (PPP) initiatives where public and private stakeholders share knowledge and expertise on critical financial crime risks including TBML, and some international bodies have adopted this approach. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "numerous countries have established Public Private Partnership (PPP) initiatives"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report says financial institutions were aware of the risks of third-party intermediaries, but other parties in the supply chain, such as legitimate importers and exporters, or those with an oversight role such as auditors and accountants, may not ask why a wholly unrelated third party takes part in settling the payment. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may not question why an entirely unrelated thirdparty is involved in the payment settlement process"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: There has been growth in comprehensive studies on trade-related activities (footnote 5 cites guides by the Wolfsberg Group, ICC and BAFT, including the 2019 Trade Finance Principles paper) and competent authorities are engaging in new bi-lateral and multi-lateral intelligence sharing and investigation initiatives disrupting TBML/TF (footnote 6 cites the J5, set up in 2018 by tax authorities of the US, Canada, the Netherlands, Australia and the UK; tax evasion is a recognised predicate offence linked to TBML). (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Joint Chiefs of Global Tax Investigation (the J5), to investigate those who enable transnational tax crimes and money laundering"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FATF's 2012 introduction of a risk-based approach to AML/CFT is arguably the most fundamental revision to the Standards in recent years; it encourages systematic analysis of exposure to ML/TF risks including TBML. A primary output is often a National Risk Assessment (NRA), which can bridge public and private sector understanding of threats and vulnerabilities, ensure consistency of risk understanding and inform risk-based policies, procedures and/or legislation. Footnote 7 explains a risk-based approach as assessing and understanding ML/TF risks and taking mitigation in line with the level of risk. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "arguably the most fundamental revision to the FATF Standards in recent years"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report gives examples of NRAs that identified TBML exposure and case studies of private institutions adapting risk assessments to better detect TBML. Given the international nature of the risk, this was recognised as vital in encouraging jurisdictions and institutions to consider exposure, particularly those newly coming to the risk through growth in trade, company formation or financial services. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an increase in company formation processes or expansion of their financial services market"</sub>

## Conclusion

- **descriptive / context**: The report is relevant both to countries with well-developed systems to disrupt TBML/TBTF and to those starting out. It offers a toolkit of initiatives that can be adapted domestically; for example, where public and private sectors are restricted in sharing actionable ML or TF intelligence, a PPP can focus more on a meaningful dialogue on strategic threat and risk understanding. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "any PPP can focus more on establishing a meaningful dialogue on strategic threat and risk understanding"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: The overarching theme is vigilance, with competent authorities, private institutions and other global supply chain participants encouraged to use the report as a guide. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The overarching theme of the report is one of vigilance"</sub>

## Background

- **descriptive / context**: The FATF's 2006 TBML study identified trade activity types exploited in TBML and recognised TBML as one of three main methods by which OCGs move funds and assets to disguise origin; a TBML Best Practices paper followed in 2008. The APG produced an updated report in 2012 noting issues hampering identification and investigation of TBML. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "one of the three main methods by which OCGs move funds and assets for the purpose of disguising its origin"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: TBML has also featured in other FATF documents, including on exploitation of Free Trade Zones (2010) and its use by Professional Money Laundering Networks (2018). (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "exploitation of Free Trade Zones (2010) and its use by Professional Money Laundering Networks (2018)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report says TBML remains a profound and significant risk given the diversity of tradable goods and services, involvement of multiple parties and speed of trade. It cites the WTO Statistical Review of 2019 (the wording of this passage is scrambled in the text; legible parts say merchandise trade volume grew 3% in 2018 and value increased 10% to USD 19.67 trillion, driven partly by fuels and mining products growth of 23%, which may resonate with respondents noting exploitation of fuels and mining products in TBML, and that world merchandise exports rose 20% in value over this period). (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "trade grew by 3% in 2018, services, the while the value of that trade increased by 10% to USD"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The same WTO review noted developing economies outperformed or equalled developed economies in world trade in most of the past ten years, suggesting expansion into previously underexploited markets that created new opportunities for manipulation of trade by OCGs, PMLs and terrorist financiers. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "developing economies outperformed or equalled the performance of developed economies in world trade"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report draws on additional insight from the FATF Global Network, Egmont Group, private institutions and multilateral bodies, offers a fresh look at TBML methodologies and mitigating measures including PPPs, and gives new insight into TBTF. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It represents a comprehensive, fresh look at TBML methodologies and mitigating measures"</sub>

## Purpose and report structure

- **descriptive / context**: The report is aimed at competent authorities identifying, investigating and prosecuting ML or TF, FIs and DNFBPs that may be at risk or identify aspects of TBML/TF without realising it, and other supply chain stakeholders. It aims to explain trade and financing complexities simply while pointing to others' works for further understanding. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "describe the complexities of international trade, and associated financing mechanisms, in an easy-to-understand way"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Contributions came from three sources: the Egmont Group, giving FIU input on sources and techniques for detecting TBML, identification of TBML/TF schemes and risk indicators, complementing law enforcement and customs experience with case studies; and several national and international FIs, who provided risk insight and examples of successful TBML scheme disruptions as alternatives to criminal investigations and prosecutions. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "highlighting examples of successful TBML scheme disruptions that are alternative measures to criminal investigations and prosecutions"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Section 1 consolidates previous TBML definitions, clarifies TBTF and the difference between TBML and trade-related predicate offences such as smuggling, and outlines trade process and financing to help public bodies understand how they are exploited, a need that was a key 2012 recommendation. Footnote 9 notes this covers firms not required to do AML/CFT but involved in trade, for example freight forwarders or customs brokers. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "clarifies the differences between TBML and trade-related predicate offences, such as smuggling"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Section 2 covers risks and trends: examples of risk analysis and assessment from public and private sectors; a non-exhaustive summary of economic sectors and products at risk; assessment of 'basic' techniques from 2006 that are more accurately 'common' techniques involving misrepresentation of goods and/or value, plus continued Black Market Peso Exchange use; newer cash integration methods (surrogate shopper networks, infiltration of legitimate supply chains not relying on misrepresentation); and TBTF insights, including its greater detection complexity. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is by no means an exhaustive list but aims to assist competent authorities with less mature TBML/TF detection processes"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Section 3 reviews operational challenges hampering identification, classification, investigation or prosecution of TBML/TBTF, mapped against the 2012 assessment with previous key findings reiterated. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These are mapped against similar analysis completed in the 2012 assessment"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Section 4 covers new initiatives to improve cooperation within and across jurisdictions, including PPPs and multi-agency taskforces, and new IT-based and system-wide approaches to risk identification, with lessons for integrated disruption and investigation. The report aims to provoke consideration, debate and cross-community engagement given the inherent flexibility of TBML/TF methods. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "given the inherent flexibility of TBML/TF methods and sectors or commodities exploited"</sub>

## Methodology

- **descriptive / method**: Three questionnaires were used: the first to competent authorities across the FATF Global Network (sectors exploited, challenges in investigations and prosecutions, case studies of successful disruptions); the second to the private sector (thoughts on TBML and activities improving risk understanding or identification); the third by the Egmont IEWG to gather FIU case studies, experiences, challenges, best practices and risk indicators. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The second questionnaire targeted the private sector to elicit its thoughts on TBML"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Other processes: reiterating key learning points of previous FATF reports, mainly the APG's 2012 report, with updated explanations of 'processes of trade finance'; review and refinement of previous 'red flag' indicators using the questionnaires and engagement with public and private firms; and a limited open-source literature review including the WCO-Egmont Customs-FIU Cooperation Handbook and the 2019 Trade Finance Principles paper co-authored by the Wolfsberg Group, ICC and BAFT. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The review and refinement of previous ‘red flag’ indicators"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The sectors and commodities identified reflect those seen by questionnaire respondents in TBML investigations; as one respondent noted, OCGs, PMLs and terrorist financiers will exploit any sector, commodity or service where they perceive an opportunity. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "will exploit any sector, commodity, or service where they perceive an opportunity"</sub>

## Defining trade-based money laundering and trade-based terrorist financing

- **descriptive / context**: Trade exploitation lets OCGs, PMLs and terrorist financiers frustrate identification and intervention by authorities and FIs, and can support other illicit flows including capital flight, sanctions evasion, customs violations and tax evasion. The section reaffirms the FATF TBML definition, suggests a practical TBTF definition, explains how TBML differs from trade-related predicate offences by focusing on intent, and describes the basics of trade financing techniques exploited. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "capital flight, sanctions evasion, customs violations, and tax evasion"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The 2006 FATF report defines TBML as "the process of disguising the proceeds of crime and moving value through the use of trade transactions" to legitimise illegal origin or finance activities. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the process of disguising the proceeds of crime and moving value through the use of trade transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The primary aim of any TBML scheme is deliberate movement of illicit proceeds through trade transactions; criminals may also prepare false invoices, mischaracterize goods to circumvent controls, and commit customs and tax violations. Unlike trade-related predicate offences, the aim of TBML is not movement of goods but movement of money, which the trade transactions facilitate. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the primary aim of any TBML scheme is the deliberate movement of illicit proceeds through the exploitation of trade transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A key distinction of TBML is the involvement of PMLs. Criminals committing trade-related predicate offences are usually the ultimate beneficiaries of proceeds, whereas PMLs offer specialist expertise and a range of ML techniques (e.g. TBML) to diversify risk exposure: they receive proceeds on behalf of the OCG, transfer or convert them, including via TBML schemes, and pass them back minus their fee or commission. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "passing them back to the OCG, minus the payment of their fee or commission"</sub>

## Defining trade-based terrorist financing

- **descriptive / finding or result**: TBTF uses the same trade processes as TBML but with a fundamental difference: proceeds or value moved can come from both legitimate and illegitimate sources, increasing complexity in detecting and disrupting TBTF. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "proceeds or value moved can come from both legitimate and illegitimate sources"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: While noting additional complexity in detecting TBTF, the referenced case studies and other material identify some aspects of TBTF that may help authorities strengthen their understanding of TBTF schemes (the excerpt ends here). (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identifies some aspects of TBTF that may help authorities to strengthen their understanding of TBTF schemes"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The report defines TBTF as the disguised movement of value through trade transactions in an attempt to finance terrorism, where the value may come from either legitimate or illegitimate sources. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "disguising the movement of value through the use of trade transactions in an attempt to finance terrorism"</sub>

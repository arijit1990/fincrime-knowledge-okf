## Domestic co-ordination and co-operation

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Previous studies highlighted collaboration between national authorities as one of the most prominent issues in countering TBML, and inputs for this report show lack of collaboration remains one of the largest concerns, hindering TBML detection and investigation. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a lack of collaboration remains one of the largest concerns across the authorities"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Investigative authorities focus on predicate offences and de-prioritise ML including TBML, especially where it is not their primary function; e.g. police may prioritise predicate offences, tax authorities tax fraud and customs authorities commercial trade fraud and smuggling, with ML a lower priority. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "investigative authorities focus on predicate offences and de-prioritise investigations into ML"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Because TBML exploits trade system vulnerabilities, some elements resemble other trade-based crimes, which may lead authorities to mischaracterize a scheme as smuggling or fraud; e.g. on finding documentation discrepancies they may stop the shipment and charge the carrier with customs fraud or intellectual property violations without examining whether it is TBML. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "mischaracterization of the identified scheme by authorities as smuggling or fraud, instead of TBML"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The report states that the FATF Recommendations require jurisdictions to ensure their investigators of predicate offences are either able to investigate associated ML themselves or able to refer the case to another agency for a follow-up parallel financial investigation. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "investigators of predicate offences are either able to investigate associated ML themselves"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report says domestic co-ordination and cooperation is recognised in the FATF Recommendations as one of the fundamental pillars of an effective AML/CFT regime. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "domestic co-ordination and cooperation is one of the fundamental pillars of an effective AML/CFT regime"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The report states that the FATF Recommendations require policymakers, FIUs, LEAs, supervisors and other relevant competent authorities to have effective mechanisms in place to cooperate and, where appropriate, coordinate and exchange information with one another; this ensures jurisdictions can link authorities that collect, analyse and store different types of data with those investigating predicate offences and ML. (p. 40)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "authorities have effective mechanisms in place to cooperate, and, where appropriate,"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Many respondents cited lack of information sharing between national authorities, or ineffective mechanisms, as a key challenge. Per FATF and FSRB mutual evaluations, many assessed jurisdictions have legal frameworks allowing exchange of financial intelligence between authorities including FIU and LEAs, but low effectiveness of cooperation in some cases does not allow sufficient detection, investigation and confiscation of proceeds. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "low effectiveness of such co-operation does not always allow authorities to detect and investigate ML"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Analysing and combining tax, trade and financial data is another frequent challenge: investigators need to match large volumes of data from sources held by different agencies (customs data, STRs, criminal records), each in internal IT systems, possibly without timely sharing mechanisms or automated cross-referencing, requiring manual cross-checking. STR and import/export data may be in unstructured formats or have quality and consistency issues. (p. 41)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may be no information sharing mechanism allowing timely access to this data by other agencies, LEAs, or the FIU"</sub>

## International co-operation

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Timely international co-operation is crucial for crimes beyond one jurisdiction and particularly for TBML, as launderers often use front companies registered in one jurisdiction and transfer funds and ship goods between others; the predicate offence often occurs in a different jurisdiction from the TBML, so prosecution relies on working international channels to establish that the predicate offence took place. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions would heavily rely on effective and working international co-operation channels"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Jurisdictions still experience ineffective information sharing; one challenge is responding to a foreign request without significant delay, sometimes because domestic co-operation in the requested jurisdiction is poor and required information is held by different authorities. When goods are seized at another jurisdiction's request, the importer must pay holding and storage costs in proportion to the seizure period, an extra financial burden. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "requires the importer to pay the additional costs of holding and storing the goods"</sub>

## Investigation and prosecution

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Per FATF's 2018 Professional Money Laundering report, TBML is one of the methods most preferred by PMLs, often combined with front companies, front men and other techniques (see joint FATF/Egmont 'Concealment of Beneficial Ownership' report in footnote 21). In professional ML networks one group launders while others commit predicate offences, so predicate investigators (e.g. anti-drug trafficking units, anticorruption agencies) may lack expertise to investigate the ML, and where domestic co-operation is weak criminals are likely charged only with predicate crimes and trade-based crimes such as VAT-fraud and smuggling. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "TBML is one the methods most preferred by PMLs, and is often combined with the use of front companies"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Prosecuting TBML requires proving the laundered funds or assets are crime proceeds and that the defendant knew it. Prosecutors may prove how proceeds were converted and transferred, but the knowledge element can be hard to meet, especially for third-party ML or where the predicate offence was committed in another jurisdiction (the defendant may claim unawareness of illicit origin). (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the knowledge requirement can be particularly hard to meet, even using the factual circumstances of the case as evidence"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Where criminals also misuse legitimate trade operations, illicit and legitimate funds are often mixed, making laundered property hard to identify; with the general lack of TBML knowledge, this often results in prosecution of predicate offences only while TBML remains out of focus. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "illicit and legitimate funds are often mixed together, creating additional challenges for the authorities"</sub>

## Challenges from the private sector perspective

- **descriptive / finding or result**: FIs and DNFBPs are often at the forefront as they move value or hold unique knowledge of clients' finances (e.g. accountants and lawyers), but must keep pace with improving criminal methods. Most trade and production chain companies do not have similar FATF reporting obligations (unless within defined business activities such as dealers in precious metals or stones) and often fall outside domestic frameworks, placing the onus on FIs and DNFBPs to detect and report via STR in a timely manner. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "most trade and production chain companies do not have similar reporting obligations under the FATF Recommendations"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Most private sector respondents, mainly FIs, consider TBML the hardest type of ML to detect. It is highly adaptive and can exploit any sector or commodity, making it hard to prioritize resources and translate insights into business rules and compliance systems; schemes can use many front companies with funds moving between several banks, so each FI sees only part of the network, limiting its ability to analyse the whole chain and detect discrepancies in supplementary documentation and customer profiles. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "consider TBML as the hardest type of ML activity to detect"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Verifying customer-provided information is another FI challenge; lack of a public registry makes verifying address, income or other data hard, and TBML worsens this. When a client submits invoice, contract or other documents to justify a cross-border transfer, a bank without access to customs data cannot promptly verify document authenticity, whether goods shipped, or whether quantity and description match the contract. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "If the bank has problems accessing customs data, it will not be able to promptly verify"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report says these measures are designed to limit criminals' ability to launder funds and other assets and finance terrorism, while ensuring FIs and DNFBPs have sufficient tools to protect themselves from misuse; even when a criminal tries to use their services, the tools should allow them to establish suspicion and promptly report it to the FIU. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These measures are designed to limit the ability of criminals to launder funds and other assets and finance terrorism"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Many TBML techniques need buyer and seller complicity, sometimes with one person or group controlling both. Beneficial owner information on counterparties may help an FI detect TBML but may be unavailable, e.g. if the counterparty never was the FI's customer, no public beneficial ownership registry exists, or the counterparty is incorporated in another jurisdiction. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "obtaining information about the beneficial owners of the customer’s counterparties may assist the FI in detecting TBML"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Estimating the 'fair price' of a traded commodity is a recurring FI challenge, especially for over-/under-pricing techniques: FIs often have only a vague description of the good, establishing fair price can need significant resources and rely on open source information, and some commodities are not publicly traded so no benchmark prices exist. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Another reoccurring challenge faced by FIs in identifying TBML schemes is estimating the “fair price” of a traded commodity."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Because TBML involves international trade, documents reach FIs in different formats and languages, so verification is likely manual, requiring extra time and resources including highly qualified staff, which is harder for smaller FIs with limited compliance budgets. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "documentation provided to FIs is often in different formats and languages, meaning that verification will likely be done manually"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Letters of credit and documentary collection, which require more customer documentation than open account trade, are often seen by the private sector as less vulnerable to TBML, so launderers may find open account more attractive given FI's limited oversight. Even when an FI suspects TBML and terminates the relationship, the customer may open a new account at another bank. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the customer may still be able to open a new account in another bank"</sub>

## Increasing the understanding of trade-based money laundering

- **expected / expectation**: Building sufficient knowledge of TBML schemes should be the foundation of any strategy against it. For the public sector this often means identifying and assessing TBML risks in the jurisdiction, including economic sectors and financial instruments involved, so public and private actors can focus resources and adjust the broader ML strategy. Lack of knowledge is a common challenge for the private sector (which may lack resources to follow developments) and for public authorities. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "building a sufficient level of knowledge of TBML schemes should be the foundation of any strategy"</sub>

## Increasing the understanding of trade-based money laundering

- **descriptive / limitation**: The NRA is one source for understanding TBML as it summarises available ML/TF risk information, sometimes by sector. Although it should reflect the full spectrum of a jurisdiction's TBML risks, it is likely intended for experts with some TBML knowledge, and in some countries it may not be public, so additional effort may be needed to make NRA content available and understandable to a broader audience. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is likely intended for experts who already have some understanding and knowledge of TBML"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The paper does not give specific guidance on how to identify and assess TBML risks, but regional initiatives have raised TBML knowledge, from 'basic' knowledge focused on FI customers to advanced expert training. They can be driven by bodies such as: a private sector entity raising customer awareness to minimise the likelihood of involvement in TBML; FIUs or authorities raising the level and quality of STR reporting from vulnerable sectors; and international bodies providing guidance or enhancing cooperation with the private sector. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a number of initiatives in various regions have focused on increasing TBML knowledge and improving risk understanding"</sub>

## Box 4.1. Dutch bank initiative

- **descriptive / case example**: In the Netherlands, an FI gave all business customers a flyer raising awareness of TBML risks, including an example TBML scheme and an email address for customers' TBML questions (source: the Netherlands). (p. 46)[^original]

## Increasing the understanding of trade-based money laundering

- **descriptive / case example**: In 2019 the FIU of Germany guided DNFBPs and other regulated entities (e.g. auto traders and art/antiquities traders) through regular lectures and engagement via the chamber of industry and trade, expected to continue in 2020 subject to COVID-19 restrictions. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FIU of Germany provided guidance to DNFBPs and other regulated entities"</sub>

## Box 4.2. Co-operation between Financial Intelligence Units and Designated Non-Financial Businesses and Professions

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The German FIU guidance led to a significant increase in registration applications of obliged entities with the FIU and better quality of questions raised at trade fairs and lectures, indicating improved ML risk understanding. After registering on its website, DNFBPs receive typologies and other ML prevention and STR reporting information; close dialogue with associations helped reach unregistered goods traders (source: Germany). (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a significant increase in the number of registration applications"</sub>

## Box 4.3 Raising financial institutions’ knowledge of TBML

- **descriptive / case example**: Singapore's AML/CFT Industry Partnership (ACIP) brings together industry, regulators, LEAs and government entities to assess and mitigate key ML, TF and proliferation financing risks. A working group on TBML (banks focused on trade finance, regulators, LEA, professional services) produced an industry best practice paper on managing TBML/trade financing risks, with preventive measures and training tips. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "One of ACIP’s working groups focuses on TBML risks"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: ACIP best-practice training tips: FIs should provide staff relevant, specific and targeted training to detect and prevent TBML and mitigate ML/TF/PF risks and comply with regulation; FIs should train and disseminate information to relevant staff on significant regulatory changes and new TBML risks and typologies; training should be refreshed periodically as determined by the FI's risk assessment, aligned with policies and procedures, and consider circumstances unique to the FI such as products, operating locations and customer types (source: Singapore). (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Training should be refreshed periodically, as determined by the FI’s risk assessment"</sub>

## Box 4.4. Collection of TBML case studies

- **descriptive / case example**: In July 2020 the Egmont IEWG endorsed a Case Book from the project 'Conclusions from large scale cross-border money laundering schemes', which pools FIU insights from past 'Laundromats' to develop approaches to identify networks, patterns and indicators of such schemes. Since FIUs saw criminals widely applying TBML techniques, case collection focused on TBML; more than 20 FIUs contributed. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "more than 20 FIUs contributed to this work stream"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Case Book is available to FIUs on the Egmont Group's secure website; other domestic competent authorities and selected reporting entities can access it via their FIU. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Other domestic competent authorities and selected reporting entities can access it via their respective FIU."</sub>

## Box 4.5. Europol Financial Intelligence Public Private Partnership (EFIPPP)

- **descriptive / case example**: EFIPPP is described as the first transnational AML/CFT information sharing mechanism, launched December 2017 as a pilot of the Europol-Institute of International Finance High Level Project. It aims to create a platform for information exchange among trusted partners across national and sectorial lines and provide practical input (e.g. typologies) to refine suspicious transaction reporting, moving from a compliance-led to an intelligence-led approach. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "moving from a compliance-led to an intelligence-led approach"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: EFIPPP brings together LEAs, FIUs and/or regulators from 11 EU member states and 4 non-EU countries, 25 financial institutions with an international footprint, EU institutions, one international organisation and civil society and research observers; it grew from 15 financial institutions and 6 countries in 2017 to 25 FIs and 15 countries. A new governance model was recently adopted; members meet quarterly and in working groups and exchange via a dedicated expert platform. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "from 15 financial institutions and 6 participating countries in 2017 to 25 financial institutions and 15 countries today"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: EFIPPP's past TBML typologies were linked to drug trafficking and VAT fraud, focusing on the interrelation between drug offences (production, cultivation, smuggling, distribution), ML and tax fraud (source: Europol). (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Past work on TBML in typologies drafted by the EFIPPP has been linked to drug trafficking and VAT fraud"</sub>

## Box 4.6. ADB experience with TBML

- **descriptive / case example**: In 2019 the Asian Development Bank (ADB) published a brief effective practices paper on AML/CFT compliance in trade finance operations. ADB also works with public and private stakeholders using the APG 2012 proposals to address quality and integration of trade data; a March 2019 Singapore AML/CFT workshop produced trade data points to feed public-private feedback loops and improve STR quality, possibly including specificity on the TBML technique, key information on counterparties, and transaction and transport details. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "trade data points that could be factored into existing or emerging feedback loops between the public and private sector"</sub>

## Financial intelligence collected by financial intelligence units

- **expected / expectation**: As central hubs of financial intelligence, FIUs can combine data from several sources to detect and analyse TBML and disseminate intelligence nationally and internationally. Because FIU analysis often triggers further public action, it is crucially important that FIUs have a well-developed understanding of TBML and sufficient resources; sources and analysis resemble other ML/TF cases but have TBML-specific aspects. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is crucially important that FIUs have a well-developed understanding of TBML and sufficient resources"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The foundation of FIU data is STRs from FIs and DNFBPs; their quality and accuracy directly affect the quality and timeliness of FIU analysis. For TBML, an STR describing the suspicious financial transaction(s), parties and basis of suspicion, plus insight into the corresponding trade activity, may boost analysis. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the quality and accuracy of the information contained in these reports have a direct impact on the quality and timeliness"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: FIs are well placed to give leads on TBML given their trade finance involvement, knowledge of customer behaviour and intermediary role; in trade finance they often have direct access to underlying documents and may have incentive for enhanced due diligence when funding or guaranteeing a client. With open account trade and correspondent banking they generally have less client information but can detect anomalies such as transactions differing from normal behaviour and other unusual patterns. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs often have direct access to documentation underlying the trade transactions"</sub>

## Box 4.6. ADB experience with TBML

(p. 51)[^original]

## Infographic 4.1. Categories of reports submitted by FIs to FIUs

- **descriptive / data or feature**: Infographic 4.1 gives three categories of FI reports: (1) reports relating to trade finance, where the FI is directly involved and has access to underlying documents, detecting e.g. irregularities in documents or in customer/counterparty activity or information; (2) reports on open account settlements, where underlying documents are not generally available but the FI detects irregularities in the customer's trade-related financial activities, transactions or counterparties; (3) reports on trade transactions in correspondent banking, where the FI has no access to underlying documents or customer information but detects irregularities on cross-border transactions, including those allegedly for goods and trade, and/or suspicious parties potentially related to previously identified ML networks (source: Egmont IEWG). (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Reports on trade transactions in correspondent banking"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: STRs from DNFBPs, especially notaries, auditors and accountants, are another valuable TBML source: their professional experience positions them to recognise companies used solely to hide the original form of payment and other complex legal structures; STRs on such corporate structures are of significant value as front and shell companies appear in a wide range of TBML techniques. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "STRs submitted by DNFBPs, especially notaries, auditors, and accountants, can be another source of valuable information"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Some FIUs also receive STRs from other sectors such as commercial entities in international trade, useful especially from sectors vulnerable to TBML; but although increased reporting could substantially help detection, it could not substitute for the insufficient DNFBP reporting noted by FIUs in some jurisdictions. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "such measures could not substitute the insufficient level of reporting from DNFBPs noted by FIUs in some jurisdictions"</sub>

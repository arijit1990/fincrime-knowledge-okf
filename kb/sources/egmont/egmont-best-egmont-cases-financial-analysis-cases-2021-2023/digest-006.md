## CASE 10

  <sub>anchor: "was extradited from Guatemala to the United States for the crime of DRUG TRAFFICKING"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: UAFE received a warning signal that began analysis of the people under investigation. It extracted data from the UAFE database in the System for the Prevention of Money Laundering and Financing of Terrorism (SISLAFT), identifying originators and beneficiaries of related transactions: cash and cheque deposits, cashed paid cheques, and transfers received and sent locally. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Information was extracted from the UAFE database that appears in the System for the Prevention of Money Laundering"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The FIU searched on an alert signal for Mr. Luargas García (Guatemalan) and his spouse Mrs. Mejía Loor Aída Ximena (Ecuadorian). A few days after his arrival in Ecuador in 2011 he received a USD 360,000 transfer from the United States, of which USD 258,000 was sent by transfers and cheques payable to Mr. Parrales Panchana Segundo Marcos. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "he received a transfer from the United States for (USD) 360,000"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Once the banks holding the subjects' accounts were identified, the FIU requested account statements, legality of funds, images of cash and cheque deposits, paid cheques and SWIFT messages of transfers received and sent abroad, and required notaries to provide deeds for the subjects' acquisitions of goods. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Once the banks where the analyzed subjects registered accounts were identified"</sub>

  <sub>anchor: "purchased a vehicle for (USD) 38,835, paid in several cash payments"</sub>

## CASE 11

- **descriptive / case example**: Case 11 (Fiji FIU): a father and son, Tallat Rahman and Joshua Aziz Rahman, were in a joint criminal enterprise importing, exporting and distributing controlled drugs across multiple jurisdictions; law enforcement in several jurisdictions ran parallel investigations of the enterprise importing methamphetamine and cocaine into New Zealand. The Fiji FIU received an information request from the Fiji Police Force's Criminal Investigation Department for financial background on them and their associates. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "involved in a joint criminal enterprise of importation, exportation and distribution of controlled drugs across multiple jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The operation was initiated by the New Zealand Police Force's National Organized Crime Group to establish links of members of an organized crime group in New Zealand, and also involved the Fiji Police Force and other regional law enforcement. From late 2018 to early 2019 Tallat Rahman was identified as a target member of an enterprise importing methamphetamine and cocaine and organizing delivery into New Zealand. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identified as a target member of a criminal enterprise tasked with importing methamphetamine and cocaine"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Fiji FIU preliminary findings: Tallat Rahman was sole owner of registered Company A (incorporated November 24, 2017); he received FJ 200,000 into a local law firm trust account from a Hong Kong company on December 5, 2017, narrated as in trust for him; on December 21, 2017 he moved FJ 40,000 from the trust account into Company A, and the funds were further transferred to an entity in Mexico. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Tallat Rahman was the sole owner of a registered"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Company A received a shipment of various homeware items from the entity in Mexico on January 18, 2018; the items were stored at the residential property leased to Tallat Rahman in Suva, where the drugs were later discovered. Tallat Rahman and a local individual also shared ownership of registered Company D, incorporated in 2012. (p. 48)[^original]

## CASE 11

- **descriptive / case example**: Between 2014 and 2015 Joshua Aziz Rahman sent approximately FJ 61,000 from Canada into a local law firm trust account, narrated as for Tallat Rahman and Company D. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Joshua Aziz Rahman sent approximately FJ 61,000"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In December 2018 the father and son came under New Zealand Police attention after being seen delivering a bag of cash to a member of the joint enterprise for what seemed to be a sale of methamphetamine. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "delivering a bag of cash to a member of the joint enterprise"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: On February 8, 2019 Tallat Rahman travelled to New Zealand and was arrested and charged with importing methamphetamine in relation to a consignment delivered from the USA on February 4, 2019. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "On February 8, 2019, Tallat Rahman travelled to New Zealand and was arrested by the New Zealand Police"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The next day Joshua Rahman came under the Fiji Police Joint Transnational Serious Organised Crime Taskforce radar after being seen with three targeted individuals allegedly involved in transnational drug shipments, leading to a search warrant at the Suva residence on February 14, 2019. Police seized 39.5 kg of cocaine (average purity 81%) valued at approximately FJ 39.5 million (USD 18.3 million); Joshua was the sole occupant of the leased property. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "discovered and seized 39.5 kg of cocaine (average purity of 81%)"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: On receiving the information request on February 14, 2019, the Fiji FIU began tactical analysis tracing proceeds of crime, drawing initial intelligence from its online database and external databases accessible via memoranda of understanding, and sent several information requests to foreign FIUs whose information gave key intelligence on the syndicate. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Fiji FIU began tactical analysis in tracing the proceeds of crime"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Sharing real-time Fiji FIU information with law enforcement during the operation enabled investigators and prosecutors to identify suspected proceeds of crime in Tallat Rahman's account. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "enabled the investigators and prosecutors to identify suspected proceeds of crime in Tallat Rahman’s account"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Funds moving into Tallat Rahman's personal and business accounts in 2017 to 2018 from two major entities in Hong Kong and Mexico established transnational links. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "from two major entities in Hong Kong and Mexico established transnational links"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The Fiji FIU issued a temporary restriction notice on bank accounts of Tallat Rahman, Joshua Aziz Rahman and related entities on February 20, 2019; issued a formal information dissemination report to the Fiji Police Force on February 28, 2019; and presented its case findings to the police the next day. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "issued a temporary restriction notice on bank accounts associated with Tallat Rahman"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The restraining order was not sufficient to conclude that the remaining funds were part of the proceeds of crime: the initial funds from the Hong Kong company had been spent, and financial institution information showed Tallat Rahman received a cheque deposit of FJ 118,118.30 from his share of his father's estate. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "that the remaining funds were part of the proceeds of crime."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Multilateral investigations, including information sharing between the New Zealand Police Force, Fiji Police Force and other local and foreign law enforcement, contributed to successful prosecution of Tallat and Joshua Rahman. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "contributed to the successful prosecution of Tallat Rahman and Joshua Aziz Rahman"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Tallat Rahman was sentenced on July 6, 2020 by the New Zealand District Court in Auckland to 16 years for importing a Class A controlled drug (Misuse of Drugs Act 1975, s 6(1)(a) and 6(2)(a)), conspiracy to import a Class A drug (s 6(1)(a) and 6(2A)), and participating in an organised criminal group (Crimes Act 1961, s 98A). (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "sentenced to 16 years of imprisonment for the following offences"</sub>

## CASE 12

- **descriptive / case example**: Case 12 (Peru FIU): the FIU's investigation dismantled a criminal organization that introduced illicit funds into the financial system to launder them. Peru's judiciary validated the freezing of funds prompted by the FIU and ordered preventive detention of those involved, as requested by the Public Prosecutor's Office. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Peru’s judiciary validated the freezing of funds prompted by the FIU Peru"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The organization allegedly sold olive oil through three front companies, which in about 18 months received wire transfers and significant money from many countries without commercial reason. The FIU detected that the three companies' bank accounts were operated from abroad and that the beneficial owners were being investigated abroad for drug trafficking. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the bank accounts of these three companies were operated from abroad"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Peruvian shareholders failed to provide information on the leasing of offices in an exclusive financial sector of Lima, purportedly for administering the group of companies; the FIU found other related companies sharing the same address and shareholders, including individuals from Peru, Colombia and Venezuela. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "failed to provide information regarding leasing of offices in an exclusive financial sector of Lima"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Individuals from Colombia and Venezuela made multiple entries into Peru on the same dates as the wire transfers occurred, and to the countries involved in the inflow and outflow of funds. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "made multiple entries to Peru on the same date as the wire transfers occurred"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The gathered information supported a first Financial Intelligence Report that led to freezing of approximately USD 3,000,000 in funds. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "leading to the freezing of approximately (USD) 3,000,000 in funds"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The investigation began after a warning from the US DEA to the FIU Peru about a drug trafficking organization using front companies A, B and C. Between April 2019 and December 2020 they received 1,317 wire transfers totalling USD 100,641,076 and executed 1,157 wire transfers totalling USD 98,256,398. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "those companies received 1,317 wire transfers for a total amount of (USD) 100,641,076"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Companies A, B and C engaged in no international trade activity and reported zero sales or income to the Tax Authority, and mainly conducted domestic transfers within the local financial system; the scheme aimed to evade detection by keeping an incongruent balance between recorded income and tax information. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "reported zero sales or income to the Tax Authority"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Financial-sector reporting entities then submitted new STRs, enabling a complementary Financial Intelligence Report that identified new alerts, new suspects, related Companies D and E, and the entire money flow route. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Reporting Entities from the financial sector then submitted new Suspicious Transaction Reports (STRs)."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The FIU Peru coordinated with national money laundering prevention system stakeholders; swift collaboration with the Public Prosecutor's Office and Judiciary was paramount in freezing funds and enabled the Judiciary to validate the freezing within 48 hours, and further coordination helped provide evidence when requested, particularly for preventive detention. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "validate the freezing of the funds within 48 hours"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Financial-sector reporting entities played a crucial role by providing information and documentary evidence of transactions, which helped validate suspicions promptly. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "They provided information and documentary evi"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The allegedly illicit funds injected by the sponsoring lawyer revealed several typologies: receipt and remittance of overseas transfers to companies unrelated to the three companies' legitimate commercial activity; use of accounts of the same company across different financial institutions that lay dormant then suddenly showed significant activity without justification; transfers to and from abroad involving recently created companies with diverse economic activities in countries deemed high-risk; substantial fund movement without corresponding declaration of sales and/or income to tax authorities; receipt and transmission of international transfers without registration of foreign trade activity with customs; and overseas management of local accounts to administer funds of illicit origin. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "revealed the utilization of various money laundering typologies"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The DEA warning concerned front companies A, B and C conducting numerous wire transfers primarily originating from Hong Kong, North American country 1 and North American country 2, directed abroad mainly to South American country 1 and Central American country 1, with domestic transfers between the companies; the scheme aimed to conceal the true balance between recorded income and tax information. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "primarily originating from Hong Kong, North American country 1, North American country 2"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Case 12 indicator: individuals set up multiple front companies to inject illicit funds from abroad and high-risk countries into the Peruvian financial system; the funds, believed to come from drug trafficking, were laundered locally before being sent abroad, primarily to Colombia and Panama, seeking higher returns through investment companies there. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Individuals established multiple front companies to inject illicit funds from abroad"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Case 12 indicator: Companies A to E (D and E found through the FIU's analysis) failed to register economic information or capital increases consistent with recorded operations; their often foreign shareholders and/or representatives also failed to report consistent economic information; and the beneficiaries of funds abroad in commercial activities differed from those of the local companies. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Companies A, B, C, D and E (the final two obtained through the FIU’s analysis)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Judicial authorities froze funds and seized assets totalling USD 3,000,000 and ordered preventive detention of those involved; proceedings include money laundering charges and were expanded to new individuals. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Judicial authorities froze their funds and seized their assets, totaling (USD) 3,000,000"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Case 12 indicator: the local companies did not engage in foreign trade, and the ordering and beneficiary companies of the transfers had different commercial activities, offering no apparent justification. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The local companies did not engage in any foreign trade activities."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Judiciary's restrictive measures (freezing funds, preventive detention) are said to underscore the coordination and functioning of Peru's money-laundering prevention system; financial-system reporting entities promptly reported suspicious transactions, helping the FIU determine where the illicit funds went and compile a complementary second report. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Reporting Entities within the financial system promptly reported suspicious transactions involving the individuals concerned."</sub>

## Fraud & Embezzlement

- **descriptive / context**: Fraud causes individuals and organizations each year to lose savings, jobs and investments. Embezzlement, closely associated with fraud, is the illegal appropriation of public and private assets for personal gain. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Embezzlement, which is closely associated with fraud, involves the illegal appropriation of public and private assets for personal gain."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Embezzlement is said to cause inefficient distribution of goods and services, including misallocated life-saving medical supplies, substandard public infrastructure, misused funds for those in need and environmental deterioration. Unlike robbery, fraud is a deliberate act of deception or trickery to acquire wealth from individuals or companies. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "fraud is a deliberate act of deception or trickery aimed at acquiring wealth from individuals or companies"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Fraud takes forms such as online sales scams, website manipulation, pyramid schemes, charity, employment and payroll schemes, identity theft, credit-card and debt-elimination scams, and fraudulent contracts and procurement practices; consequences of fraud and embezzlement cited include collapse of bridges and buildings, contaminated water and air, and harmful food and medicine. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "online sales scams, website manipulation, pyramid schemes and other types of schemes involving charities"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The global pandemic created a fertile environment for an unprecedented surge in fraud as governments urgently procured PPE, tests and vaccines and provided financial relief. Fraudsters collaborated with corrupt public- and private-sector officials to divert pandemic-response resources, and much of the misappropriated money entered the global financial system, prompting FIUs worldwide to trace funds and analyze new fraud and embezzlement typologies. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Fraudsters collaborated with corrupt public- and private-sector officials to divert resources meant for pandemic response"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The cases illustrate the transnational nature of corruption and fraud and how criminals move quickly between illicit schemes, hiding gains in multiple jurisdictions; effective collaboration among law enforcement, financial regulators and public-private partnerships is described as vital, allowing the sectors to assess risks, detect illicit activity and recover stolen assets. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Effective collaboration among law enforcement agencies, financial regulators and public-private partnerships is vital"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The examples are fraud and embezzlement cases detected by FIUs over the past four years, covering financial statement fraud, misappropriation of assets, cash skimming and larceny, misuse of company resources, theft of intellectual property and trade secrets, consumer fraud, insurance and procurement fraud; the embezzlement cases include abuse of office for personal or third-party gain, misappropriation and diversion of public property, and trading in influence. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "financial statement fraud, misappropriation of assets, cash skimming and larceny"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Fraud and embezzlement indicators: rapid, consecutive money transfers to another account shortly after deposit; several international transfers to the same beneficiary in a single day; international transfers of substantial amounts. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Rapid and consecutive money transfers to another account shortly after deposit."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Fraud and embezzlement indicators: unjustified movement of funds to countries known for opaque financial-sector regulation or high perceived corruption; U-turn transactions (funds sent out of a country and some brought back into it); difficulty verifying customer identification details; a series of low-value international transfers. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "U-turn transactions, involving the transfer of funds out of a country and then bringing some"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Fraud and embezzlement indicators: unnecessarily complex corporate ownership structuring and concealed beneficial ownership; transfers to recipients in countries where they lack a valid economic or financial reason to hold a bank account; schemes promising abnormally high investment returns; multiple customers sending international transfers to the same beneficiary abroad; transfers to companies owned by relatives or associates of politically exposed individuals. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Unnecessarily complex corporate ownership structuring and concealed beneficial ownership."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: A following case heading (Italy UIF and Hungary's FIU) concerns cash withdrawals of approximately EUR 37 million in Italy from Hungarian shell companies for the purpose of tax evasion; the excerpt ends at the heading. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Cash withdrawals of approximately (EUR) 37 million in Italy from Hungarian shell companies for the purpose of tax evasion"</sub>

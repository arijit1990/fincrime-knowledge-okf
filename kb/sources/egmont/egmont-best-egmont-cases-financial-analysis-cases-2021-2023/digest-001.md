## INTRODUCTION

- **descriptive / context**: The Best Egmont Case Award (BECA) annual competition was established in 2011 by the Training Working Group, now the Technical Assistance and Training Working Group (TATWG); its primary goal is to inspire Egmont members to contribute to the Egmont Group's database on ML/TF cases, benefiting FIUs and AML/CFT stakeholders. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "established in 2011 by the Training Working Group"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The competition starts with a call letter from the BECA Champion inviting all Egmont member FIUs to submit cases; there is no limit on the number of cases an FIU can present, and FIUs can submit independently or jointly. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there are no restrictions on the number of cases that an FIU member can present"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: After the submission period a panel of four to six judges assesses entries against predefined criteria to identify the top two cases; finalist FIUs present at the plenary where Heads of FIU vote for the most outstanding case, and the winner receives the BECA trophy. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a panel of four to six judges assesses all entries against a predefined set of criteria"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The second BECA publication covered the best submissions from 2014 to 2020: in June 2020 a subgroup of 12 FIUs was formed to review submissions, balancing regional representation and diversity of predicate offenses, and from 117 submissions selected 26 best cases in seven categories. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "From 117 case submissions, the subgroup selected 26 best cases"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The first BECA Book, published in 2015, contained 22 best case submissions from 2011 to 2013 and has become a valuable reference for case studies and best practices of FIUs globally. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "containing 22 best case submissions between 2011 and 2013"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In October 2023 a team of nine FIUs assessed submissions for the third BECA book, balancing regional representation and predicate offenses; from 78 submissions it chose 24 top cases in six categories: 1. Bribery, Corruption and Tax Evasion; 2. Cybercrime, Virtual Assets and Child Pornography; 3. Drug Smuggling and Gambling; 4. Fraud and Embezzlement; 5. Trade-Based and Third-Party Money Laundering; 6. Terrorism, Human Trafficking and Organized Crime. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Out of 78 submissions, the team chose 24 top cases"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In 2020, the 10th anniversary of the BECAs, the competition went ahead despite the pandemic, with 24 case submissions from 19 FIUs, and for the first and last time the winner was chosen by virtual voting. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There were 24 case submissions from 19 FIUs"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Case submissions 2021-2023 (year, cases/FIUs, finalists, winner): 2021, 24 cases from 17 FIUs, finalists UIF Italy with FIU Hungary and IMPA (FIU-Israel), winner IMPA; 2022, 32 cases from 20 FIUs, finalists UIF (FIU-Italy) and FID (FIU-Latvia), winner FID; 2023, 22 cases from 12 FIUs, finalists PPATK (FIU-Indonesia) and TRACFIN (FIU-France), winner PPATK. (p. 7)[^original]

## UNVEILING FINANCIAL CRIMES: INSIGHTS FROM EXCEPTIONAL FINANCIAL ANALYSES BY FIUS

- **descriptive / context**: The selected cases share a theme of exceptional financial analysis by FIUs: information often came from suspicious activity or transaction reports giving fragmented details, which analysts pieced together to reveal schemes with numerous transactions and layered corporate and legal structures, tracing multimillion-dollar frauds across countries and continents despite limited data. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIU analysts pieced together these fragments to reveal intricate schemes"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The case selection process evaluated the quality and range of analytical tools used and the clarity of planning, data collection, evaluation and presentation methods; the cases are meant to educate others about successful techniques. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the quality and range of analytical tools used, as well as the clarity of the planning"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Sharing the cases shows AML authorities which financial analysis tools worked and how they can be replicated or adapted locally; the cases show FIUs using investigative and analytical techniques and intelligence to gather evidence from many sources and build a fact-based narrative, leading to confiscation of stolen assets and punishment of offenders. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "which financial analysis tools were successful in uncovering crimes"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The publication states that no single approach to financial analysis fits every case. The cases show varied approaches, among them analysing open-source financial data, working with other FIUs, collecting data from the private sector, and drawing on criminal and financial information held by justice and regulatory bodies. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There is no one-size-fits-all approach to financial analysis."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The publication also serves as an educational tool for FIUs, financial regulators and the wider anti-money laundering community in both the public and private sectors. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This publication also serves as an educational tool for FIUs, financial regulators"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Some AML authorities and FIUs may meet these types of cases for the first time, so the cases help them prepare for similar schemes in their jurisdictions; adopting this hands-on mindset may help readers detect such schemes more easily and mitigate damages. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may encounter these types of money laundering cases for the first time"</sub>

## Bribery, Corruption and Tax Evasion

- **descriptive / context**: Bribery, corruption and tax evasion each threaten states worldwide; while globalization promotes economic transparency and unrestricted movement of capital, goods and labour, that openness also allows criminalization of national economies. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "that openness also allows for the criminalization of national economies"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Personal gain and consolidation and retention of power motivate these misdeeds, creating a vicious circle in which corrupt officials build a network where each person's involvement in bribery supports a corrupt structure; corruption undermines trust in governments and weakens the social contract. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Corruption undermines trust in governments and weakens the social contract."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The World Bank Group recognizes corruption as a significant obstacle to ending extreme poverty and promoting shared prosperity for the poorest 40 percent in developing countries; studies show the poor bear the brunt, often paying the highest percentage of income in bribes, and in some cases may be deliberately targeted for perceived inability to complain. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "poorest 40 percent of people in developing countries"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The global community is committed to combating corruption by raising standards for those in government roles and implementing regulations to improve the relationship between state institutions and the population. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "raising the standards for those employed by or appointed to governmental bodies"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Every embezzled or misused unit of currency denies the poor equal opportunities and prevents governments investing in human capital; in the public sector corruption goes beyond bribes for contracts or favouring acquaintances in approving projects. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "denies the poor equal opportunities in life and prevents governments from investing in their human capital"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Corrupt officials may intentionally delay bureaucratic processes to improve their chances of personal enrichment; lengthy queues or complex paperwork create incentives for citizens to offer bribes to jump the queue or bypass hurdles. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Corrupt officials may intentionally delay bureaucratic processes to increase their chances of personal enrichment."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Tax evasion is the illegal act of deliberately avoiding taxes owed by underreporting income, inflating deductions, hiding money or assets or other fraudulent means, intentionally deceiving tax authorities; it is a serious offense with penalties including fines, imprisonment and forfeiture of assets, and differs from tax avoidance, which uses legal methods to minimize tax liability. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Tax evasion refers to the illegal act of deliberately avoiding paying taxes owed to the government"</sub>

## Indicators

- **descriptive / context**: The factors described highlight the need to intensify the global fight against corruption, crucial for economic recovery, efficient use of taxpayers' money and restoring public trust; international organizations and governments have introduced ML measures targeting conditions that help legitimize illicit funds. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These factors highlight the need to intensify the global fight against corruption."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Corruption indicator: suspicious cash-based transactions, such as buying expensive items, paying off credit card debt, making large deposits into personal accounts and then transferring them to overseas accounts, or primarily using cash through intermediaries to hide the audit trail. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Suspicious cash-based transactions, such as buying expensive items, paying off credit card debt"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Corruption indicator: engaging in multiple financial transactions to obscure the source of funds. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Engaging in multiple financial transactions to obscure the source of funds."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Addressing corruption in government, political organizations or businesses often needs determined efforts against vested interests; transparency and open governance are seldom sufficient alone, and when public discontent reaches a tipping point the political benefits of acting often outweigh the costs of disrupting vested interests. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Addressing corruption—whether it’s within government ranks, political organizations or businesses"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Corruption indicator: using currency exchanges to send money abroad. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Using currency exchanges to send money abroad."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Corruption indicator: employing multiple offshore companies to hide the true owner and complicate the audit trail. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Employing multiple offshore companies to hide the true owner and complicate the audit trail."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Corruption indicator: justifying receipt of large sums through fraudulent contracts. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Justifying the receipt of large sums of money through the use of fraudulent contracts."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The section's examples show FIUs' vital role in uncovering sophisticated corruption schemes, including profiting from bankrupt individuals' misfortune, laundering bribes through complex offshore accounts, disguising multimillion-dollar pork barrel scams as NGOs, and using corrupt officials to secure government tenders and embezzle public funds. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "disguising multimillion-dollar pork barrel scams as non-governmental organizations"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Corruption indicator: embezzlement of state funds through fraudulent NGOs. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Embezzlement of state funds through fraudulent NGOs."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Corruption indicator: corrupt government officials receiving kickbacks or commissions for facilitating transfer of state funds. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Corrupt government officials receiving kickbacks or commissions for facilitating the transfer of state funds."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: To eradicate corruption it is necessary to establish strong, legal and democratic practices within states; absence of corruption is indicative of a society where most people are free, responsible and innovative. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "To eradicate corruption, it is necessary to establish strong, legal and democratic practices within states."</sub>

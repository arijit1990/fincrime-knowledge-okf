## CASE 20

- **descriptive / case example**: Case 20 (Tunisia CTAF) is described as trade-based money laundering, fraud, exploitation and misuse of advantages granted by the Tunisian state, and money smuggling. (p. 82)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This is a case of trade-based money laundering, fraud, exploitation and misuse of advantages"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: YIN, a Tunisian car dealership company created in the 1990s, was the exclusive importer and legal representative of a world-renowned car brand. The Tunisian Commerce Ministry allocated it an annual quota to settle foreign supplier invoices in foreign currencies under Tunisia's Foreign Exchange Regulations (which set the conditions for converting Tunisian Dinar), and YIN also benefited from a Flat-Rate Tax System. Its CEO was "Mr. Ahmed". (p. 82)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "YIN was thus allocated by the Tunisian Commerce Ministry an annual quota"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: YIN's car parts supplier YANG (in Country A) opted for a Cost and Freight rule for goods shipped to the Port of Rades (Tunisia). OCEANA was a sea carrier connected to a large multinational in Country B operating through subsidiaries worldwide. (p. 82)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "opted for a Cost and Freight rule for the goods transported to the Port of Rades (Tunisia)"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Transport costs negotiated between YIN and OCEANA were increased by 50% and incorporated into the invoices established by YANG. After imports were settled and transport costs paid, OCEANA transferred the increased amount to account XYZ opened in Country C and managed by VERA, a company providing quality management, trustee and consultancy services and company incorporation and domiciliation, located in Country D (described as one of criminals' favourite tax havens). (p. 82)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The transport costs negotiated between YIN and OCEANA increased by 50%"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Several credit notes issued by OCEANA for the benefit of YIN were addressed to a person named "Mrs. Helene". (p. 82)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Several credit notes issued from OCEANA for the benefit of YIN"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: CTAF sent several requests to the FIUs of Countries A, B, C and D. From the FIU of Country D (listed as written) it requested information on VERA and any economic or financial connection between YIN, Mr. Ahmed and VERA, and the total volume of transactions between OCEANA and YIN; from the FIU of Country C it requested information on account XYZ (origin and destination of funds) and on YIN and Mr. Ahmed. (p. 82)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Several requests were sent to the FIUs of Countries"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Financial analysis was conducted on accounts opened abroad, including access to the Tunisia Trade Network database, domestic cooperation with the General Directory of the Foreign Exchange Operations within the Central Bank of Tunisia, and OSINT. (p. 82)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This included accessing the Tunisia Trade Network database"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Once OCEANA collected its 100 percent freight, it transferred the 50% increase smuggled from Tunisia by seemingly issuing credit notes for Mrs. Helene, but the 50% actually went to FIDUCIA's account XYZ in Country C. In 2016 that 50% sum was no less than EUR three million. (p. 83)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In 2016, that 50% sum represented no less than (EUR) three million."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The money was then transferred to Mr. Ahmed's personal account in Country C as dividends; between 2017 and 2019 the account received EUR nine million as dividends, and Mr. Ahmed was the beneficial owner of the arrangement. (p. 83)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Between 2017 and 2019, that account received (EUR) nine million as dividends."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The investigation found YIN, YANG and OCEANA to be instigators and key actors of an illicit financial and legal arrangement: YANG invoiced YIN with costs plus 150 percent freight; YIN paid YANG the amount (in foreign currency allocated by the Tunisian government); OCEANA then invoiced YANG for 150 percent freight negotiated between YIN and OCEANA, and YANG transferred that money to OCEANA. Regardless of the amount, YIN paid customs duties on a fixed fee basis. (p. 83)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "YANG issued import invoices to YIN, including costs plus 150 percent freight."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A world-renowned financial institution created a trust (FIDUCIA) for Mr. Ahmed that received the money leaked from Tunisia; VERA managed FIDUCIA and its flows, disguising the scheme to give it a seemingly valid economic and legal appearance. (p. 83)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A world-renowned financial institution created a trust for Mr. Ahmed"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Role of Mrs. Helene: the credit notes issued to her camouflaged the flows. The increased 50 percent fees were issued to her benefit, but emails between her and OCEANA's bank in Country C show she personally asked the bank to send the money to FIDUCIA's account in Country C. All parties received commissions (exact amounts unknown). (p. 83)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "she personally asked the bank to send the money to FIDUCIA’s account in Country C"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In the Tunisian trade-based laundering case, the lawyer Marc's role was to prepare FIDUCIA's financial statements and to record the flows leaked from Tunisia to Country C as commissions resulting from an agreement between FIDUCIA and OCEANA. FIDUCIA negotiated the best freight rates with OCEANA on behalf of its sister company YIN. The excerpt also mentions the amount to be transferred to Mr. Ahmed as his dividend as a shareholder of FIDUCIA. (p. 83)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Marc’s role was to prepare the financial statements of FIDUCIA"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: CTAF's analysis confirmed the suspicions of the Tunisian Regulation Authority. Possible crimes: trade-based money laundering via over-invoicing; fraud by establishing and presenting fake invoices; exploitation and misuse of tax and customs advantages; money smuggling from Tunisia to Country C; constitution of assets abroad in violation of Tunisian foreign exchange regulations; and money laundering. (p. 84)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Trade-based money laundering via over-invoicing."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The case demonstrated why authorities needed to review policies and improve involvement of nonfinancial businesses (lawyers, trusts, company service providers) in money laundering cases. The investigation also found a lack of instances of non-compliance by banks; the FIU received no STRs from the Tunisian banking sector, and it credited the regulation authority for reporting. Egmont Secure Web cooperation between FIUs was an effective way to obtain information abroad. (p. 84)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FIU didn’t receive any STRs from the banking sector in Tunisia."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The case was disseminated to the Public Prosecutor's Office, which considered the evidence well founded and ordered a judicial inquiry by Tunisian Customs and Anti-Tax Evasion Department. Over the past 30 years an estimated EUR 90 million was leaked to Country C. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Over the past 30 years, an estimated (EUR) 90 million was leaked to Country C."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Case 20 indicators: the name in the credit notes differed from the beneficiary of the funds; movement of funds through multiple jurisdictions; misuse of legal arrangement. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The name mentioned in the credit notes was different from the beneficiary of the funds."</sub>

## Terrorism, Human Trafficking and Organized Crime

- **descriptive / definition**: Terrorism is commonly defined as violent acts against civilians in pursuit of political or ideological objectives. Terrorist organizations may have legitimate businesses as a funding source but can also be funded by illegal activities, making them similar to other criminal organizations; identifying TF trends, methods and indicators is described as crucial. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Terrorism is commonly defined as violent acts committed against civilians in pursuit of political or ideological objectives."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Indicators listed first: large purchases of foreign currency; small-sized wire transfers to avoid detection; use of nonprofits to generate or transfer funds; and opening accounts in the name of legal entities or organizations with suspicious fund movements. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Small-sized wire transfers to avoid detection."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Further indicators: establishing companies purporting to offer tourism or employment services in foreign countries; international money transfers using remittance services from high-risk countries; a disproportionate number of remittance senders compared to recipients; and splitting of remittances among related accounts. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Establishing companies purporting to offer tourism or employment services in foreign countries."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The primary goal of organized crime is financial gain; globalization and technology have contributed to its growth and internationalization. Law enforcement has observed a connection between organized crime and terrorist financing, each capitalizing on opportunities presented by the other. Income is hard to estimate, but around USD two trillion is believed to be laundered annually. (p. 86)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is believed that around (USD) two trillion is laundered annually"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The text states the importance of detecting, disrupting and preventing terrorism, organized crime and human trafficking cannot be overstated; information exchange and international cooperation are key, and FIUs play a crucial role, with many successful human trafficking cases. (p. 86)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Information exchange and international cooperation are key in the fight against money laundering"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Organized crime groups often engage in crimes depriving people of freedom, including human trafficking, which violates fundamental human rights and has become a significant income source rivaling drug trafficking. AML and counter-terrorism funding experts explore using the AML system to detect, deter, disrupt and investigate human trafficking. (p. 86)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "has become a significant source of income for criminal organizations, rivaling traditional funding sources like drug trafficking"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Further indicators in the terrorism, organized crime and human trafficking list include: funds received through remittances or transfers being withdrawn quickly; use of foreign banks and foreign currency; remittance senders whose economic activities are inconsistent with the remittances; use of nominees, fronts or other methods to hide ownership of assets or accounts; receiving funds from senders who are unrelated; and known criminal associations. (p. 86)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Quick withdrawal of funds received through remittances or transfers."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Further indicators in the same list include: deposits followed straight away by transfers to countries of concern; telecom or internet service providers receiving funds for services that are undisclosed or only vaguely described; large cash deposits or withdrawals; and air tickets paid for by third parties. (p. 86)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Funds received by telecom or internet service providers through undisclosed or vaguely described services."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The last indicators in the same list are: buying legitimate offshore companies in order to open bank accounts in several countries; links between businesses that appear to be independent; use of 'ghost' employees; use of online credit card systems to move money to offshore companies or credit card accounts; the presence of silent partners; and ownership of hidden assets. (p. 86)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Acquiring legitimate offshore companies to open bank accounts in different countries."</sub>

## CASE 21

- **descriptive / case example**: Case 21 (Indonesia PPATK): the terrorist group Mujahidin Indonesia Timur (MIT) generated funds from an Australian-based NPO, The One Banner Project Incorporated (TOBPI); the case also shows effective PPATK cooperation with domestic partners and other FIUs. (p. 87)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "generated its source of funds from an Australian-based NPO, The One Banner Project Incorporated (TOBPI)"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Special Detachment 88 Anti-Terror met PPATK on July 27, 2021 to coordinate financial information assistance. PPATK quickly revealed St Rugaya Umar was a beneficiary who actively received transfers from TOBPI, an NPO under PPATK monitoring since 2020; she was arrested in Makassar, South Sulawesi on July 29, 2021. (p. 87)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This NPO has been under PPATK monitoring since 2020."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: West Jakarta District Court Decision No. 330/Pid.Sus/2022/PN Jkt.Brt found St Rugaya Umar guilty of financing terrorism for MIT, sentencing her to three years' imprisonment plus an IDR 50,000,000 fine (subsidiary three months' imprisonment). To disrupt fund flows the DTTOT Task Force added TOBPI and Amin Kobaitri to the Domestic Targeted Financial Sanctions through Central Jakarta District Court Decision No. 05/Pen.Pid/2023/PN Jkt.Pst dated April 5, 2023. (p. 87)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "found guilty of financing terrorism for the MIT terrorist group"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In the Indonesian PPATK case, the total sent to St Rugaya Umar was IDR 133.2 million by TOBPI and IDR 13.7 million by Amin Kobaitri. (p. 87)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "amounts sent by TOBPI and Amin Kobaitri to St Rugaya Umar were (IDR) 133.2 million and (IDR) 13.7 million"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: On June 30, 2021, the Special Detachment of the Indonesian National Police arrested three suspected terrorists on suspicion of shipping packages of weapons from Bangka Belitung to Jakarta. The investigation found the weapons were intended to be smuggled to Poso, Central Sulawesi, a known location of MIT operations, and this revealed St Rugaya Umar as a funding facilitator. (p. 87)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "On June 30, 2021, the Special Detachment of the Indonesian National Police (INP) arrested three suspected terrorists"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: MIT was designated by the UN Security Council under the Al-Qaeda Sanctions Committee in September 2015. The Indonesian government (through its Domestic Targeted Financial Sanctions list) and the US Department of State have designated MIT as a terrorist organisation, and MIT pledged allegiance to Islamic State/ISIS/ISIL in 2014. (p. 87)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "UN Security Council under the Al-Qaeda Sanctions Committee in September 2015."</sub>

## A. INITIAL DETECTION

- **descriptive / finding or result**: PPATK's analysis showed TOBPI and Amin Kobaitri (TOBPI's main controller) actively sent funds to St Rugaya Umar from December 2019. (p. 88)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "actively sent funds to St Rugaya Umar since December 2019"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: TOBPI made wire transfers totalling AUD 375,915.47 to the Philippines, Uganda, Indonesia, United Arab Emirates and Turkey. By IFTI destination: Philippines 173 reports, AUD 299,361.09; Uganda 31, AUD 37,882.03; Indonesia 27, AUD 22,878.04; United Arab Emirates 3, AUD 13,574.04; Turkey 1, AUD 2,220.27. (p. 88)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Philippines 173 299,361.09"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: From April to May 2020 PPATK and AUSTRAC conducted bilateral joint analysis (Analyst Exchange Program/AEP) on alleged terrorism financing through collection and distribution of donations by several NPOs in Indonesia and Australia; TOBPI, registered with the Australian Charities and Not-for-profits Commission, was one NPO identified. The June 30, 2021 arrest was preceded by coordination between PPATK and Special Detachment 88. (p. 88)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "From April to May 2020, PPATK and AUSTRAC (Australia’s FIU) were involved in bilateral cooperation"</sub>

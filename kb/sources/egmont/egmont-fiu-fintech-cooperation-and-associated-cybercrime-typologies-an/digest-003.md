## CASE STUDY 15:

- **descriptive / case example**: Case study 15: criminals set up or took over businesses in the construction and cleaning sectors as cover to employ non-declared workers and make illicit transfers offshore. The FIU found the companies were part of a network of entities with similar profiles used for a limited time only, and that in recent years these networks had begun to incorporate accounts at foreign FinTech entities, making the ML schemes more complex. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these networks had started incorporating accounts held at foreign FinTech entities into their ML schemes"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In case study 15 a new construction company with no registered workers, showing the network's characteristics, received invoice-related payments from other companies totalling more than EUR 2,000,000 in its first months, then transferred funds to a country in Asia and to private accounts of its manager. The manager opened accounts with ten overseas-based FinTechs to gather and withdraw funds. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "received payments related to invoices from other companies for a total amount of more than EUR 2,000,000"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In case study 15 the FinTechs were outside national jurisdiction so the FIU could not address requests to them directly and relied on international cooperation, which proved highly efficient and confirmed the company and manager belonged to a criminal organization laundering illicit funds. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FIU could not directly address its requests to these entities and had to rely on international cooperation"</sub>

## 7. Tools for the job

- **expected / recommendation**: Section 7 key takeaways: FIUs need to adapt swiftly to understand and address new risks as technology evolves; they need to be agile, continuously enhancing capabilities through digital transformation and a culture that values continuous learning and sharing of knowledge and experience. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIUs need to be agile, continuously looking to enhance their capabilities through digital transformation"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Section 7 key takeaways: FIUs need to engage with FinTechs to understand their product offerings, operations and what data is available on request, and partnerships are important; despite technological advances requiring new knowledge and skills, traditional financial intelligence analysis methods continue to be of fundamental value against FinTech-related crime. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIUs need to engage with FinTechs to better understand their product offerings"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FinTech provides the same financial services as traditional providers (banking, value exchange and transfer, investment, loans, forfeiting, trade, gambling, etc.) through a new delivery method, so criminals use the same services to profit from crime, launder money, finance terrorism and proliferate weapons of mass destruction. Differences lie in the speed at which FinTech can facilitate transactions and the emergence of virtual assets as a funding mechanism. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "criminals continue to use the same financial services and products to profit from crime"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The project team used 41 questionnaire responses to examine (1) what technical information member FIUs collect and process, (2) what tools are used to process it, and (3) what intelligence value it provides; this gives an 'as is' picture to inform the second phase of the IEWG project on digital transformation of FIUs. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The project team utilized the 41 questionnaire responses to gain insights"</sub>

## 7.1. What technical information is available?

- **descriptive / data or feature**: FinTech reports contain the typical KYC/CDD and transaction data FIUs already process, but FinTech's technological nature also offers new data. A non-exhaustive list of new data for FIU analysis: cryptocurrency wallets/addresses and associated blockchain records; identification numbers including IMEI, IMSI or SEID numbers and MAC addresses; login behaviour and IP data; geolocation data; identification (e.g. authentication cookies) and information stored on devices. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The following is a non-exhaustive list of new data that can be incorporated into the analysis of FIUs"</sub>

## 7. Tools for the job

- **descriptive / context**: The report says that examining FinTech activity need not be as daunting or specialised as it first seems: financial intelligence analysts are still required to 'follow the money', and data held by FinTechs may let them build a much broader and more accurate intelligence picture, provided they have the tools to do so. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial intelligence analysts are still required to ‘follow the money.’"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The report states that, given the evolving technological landscape, FIUs must adapt their working methods and tools to understand and handle the new risks FinTech poses; this calls for digital transformation of FIUs and adequate technological tools for analysts. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIUs must, however, adapt their working methods and tools"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report notes that although technology allows a quicker response to criminality, and new commercial tools and knowledge are needed to understand and analyse virtual asset activity, these do not take the place of traditional analysis techniques against FinTech-enabled crime. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these do not replace traditional analysis techniques to tackle FinTech-enabled crime"</sub>

## 7.1. What technical information is available?

- **recommended / recommendation**: Where the relevant technical data is neither mandatorily reportable to FIUs under national AML/CFT legislation nor obtainable from open sources, the report says FIU analysts should consider approaching the FinTech with a formal information request, provided there are sufficient legal grounds. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIU analysts should consider engaging with the FinTech and making a formal request of information"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 17 defines geolocation as helping identify the actual geographic location of objects such as mobile devices or terminals connected to the internet, and the term covers both the process of localization and the identified location. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Geolocation helps identify the actual geographic location of objects"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Fewer than one-third of project participants have the digital capability to process email addresses and IP data, and the ability to analyze geolocation or cookie data is less prevalent. Figure 8 (information the FIU can process digitally) lists device information, email, geolocalization, IP addresses, other identification data (e.g. cookies) and other; the chart values (9%, 15%, 9%, 24%, 30%, 13%) are scrambled and cannot be reliably mapped. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "less than one-third of project participants possess the digital capability to process email addresses and IP data"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A report by the FinTech FinCrime Exchange (FFE) with RUSI, Regulatory DataCorp and 16 FinTech entities (FFE, 2021) found FinTechs hold data not routinely requested by law enforcement or FIUs, including geolocation data, login behaviour and device information. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FinTech entities hold data not routinely requested by law enforcement or FIUs"</sub>

## 7.2. What tools are currently in use?

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Most FIUs have specific software for receiving, integrating and analysing information digitally, yet respondents said they use a range of other applications for analysing FinTech transactions, because their primary tools cannot supply the following: network analysis and graphical depiction tools; blockchain analysis tools; domain analysis tools; commercial databases and threat feeds; open source and social media; programming tools for finding trends and extracting critical information from transactional data; and geographical analysis tools. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Network analysis and graphical depiction tools"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Analysts commonly use a combination of commercial and open-source intelligence tools depending on case requirements. For VASPs, some FIU respondents could not analyze blockchain transactions at all, and over half relied on open-source intelligence as an alternative because internal software lacks the capability. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Over half of the respondents indicated they rely on open-source intelligence information"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report notes virtual-asset technology has moved exponentially with legislation and oversight catching up; as VASP uptake grows, international pressure on jurisdictions to legislate, regulate and monitor in line with FATF standards will intensify and the volume of reported data from the sector will grow. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this will intensify international pressure on jurisdictions to legislate, regulate and monitor these"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The joint EG-FATF Digital Transformation Report recognizes technology's potential to increase efficiency of AML/CTF workflows and effectiveness against serious crime, and gives examples of FIU digital tools ranging from automation to large datasets, big data and advanced analytics such as AI and machine learning. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "recognizes that technology has immense potential to increase the efficiency of AML/CTF workflows"</sub>

## 7.3. Intelligence value-add of technical information

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The study found most FIUs use email and IP addresses for network analysis, which identifies links between FIU databases (such as transaction reports) and external databases including open-source and LEA-held ones; FIUs in public-private partnerships can use private sector datasets to enrich the picture and find activity that would otherwise go undetected. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "most FIUs use email and IP addresses for network analysis"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: FIUs value virtual asset addresses for finding open-source information on blacklisted wallets, scams, sanctions issues or ransomware cases such as the Wannacry attack, helping locate other accounts owned by persons of interest, other victims, and opportunities to cooperate with counterparts. Depending on VA tools, analysts can obtain: the wallet owner's name; first and last date a wallet/address was used; number and value of transactions into and out of the wallet including associated addresses for network analysis; the time transactions occur (indicating time zone and geolocation); cashing in and out points; and a risk rating for the wallet. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "First and last date a wallet/address was used."</sub>

## 7.2. What tools are currently in use?

- **expected / expectation**: The report says FIUs have a real need to actively work on understanding these products and their vulnerabilities, so that they can analyse, investigate and work with partners against the crime; building knowledge, capability and confidence in how the products work can no longer be ignored. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There is a real need for FIUs to actively work to build their understanding of these products"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report states that digital transformation of FIUs is now an established priority, recognised by the Egmont Group and other international bodies such as the FATF. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Digital transformation of FIUs is now an established priority"</sub>

## 7.3. Intelligence value-add of technical information

- **descriptive / limitation**: Several FIUs use IP data for geographical analysis, with visualizations spanning local to worldwide scenarios. IP data may be considered somewhat unreliable because ISPs, VPNs, virtual machines and proxy servers can distort the true IP address, but it can still be useful; respondents noted that if address information including IP is not validated or geo-coded against any systems, manual conversion to latitude and longitude may be needed. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Internet service providers, VPNs, virtual machines, and proxy servers can distort the true IP address"</sub>

## Analysis of information received from FinTech – technical data

- **descriptive / case example**: Case study 16: an STR reported 91 fraudulent transactions reported by foreign card owners, with debit operations on a card as little as a minute apart, all executed through the e-commerce payment module of a T-shirt printing company. The FIU used IP addresses to identify the presumed country of each transaction but found most were executed locally, with 18 different credit cards from nine countries used. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A total of 18 different credit cards from nine different countries were used."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case study 16 revealed the website owners used the e-commerce payment module to debit foreign bank accounts with stolen card data; given the heterogeneity of issuing countries, the data was most likely acquired through the dark web. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the data was most likely acquired through the dark web"</sub>

## 8. Risks and offence types

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Section 8 key takeaways: fraud-related offences remain the most reported transactions from FinTech entities, often due to perceived anonymity and remaining inconsistencies in regulation; FinTech entities play an important role in detecting ML that uses virtual assets; traditional criminal organisations may use FinTech products and services as an alternative to highly regulated sectors. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Offences relating to fraud remain the most reported transactions from FinTech entities"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: Section 8 key takeaways: red flag or indicator reports are likely to help new FinTech entities set up transaction monitoring processes and programs, with education key to reducing the risk their products are misused; sharing intelligence and cooperation between international FIUs is vital where perpetrators span multiple jurisdictions. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Red flag or indicator reports are likely to assist new FinTech entities"</sub>

## 8.1. Common offence types reported

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Offences reported by FinTech entities range from fraud and tax crimes to extortion, illicit drugs and arms trafficking. Fraud is by far the most reported offence, with some FIUs saying it is 100% of STRs from FinTechs. Tax-related offences are also commonly reported, at 45% to 75% of total FinTech STRs for some FIUs. Forgery is third most common, with most FIUs reporting 10% to 36%. The sample of responses is limited. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Fraud is by far the most reported offence type, with some FIUs reporting that this encompasses 100%"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Narcotics trafficking STRs were less than 5% of reports from the sector among respondents. Other commonly reported offences included illegal sale of prohibited products, extortion, and cybercrime such as DoS attacks and ransomware. Terrorism-related offences were 0.1% to 3% of all STRs from the industry, though only five FIUs said they had received such reports. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "such STRs accounted for less than 5% of reports received from the sector"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: For VASPs the most reported offence was also fraud; many FIUs said reports also linked to identity theft, forgery or false ID, with examples including Ponzi schemes, romance scams and stolen credit card offences. Tax crimes featured less prominently in VASP STRs, with only a few jurisdictions receiving tax-related STRs from VASPs. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Some examples of fraud reported to FIUs included Ponzi schemes, romance scams"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: One FIU wrote that murder and grievous bodily harm make up a significant proportion of its STRs from VASPs, with theft and robbery also seeming common in that jurisdiction. STRs from VASPs relating to terrorism financing were more common than for other FinTechs, ranging from 3% to 9% of the sector's total STRs. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "ranged between 3% to 9% of the total number of STRs filed by the sector"</sub>

## 9. Conclusion

- **descriptive / context**: The conclusion restates that FIUs may struggle with FinTech through lack of understanding of the mechanics and risks of new payment services; that all AML/CFT actors have a role; that analysts need a baseline understanding of FinTech services and how to interpret submitted data; and that regulatory sandboxes and public-private partnerships were two best-practice examples. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Two examples of best practice occurred via regulatory sandboxes and public-privatepartnerships."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report says understanding jurisdictional differences and comparing key aspects of national AML/CFT regimes is of considerable value to FIUs following international funds flows, enabling streamlined intelligence exchange and supporting law enforcement. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Understanding jurisdictional differences and comparing key aspects of national AML/CFT regimes"</sub>

## 8.2. Common typologies/indicators

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Fraud again appeared as the most reported typology, with credit card fraud, identity fraud and scam-related activity reported as the leading typology. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The most reported typologies appeared again to be fraud"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Typologies commonly seen in FinTech reports were: use of e-wallets and problems over access to and ownership of virtual asset wallets; transactions using virtual assets; shell companies and bank accounts opened by a third party; and prominent use of fake IDs or stolen KYC data. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Involvement of shell companies and bank accounts opened by a third party"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The main grounds for suspicion in FinTech entities' reports were transactional activity that was unexplained or did not fit the subject's known profile (for example money mules), and a lack of adequate supporting documentation. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "transactional activity that was unexplained or inconsistent with the subject’s known profile (e.g., money mules)"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Other red flags that were raised: payment requests naming unrelated third parties or persons in high-risk jurisdictions, adverse open-source intelligence concerning the report, and enquiries about the subject from law enforcement agencies. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "requests for payments to be made to unrelated third parties or persons in high-risk jurisdictions"</sub>

## 9. Conclusion

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Survey results showed that jurisdictions worldwide differ in how they classify and regulate FinTechs: some classify these reporting entities by type of business, while others use a tech-neutral approach covering specific services at risk of ML/TF exploitation. VASPs and blockchain technology are examples where many jurisdictions have no regulation yet; where such entities are covered by national law, the regulatory environment differs between jurisdictions mainly because each service or product is classified differently. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The survey results illustrated a varied approach to classifying and regulating FinTechs globally."</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: To reduce the risk that criminal organisations use unregulated FinTech products or services to avoid global reporting requirements, the conclusion states that FIUs must agree on a standard reporting format, including technical data, together with FinTech entities, so that the widest possible range of financial information is collected. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIUs must agree on a standard reporting format – including technical data – in partnership with FinTech entities"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The conclusion states that FIUs must ensure FinTech entities fully address the risk of their products and services being misused for criminal purposes, by actively reporting suspicious matters and other relevant financial transactions to one or more FIUs. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIUs must ensure FinTech entities fully address the risks of their products and services being misused"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: The conclusion says STRs that involve other jurisdictions may require a spontaneous disclosure to that jurisdiction's FIU, which helps to identify suspicious activity linked to potential entities of interest and gives a better understanding of national ML/TF risks. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "STRs submitted involving other jurisdictions may require a spontaneous disclosure to the jurisdiction’s FIU."</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Given the speed of the digital world, timely FIU cooperation is vital; in jurisdictions with a centralized approach to reporting, an automated dissemination mechanism is recommended so information can be on-shared with relevant FIUs as required. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an automated dissemination mechanism is recommended to be set up"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Because international cooperation between FIUs plays a fundamental role in gathering financial intelligence, the conclusion recommends that information concerning FIUs in other jurisdictions be disseminated spontaneously. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is recommended that the spontaneous dissemination of information concerning FIUs in other jurisdictions occur"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: FIUs should obtain conclusive information from FinTechs in order to ensure effective international cooperation. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIUs should obtain conclusive information from FinTechs to ensure effective international cooperation."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The conclusion says FIUs need to adapt swiftly to the rapid evolution of the technology behind FinTech products and services, in order to update their approach and address new and emerging risks. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIUs need to adapt swiftly to update and address new and emerging risks."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A culture that values and promotes continuous learning and the sharing of knowledge and experience can be fostered by engaging with FinTechs to understand how they operate and by strengthening capabilities through digital transformation. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Fostering a culture that values and promotes continuous learning, sharing knowledge and experience"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Red flag or indicator reports can assist newly established FinTech entities in setting up their transaction monitoring processes and programs, helping the sector reduce the risk of its products and services being used for crime. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Red flags or indicator reports can help newly established FinTech entities set up their transaction monitoring"</sub>

## Background

- **descriptive / context**: While preparing the concept note, it became evident that 'FinTech' could have different meanings and that FIUs differ in the priority given to FinTech information about ML/TF suspicions. Some FIUs have problems collecting information from FinTechs in their jurisdiction, while the information collected, including technical data, cannot be fully leveraged by all FIUs. These challenges are accentuated by new actors such as Virtual Asset Service Providers (VASPs). (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "some FIUs encounter problems collecting information from FinTech operating in their jurisdiction"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: A questionnaire was circulated to Egmont Group member FIUs to learn which types of FinTech are regulated in each jurisdiction, how legislation treats them, what they report to authorities and how, and how FIUs use the information received. It came with a call for case studies on cyber-enabled financial crime and associated FinTech risks. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a questionnaire was circulated to Egmont Group member FIUs"</sub>

## Key takeaways

- **descriptive / definition**: Key takeaways of section 1: 'FinTech' is short for 'financial technology'; there is no commonly agreed definition; FinTech aims to improve provision of financial services by using technology to enhance accessibility and increase profitability by cutting operating costs; and FIUs may grapple with FinTech due to a lack of understanding of the mechanics of technology-based innovations and of the risks and vulnerabilities of new payment services and products. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There is no commonly agreed definition of FinTech."</sub>

## 1. What Is FinTech?

- **descriptive / context**: Factors that contributed to rapid growth of FinTech include increased internet access, the prevalence of mobile devices, the emergence of blockchain technology and growing digital storage capacity, amongst others. In the broad sense the term refers to computer programs and other technology used to support or enable access to banking and financial services. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "increased access to the internet, the prevalence of mobile devices, the emergence of blockchain technology"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: For this project, FinTech refers to entities that enable payments or transfers of value using new or emerging technologies; Egmont Group members were invited by survey to indicate which entities they include under this definition. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FinTech refers to entities that enable payments or transfers of value by using new or emerging technologies"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Common examples of FinTech providing financial services are internet banking, mobile banking, digital or electronic money, money transfer platforms, non-face-to-face investments, crowdfunding platforms and VASPs. Footnote 2 says VASPs include, but are not limited to, digital currency exchanges, cryptocurrency ATM operators, administrators of stablecoin arrangements, wallet custodians and hedge funds dealing in virtual assets, and anything tokenized as an asset and transferred on a blockchain or other digital peer-to-peer format. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Common examples of FinTech providing financial services include:"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The report says FIUs need to better understand the mechanics behind technology-based innovations, as well as the threats and vulnerabilities associated with new payment services and products. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIUs need to better understand the mechanics behind technology-based innovations"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: FATF Recommendation 15 (New Technologies) requires jurisdictions to address risks arising from new and emerging technologies and to strengthen AML/CFT systems and controls. FATF recently amended it to require jurisdictions to regulate VASPs for AML/CFT purposes, including ensuring such entities have effective systems and controls to monitor and ensure compliance with the AML/CFT measures in the FATF Recommendations. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "requires jurisdictions to address risks arising from new and emerging technologies"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Not all entities providing FinTech services are defined as reporting entities under international standards, so they may not be subject to AML/CFT regulatory oversight or required to report suspicious matters or other transactions. This may make it difficult for FIUs and partners tracing illicit fund flows to access information to build an intelligence picture. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Not all entities providing FinTech services are defined as reporting entities under international standards"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The project aims to: provide an understanding of how FinTech entities cooperate with FIUs in Egmont member jurisdictions; explore the regulatory environment in which they operate; and define potential best practices to engage the FinTech sector, including identifying relevant risks and vulnerabilities. A survey ran between March 2020 and June 2020 and drew input from 41 members across North America, Europe, the Middle East, Africa and the Asia-Pacific region. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Egmont Group members were invited to participate in a survey between March 2020 and June 2020"</sub>

## Key takeaways

- **descriptive / context**: Key takeaways of section 2: faster, more streamlined transactions on blockchain and FinTech platforms create new opportunities for financial crime; and while FIUs know most FinTech entities that provide traditional services through new technology, others are relatively new and present a significant challenge in understanding how their services can facilitate financial crime. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "new opportunities for financial crime are presented"</sub>

## 1. What Is FinTech?

- **mandatory / expectation**: Because the environment in which FinTechs operate is by its nature borderless, the report states that FIUs must adequately harness more effective information-sharing mechanisms to address emerging challenges. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Therefore, FIUs must adequately harness more effective information-sharing mechanisms to address emerging challenges."</sub>

## 2. Types of FinTech

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Internet-based payment, mobile payment and electronic money providers offer products considered mainstream by design, and FIUs largely understand their use of technology because they provide familiar services online or by mobile. By contrast VASPs are considered relatively new, often providing services only beginning to be understood by some FIUs. Footnote 3 gives the FATF description of electronic money as a record of funds or value available to a consumer stored on a payment device (e.g. chip on a prepaid card, mobile phones, computer systems), which can be differentiated into network money, mobile money, electronic purse and electronic wallet. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Conversely, VASPs are considered relatively new, often providing services only beginning to be understood by some FIUs"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The FATF definition of VASPs gives examples of financial activities and functions but does not limit the definition to a particular kind of entity, looking at how a person uses virtual assets and for whose benefit. A natural or legal person engaged as a business, for or on behalf of another person, in any of the listed activities is by definition a VASP regardless of the technology used. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "regardless of the technology used to facilitate these activities"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FATF definition extends to digital currency exchanges, cryptocurrency ATM operators, wallet custodians and hedge funds. Entities such as cryptocurrency miners may or may not be captured depending on activities and functions: an individual miner's activity may not be enough to be a VASP, but a mining pool may be captured if it engages in any activity in the FATF definition. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the activity of a cryptocurrency mining pool may well be captured"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Crowdfunding networks and peer-to-peer lending (also called 'marketplace lending', which directly matches borrowers with investors) let users send and receive money online via mobile apps and compete directly with traditional institutions, making the process cheaper and more efficient. They can pose challenges for regulators in defining which party has the AML/CFT obligation to conduct customer due diligence and report suspicious activity, particularly when investor anonymity is promoted. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These services provide direct competition to traditional financial institutions that rely on large complex systems"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Neo-banks or digital banks operate exclusively online without physical branch networks, offering via mobile apps most services of a conventional bank except in-person branch service. Some partner with traditional financial institutions for tailored solutions and may use artificial intelligence and machine learning. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Neo-banks or digital banks operate exclusively online without traditional physical branch networks"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Robo-advising and stock-trading apps use intelligent algorithms to give asset recommendations and let investors trade stocks by smartphone. Insurtechs work to optimize access to insurance products via apps and collaborate with conventional insurers to automate insurance procedures and extend coverage. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Robo-advising and stock-trading apps use intelligent algorithms to provide intuitive asset recommendations to users"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Buy-now-pay-later (BNPL) providers have emerged in retail credit and are mostly marketed as a budgeting tool or way to make more purchases; they are affordable and generally facilitated via mobile app. Because BNPL models provide both a factoring service to the retailer and loans to the retail customer, the products pose money laundering risks. Footnote 7 defines factoring as an arrangement where a business sells its trade receivables to the factor (e.g. a BNPL provider or bank) and receives cash. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the products pose money laundering risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Under the FATF approach described in the report, a person engaged as a business in any of the following activities for or on behalf of another person is a VASP, whatever technology is used: exchanging virtual assets for fiat currencies; exchanging one or more forms of virtual assets; transferring virtual assets; safekeeping and/or administering virtual assets or instruments that give control over them; and taking part in or providing financial services connected with an issuer's offer and/or sale of a virtual asset. Footnote 5 explains that, here, transfer means carrying out a transaction on behalf of another natural or legal person that moves a virtual asset from one virtual asset address or account to another. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "safekeeping and/or administration of virtual assets or instruments enabling control over virtual assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The report cites the FATF's October 2015 report on emerging terrorist financing risks as defining crowdfunding as an internet-enabled way for businesses, organizations or individuals to raise money, through donations or investments, from many individuals; crowdfunding websites make it easy to set up a fundraising page and collect donations from different sources. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an internet-enabled means for businesses, organizations, or individuals to raise money"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FinTech is also changing global trade finance using blockchain and distributed ledger technology: speeding up trade finance lending, streamlining cross-border trade, providing supply chain management and financing platforms that let businesses manage and pay local and international suppliers, and settling agricultural commodities via blockchain. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FinTech is also facilitating evolution in global trade finance using blockchain and distributed ledger technology"</sub>

## Use of electronic money providers by suspects

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 1: in December 2018 an FIU opened a suspected drug money laundering case from a bank's STR. The bank saw three customers receiving suspicious regular small ATM deposits. CDD information received by the FIU helped identify accounts at other institutions, and the analysis revealed the suspects had registered over a hundred e-money accounts. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the suspects registered over a hundred accounts with electronic money providers"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In Case Study 1, further investigation showed the e-money accounts received drug transaction funds, which were then transferred to several other e-money accounts in different countries to conceal their origin. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "transferred to several other e-money accounts in different countries to conceal their origin"</sub>

## 3. Legal Frameworks to Regulate FinTech

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Many FinTechs simply offer financial services already subject to AML/CFT regulation through a new platform, and in various jurisdictions most of the FinTech types mentioned are subject to national AML/CFT requirements and oversight. In some circumstances gaps exist where lack of oversight challenges FIUs; this has been dubbed a 'sunrise-issue' in the latest FATF Recommendations on Virtual Assets. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "in the latest FATF Recommendations about Virtual Assets"</sub>

## 3.1. Regulation

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The FATF standards acknowledge that not all financial services face the same ML/TF risks and that supervision may differ across sectors. Questionnaire responses showed a varied approach to classifying and regulating FinTechs: some jurisdictions classify reporting entities by business type, while other AML/CFT frameworks take a tech-neutral approach to capture provision of specific services at risk of exploitation for ML/TF. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "adopt a tech-neutral approach to capture the provision of specific services at risk of exploitation for ML/TF"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Because of the pace at which technologies such as VASPs entered the market, there will likely be FinTech subsets many jurisdictions have yet to start regulating. Understanding jurisdictional differences gives FIUs following international funds flows considerable value, enabling streamlined intelligence exchange and supporting law enforcement and the progression of evidentiary requests. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there will likely be certain FinTech industry subsets that many jurisdictions are yet to commence regulating"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Most respondents said internet-based payment services are regulated as payment service providers or payment institutions (with slightly different definitions, e.g. 'operator of payment systems'); some said they are regulated as banking institutions; others said they are regulated when an entity begins offering the specific service rather than at registration. About 23% of respondents said such services are not currently regulated but regulation is in preparation, and about 8% said they are not regulated. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "approximately 23% of respondents stated that internet-based payment services are currently not regulated"</sub>

## Key takeaways

- **descriptive / context**: A key takeaway of section 3 is that the legal status and regulation of FinTech varies across the globe. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The legal status and regulation of FinTech varies across the globe."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: A key takeaway of section 3 is that it is important for FinTechs to be brought within AML/CFT legislation so that they cooperate actively and passively with FIUs. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is important FinTechs are captured by AML/CFT legislation to ensure active and passive cooperation with FIUs"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: A key takeaway of section 3 is that, given the online environment in which FinTechs operate, FIUs need a good understanding of the FinTechs operating in their jurisdiction. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIUs need a good understanding of the FinTechs that operate in their jurisdiction"</sub>

## 3.1. Regulation

- **descriptive / finding or result**: For e-money institutions, most respondents said they are regulated as such; some said they are regulated as a non-financial bank/business/professional entity or as a bank or other traditional financial institution. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "As for e-money institutions , most respondents stated these were regulated as such"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Regulation of the mobile payment service (MPS) industry differs significantly globally and depends on interpretations of what constitutes an MPS. About 30% of respondents said MPS entities are regulated collectively as MPS, 60% said MPS are regulated under different legal categories, and in about 13% of jurisdictions MPS were not subject to regulatory oversight. MPS are commonly regulated as payment service providers or payment institutions and less commonly as non-financial institutions, and some respondents said MPS are regulated only when the entity begins offering a service captured by national AML/CFT legislation. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Regulation of the mobile payment service (MPS) industry differs significantly across the globe"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: VASPs and the distributed-ledger technology behind them are among the most recent, complex and evolving FinTech types, with regulation not yet in place in many jurisdictions. Where legislation captures them the regime varies widely, often because of different classifications, and several jurisdictions had legislation to supervise exchanges between fiat and cryptocurrencies. About 50% of respondents said digital or virtual currency exchanges/operators are 'financial institutions' subject to oversight. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "approximately 50% of the survey respondents advised that digital or virtual currency exchanges/operators"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Where regulation is pending, most jurisdictions plan to regulate VASPs as digital or virtual currency exchanges or to include them in existing 'financial institution' definitions; the remainder considered regulating them as a stand-alone cohort of reporting entities. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An alternative approach considered by the remainder of respondents planning to regulate VASPs"</sub>

## STRs from VASPs

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 2: an FIU received several STRs about a non-payment fraud scheme involving virtual assets. Criminals promised large gains and promoted lucrative Bitcoin investments, and the main suspect gathered approximately EUR 120,000 per day. After national cooperation and international cooperation with foreign FIUs, the suspect was arrested and the operation shut down in July 2019. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the main suspect managed to gather approximately EUR 120,000 per day"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The text notes several kidnapping cases in which perpetrators demanded ransom payment in Bitcoin, leading into Case Study 3. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Several kidnapping cases were reported where the perpetrators demanded a ransom to be paid in Bitcoin"</sub>

## CASE STUDY 3:

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 3: in May 2018 a 13-year-old boy was abducted and his parents received a ransom note demanding 15 Bitcoin to a specific address. Technical data linked to that address let the local FIU identify its users; it was a multi-signature address requiring at least two of three private keys. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "requiring at least two out of three private keys to access the virtual assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In Case Study 3, transaction analysis and wallet clustering analytics helped identify the Digital Currency Exchanges involved. The ransom was split into smaller units and sent to several addresses: in the first layer 0.5 BTC was exchanged for fiat in a European country; in the second and third layers more was exchanged at several other VASPs; the remainder was invested in 'PlusToken', a Ponzi scheme in Asia. Two main suspects were arrested and the victim reunited with his family. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The transaction analysis and the wallet clustering analytics helped identify the Digital Currency Exchanges involved"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 4: an international cyber criminal group targeted local governments and prominent businesses with ransomware that encrypted specific hard drives, decrypted only after Bitcoin ransom payment. Over 2 years and 3 months a total of 20.16165158 BTC was transferred to wallets linked to the suspects. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a total of 20.16165158 BTC were transferred to Bitcoin wallets linked to the suspects"</sub>

## ##### CASE STUDY 5:

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 5: an individual asked a VASP to convert substantial Bitcoin holdings to fiat, requesting Bitcoin be sold for DASH (a privacy coin) and then back to Bitcoin before conversion to Euro, claiming tax reasons. The Digital Currency Exchange reported suspicions to the local FIU, which found the individual was known to law enforcement for drug convictions and had ties to organized crime groups; findings were shared with law enforcement agencies and the taxation office. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "he requested the Bitcoin be sold for DASH, a privacy coin, and then sold back to Bitcoin"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The survey revealed no specific regulation for crowdfunding services/platforms in many jurisdictions. A considerable number of respondents treat crowdfunding entities as reporting entities because their products match existing designated services (making loans, allowing transactions, remittance). About 16% said specific crowdfunding legislation is being considered or is pending. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The survey revealed no specific regulation for crowdfunding services/platforms in many jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: BNPL, peer-to-peer lending and investment management platforms, though not explicitly in the questionnaire, seem captured under existing traditional financial service definitions in many jurisdictions: lending and forfaiting/factoring FinTechs may count as consumer credit providers, and FinTechs packaging and marketing investments could be investment firms. Technology facilitates the service rather than being the service. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FinTech entities offering lending and forfaiting/factoring services may be captured as entities offering consumer credit"</sub>

## 3.2. Identifying FinTech entities

- **descriptive / limitation**: Many FinTechs offer services online with global reach, so a FinTech serving a jurisdiction is likely not domiciled or registered there, which may result in a lack of oversight (e.g. of STRs) by the FIU of the country where the transaction occurs. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is likely not domiciled or registered in that same jurisdiction"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Survey responses suggest cooperation between AML/CFT supervisors and FIUs is a common way to detect new FinTech products and providers, and the financial supervisor/regulator plays a major detection role in almost every responding jurisdiction. Some jurisdictions also use open-source information, regular meetings with competent authorities/regulators, and engagement with the private sector, academia, industry associations or technology communities. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the financial supervisor/regulator plays a major detection role in almost every responding jurisdiction"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Although limited, some countries use Regulatory Sandbox Frameworks to test start-ups and new products in a reduced regulation environment, enabling both parties to identify ML/TF risks of FinTech products, understand new technologies and adapt regulation for integration into the economy as applicable. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "some countries leverage Regulatory Sandbox Frameworks to test start-ups and new"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Where FinTechs operate in a jurisdiction without being regulated, survey respondents (36%) most commonly rely on open-source information or regular monitoring by law enforcement agencies and regulators. Whistleblowing by industry competitors or unsolicited public disclosures are also used by FIUs to identify FinTechs operating in a jurisdiction and potentially circumventing AML/CFT requirements. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "survey respondents (36%) most commonly rely on open-source information or regular monitoring"</sub>

## Importance of identifying FinTech entities: use of domestic e-money institutions in an international fraud scheme

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 6: six foreign companies from Country X (Companies I-VI), all represented by Individual A, a Country X resident, opened accounts with a local e-money institution. Over two days Companies II and III received EUR 154.460,36 in 8 transfers from Company I's bank account in Country X. Some funds went at once to an Asian bank account, supposedly for invoices for electronic devices, clothes, shoes and furniture; the rest went to Company IV's e-money account, supposedly for event and VIP meal invoices, and Company IV wired it to the Asian account. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Six foreign companies from Country X opened accounts with a local e-money institution"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In Case Study 6, the next day the bank that approved the initial transfers asked for a refund due to 'fraudulent payments', also citing transfers made days earlier to e-money accounts of Companies II-V. Months later Company VII, registered in Country Y and represented by Individual B (a Country X resident), opened an account at the same institution and received EUR 10.000 in two transfers for supposed advertising invoices, which looked identical to Company IV's earlier invoices. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The e-money institution noticed that the invoices looked identical to Company"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Local law enforcement found the Case Study 6 transactions were part of an international fraud scheme: victims received calls from alleged financial brokers persuading them to invest in fictitious financial instruments, and the brokers then gave victims the wire details of the e-money accounts. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "persuading them to invest funds in fictitious financial instruments"</sub>

## 3.3 Navigating legislative frameworks in other jurisdictions

- **descriptive / finding or result**: New financial technologies develop rapidly and national regulation may not keep pace; many jurisdictions are drafting legislation to close supervisory gaps. As many frameworks are pending or being implemented, international cooperation remains key to understanding what information can be obtained from other jurisdictions. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "international cooperation remains the key to understanding what information can be obtained from other jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: Establishing a baseline understanding of the legislative framework in a jurisdiction lets requests to foreign counterparts be targeted on specific issues, allowing the recipient to respond more promptly, easily and efficiently. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "information requests to foreign counterparts can be targeted on specific issues"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Resources available to FIUs to identify FinTech entities/VASPs and build an intelligence overview are: the Egmont Group's eCatalogue on VASPs; FATF/FSRB mutual evaluations or follow-up reports; and open-source rating websites listing the top 100 cryptocurrency exchanges with registration details. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Open-source rating websites list the top 100 cryptocurrency exchanges and their registration details"</sub>
- **mandatory / expectation**: The report says FinTech legal frameworks may differ significantly across the globe, unlike banking and other traditional sectors, and states that analysts must consider this and know where to find information on regulatory regimes. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Analysts must consider this and know where to find information on regulatory regimes"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report says regulatory expertise is not necessary for analysts, but a healthy appreciation of regulations in other jurisdictions is helpful, mainly for providing a qualitative international exchange of information upon request. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "While expertise in regulation is not necessary for analysts, a healthy appreciation of regulations in other jurisdictions is helpful"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: The report says there is still room for the Egmont Group to explore a better understanding of national regulatory approaches in practice, and that its future work programs could consider progressing a project to consolidate national registries of FinTech entities. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "future work programs could consider progressing a project to consolidate national registries of FinTech entities"</sub>

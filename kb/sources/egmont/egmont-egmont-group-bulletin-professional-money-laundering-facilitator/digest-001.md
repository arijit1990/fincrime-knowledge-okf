## EGMONT GROUP BULLETIN Professional Money Laundering Facilitators

- **descriptive / context**: The July 2019 public Egmont Group bulletin on professional money laundering networks is intended to help authorities and reporting entities better detect, investigate and report the proceeds of professional money laundering. It describes the Egmont Group as a united body of 164 FIUs providing a platform for secure exchange of expertise and financial intelligence to combat ML/TF. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This Bulletin should assist authorities and reporting entities in better detecting, investigating, and reporting the proceeds"</sub>

## THE EGMONT GROUP OF FINANCIAL INTELLIGENCE UNITS

- **descriptive / context**: The bulletin carries identification number EG-Bulletin-02/2019, dated June 30, 2019, and its intended recipients are Financial Intelligence Units, Law Enforcement Authorities and Reporting Entities. (p. 2)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Identification number: EG-Bulletin-02/2019 Date: June 30, 2019"</sub>

## DEFINITIONS1

- **descriptive / definition**: Professional money laundering (PML) is described as a subset of third-party ML. Its main distinguishing characteristic is the provision of ML services in exchange for a commission, fee or other type of profit. (p. 2)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Professional money laundering is a subset of third-party ML"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The bulletin says PMLs can belong to three categories: individuals, organisations or networks. Footnote 1 states that the FATF (2018) report 'Professional Money Laundering' served as the basis for the analysis. (p. 2)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "PMLs can belong to three categories: (i) individuals, (ii) organisations or (iii) networks"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: An individual PML has specialised skills or expertise in placing, moving and laundering funds and specialises in providing ML services, which can also be performed while acting in a legitimate professional occupation. Such services can include, but are not limited to, accounting services, financial or legal advice, and the formation of companies and legal arrangements. (p. 2)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An individual PML possesses specialised skills or expertise in placing, moving and laundering funds"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: A Professional Money Laundering Network (PMLN) is a collection of associates or contacts working together to facilitate PML schemes and/or subcontract services for specific tasks. Such networks usually operate globally and can include two or more PMLOs working together, or operate as informal networks of individuals offering the criminal client a range of ML services; the interpersonal relationships are not always organised and are often flexible. (p. 2)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is a collection of associates or contacts working together to facilitate PML schemes"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The bulletin defines a Professional Money Laundering Organisation (PMLO) as an autonomous, structured group of two or more individuals that specialises in offering services or advice to launder money for criminals or other OCGs. (p. 2)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "consists of two or more individuals acting as an autonomous, structured group"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: According to the bulletin, laundering funds may be the core activity of a PMLO but is not necessarily its only activity, and most PMLOs have a strict hierarchical structure in which each member is a specialised professional responsible for particular elements of the ML cycle. (p. 2)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Most PMLOs have a strict hierarchical structure, with each member acting as a specialised professional"</sub>

## COMMISSIONS / FEES

- **descriptive / finding or result**: A number of different and overlapping factors affect the fee or commission paid to PMLs. The fee often depends on the complexity of the scheme, the methods used and knowledge of the predicate offence, and the rate may change with the level of risk the PMLs assume. Commission rates are often influenced by the countries or regions involved. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The fee will often depend on the complexity of the scheme, methods used and knowledge of the predicate offence"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Other factors influencing PML commission rates are: (a) the reputation of the individual PML; (b) the total amount of funds laundered; (c) the denomination (value) of banknotes in cash cases; (d) the time a client allows to move or conceal funds, with a shorter period meaning a higher commission; and (e) the imposition of new regulation(s) or law enforcement activities. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the amount of time requested by a client to move or conceal funds"</sub>
- **permissive / typology or red flag**: To obtain their commission, PMLs may take commission in cash in advance, transfer a portion of the money laundered to their own accounts, or have the commission integrated into the business transaction. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "To obtain commission for their services, PMLs may (i) take commission in cash in advance"</sub>

## ADVERTISING / MARKETING

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: PMLs often market their services actively by word-of-mouth through an informal criminal network; criminal links and trust built through earlier criminal engagement strengthen bonds and can encourage further co-operation. Authorities have also identified posted advertisements for PML services on the Dark Web. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Authorities have also identified the use of posted advertisements for PML services via the Dark Web"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Law enforcement reports that PMLs often keep a shadow accounting system with detailed records using code names, possibly detailed spreadsheets tracking clients (by code name), funds laundered, origin and destination of funds, relevant dates and commissions received. Records may be electronic (e.g. a password-protected Excel spreadsheet) or on paper, and are an invaluable resource for investigators. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "PMLs often keep a shadow accounting system that contains detailed records with code names"</sub>

## ROLES AND FUNCTIONS

- **descriptive / context**: PMLs perform numerous roles and functions; depending on the type of PML, an individual may perform a unique function or several roles at once. The bulletin says understanding these roles is important to identify all relevant players and ensure all aspects of PMLs are detected, disrupted and ultimately dismantled. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Depending on the type of PML, an individual may perform a unique function or perform several roles simultaneously"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Role 1, leading and controlling: individuals provide overall leadership, strategic planning and decision making. Control over the group's ML activities is normally exercised by a leader but may be exercised by others responsible for the funds from collection from clients until delivery (e.g. arranging cash collection and delivery at a chosen international destination). They also determine the commission charged and pay salaries to other PMLO/PMLN members. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "who provide the overall leadership and direction of the group"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Role 2, introducing and promoting: specific individuals bring clients to the PMLs and manage communications with criminal clients, including managers who establish and maintain contact with other PMLOs or individual PMLs locally or abroad, through which the PMLO gains access to infrastructure already established by other PMLs. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "who are responsible for bringing clients to the PMLs and managing communications with the criminal clients"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Role 3, maintaining infrastructure: these individuals establish PML infrastructure or tools such as setting up companies, opening bank accounts and acquiring credit cards. They may also manage a network of registrars who find and recruit nominees (e.g. front men) to register shell companies for the client, receive online banking logins/passwords, and buy SIM-cards for mobile communication. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "opening bank accounts and acquiring credit cards"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Item 4 gives the 'money mule herder' as an example of managing infrastructure: this person recruits and manages money mules (e.g. via job ads and personal introduction) and pays their salaries, either as a fee for their money transfer services or as a one-time payment. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "responsible for recruiting and managing money mules (e.g. via job ads and via a personal introduction)"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Role 5, managing documents: individuals create the documentation needed for laundering and in some cases produce or acquire fraudulent documents, including fake identification, bank statements and annual account statements, invoices for goods or services, consultancy arrangements, promissory notes and loans, false resumes and reference letters. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "including fake identification, bank statements and annual account statements, invoices for goods or services"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Role 6, managing transportation: individuals receive and forward goods internationally or domestically, provide customs documentation and liaise with transport or customs agents. This role is particularly relevant to TBML schemes. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This role is particularly relevant to TBML schemes"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Role 7, investing or purchasing assets: where needed, real estate or other assets such as precious gems, art, luxury goods and vehicles are bought to store value for later sale. Criminals seek help buying real estate overseas, and PMLs have been known to use elaborate schemes with layers of shell companies to facilitate this. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "PMLs have been known to use elaborate schemes involving layers of shell companies to facilitate this"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Role 8, collecting: individuals collect illicit funds and handle the initial placement stage. Being at the front end they are most likely to be identified by law enforcement, but they often leave little paper trail and can layer proceeds by depositing commingled funds using cash-intensive businesses. They are aware of their role, unlike some money mules who may be unwitting participants. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they are most likely to be identified by law enforcement"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Role 9, transmitting: individuals move funds from one location to another in the PML scheme regardless of mechanism, receiving and processing money through the traditional banking system or MVTS providers, and often performing cash withdrawals and subsequent currency exchange transactions. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "responsible for moving funds from one location to another in the PML scheme"</sub>

## GENERAL BUSINESS MODEL OF PROFESSIONAL MONEY LAUNDERING NETWORKS

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: In general, PML financial schemes consist of three stages. In the first, funds are transferred, physically or electronically, to PMLs or entities acting for them; the manner of introduction varies with the predicate offence(s) and the form of the proceeds (cash, bank funds, virtual currency, etc.). (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In general, financial schemes executed by PMLs consist of three stages"</sub>

## _Stage I: Criminal proceeds are transferred to, or collected by, PMLs

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Stage I, cash: illicit proceeds introduced as currency are usually passed to a cash collector, who may ultimately deposit the cash in bank accounts. The collector introduces cash into the financial system via cash-intensive businesses, MVTS providers or casinos, or physically transports it to another region or country. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they are usually passed over to a cash collector"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Stage I, bank accounts: some crimes such as fraud, embezzlement and tax crimes generate proceeds held in bank accounts; unlike drug proceeds they rarely start as cash but may end as cash after laundering. Clients usually set up legal entities under whose names accounts are opened, and these accounts transfer money to a first layer of companies controlled by the PMLs. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Unlike drug proceeds, proceeds of these crimes rarely start out as cash but may end up as cash after laundering"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Stage I, virtual currency: criminals holding proceeds as virtual currency (e.g. owners of online illicit stores, including Dark Web marketplaces) must have e-wallets or an address on a distributed ledger platform that the PMLs can access. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "must have e-wallets or an address on a distributed leger platform, which can be accessed by the PMLs"</sub>

## _Stage II: Layering stage executed by individuals and/or networks

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Stage II, layering: most PMLs use account settlement mechanisms to make funds harder to trace, a combination of techniques may be used in one scheme, and the stage is managed by individuals who co-ordinate financial transactions. For cash proceeds, layering commonly includes TBML and fictitious trade, account settlements and underground banking. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the majority of PMLs use account settlement mechanisms to make it more difficult to trace the funds"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Stage II, bank accounts: funds in accounts managed by the professional launderer are in most cases moved through complex layering schemes or proxy structures, which are complex chains of shell company accounts set up domestically and abroad. Funds from different clients are mixed within the same accounts, making it harder to trace funds from a particular client. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Proxy structures consist of a complex chain of shell company accounts, established both domestically and abroad"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Stage II, virtual currency: criminals engaged in cybercrime or computer-based fraud, or selling illicit goods through online stores, often use money mule networks. Proceeds are often held as virtual currency in e-wallets or virtual currency wallets and go through a complex chain of transfers. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "often use the services of money mule networks"</sub>

## _Stage III: Laundered funds are handed back over to clients for investment or asset acquisition

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Stage III: funds are transferred to accounts controlled by the PML's clients, their close associates or third parties acting for them or for affiliated legal entities. The PML may invest the proceeds for these clients in real estate, luxury goods and businesses abroad (or in some cases in the countries the funds came from), and funds can also be spent on goods deliveries to the country of origin or a third country. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "funds are transferred to accounts controlled by the clients of the PML, their close associates or third parties"</sub>

## RECOMMENDED RED FLAG INDICATORS FOR FIUS AND PRIVATE SECTOR

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Red flag list, items 1-4: transactions via different bank accounts managed from the same set of IP addresses; different geographic locations of companies, bank accounts, owners and managing directors while the same IP addresses are used for online account management; an individual or single entity opening or managing numerous bank accounts in a coordinated fashion; and transit movement of funds through company accounts that receive and immediately transfer money, with a zero balance by close of day. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial transactions via different bank accounts are managed from the same set of IP addresses"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Red flag list, items 5-7: no tax, insurance or social payments by companies used for transit movement of funds; regular cross-border transfers via shell company accounts related to loan agreements and payments for services; and transferring funds abroad to buy goods that are never shipped, received or documented. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "No tax, insurance or social payments are conducted by companies that are used for the transit movement of funds"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Red flag list, items 8-10: payments for real estate and other high-value assets made by offshore companies; payments for goods delivered to third countries made by offshore companies that are not parties to the contract; and bank accounts in one jurisdiction held by corporate structures in a second jurisdiction with beneficial owners in a third, used to pay or receive transactions for goods or services unconnected to the businesses involved or to the trade concerned. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Payments for goods delivered to third countries are performed by offshore companies that are not parties to contract"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Red flag list, items 11-12: a significant number of STRs/SARs about legal entities registered via the same TCSP or having the same nominal shareholders and directors; and cash withdrawals or deposits from or to multiple bank cards made systematically via the same ATMs, where very small intervals between transactions with different cards indicate management by the same person. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A significant number of STRs/SARs regarding legal entities registered via the same TCSP"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Red flag list, item 13: participating companies are registered at one or more 'virtual' addresses, or at mailbox addresses using special services providers (advocates, lawyers and others). (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Participating companies are registered at one or more"</sub>

## CYBERCRIME AND CRYPTOCURRENCY

- **descriptive / context**: Governments worldwide are struggling to develop regulatory frameworks for cryptocurrencies and to determine how to treat digital currency from tax, asset and monetary policy perspectives; FIUs play a vital role, including freezing funds and identifying relevant authorities to contact, and the nature of cybercrime and virtual assets makes information exchange between FIUs key. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "make the exchange of information between FIUs key to combat this types of crimes"</sub>

## Indicators

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Cybercrime indicators: crimes involving multiple jurisdictions; use of smaller financial institutions; regulatory weaknesses; extended statutory holidays that attract cybercrime; fraudsters registering a domain similar to a legitimate business to fool the target. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Fraudster usually registers a domain similar to a legitimate business to fool the target"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The cases show FIU efforts uncovering frauds using virtual assets, SWIFT heists and business email compromise schemes; these activities affect financial institutions, expose the sector to billions of dollars in losses worldwide, and compromise business or personal email accounts with false payment instructions. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "expose the financial sector to billions of dollars in losses worldwide"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Business email compromise indicators: schemes usually appear to come from the chief operating officer, CEO or senior management asking to transfer funds to a 'changed' bank account; funds go to a recipient in the company the victim has never dealt with; transfers are initiated near end of day or just before weekends or public holidays; the receiving account has no history of receiving large transfers; the receiving account is a personal account. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Transfers are initiated near the end of day or just before weekends or public holidays"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Financial institutions can play an important role in identifying, preventing and reporting fraud schemes by strengthening communication and collaboration with law enforcement; FIUs are encouraged to work with financial institutions and law enforcement to help recover victims' funds by quickly disseminating information on suspected fraud, and quick action by victims, institutions and law enforcement plus international information exchange contribute to recovery. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIUs are encouraged to work collaboratively with financial institutions and law enforcement"</sub>

## Unprecedented Global Multibillion Cryptocurrency Euro Fraud (Kosovo, FIU-Kosovo) 5

- **descriptive / case example**: Kosovo case 5: through the bank's STR, FIU-Kosovo found three entities: two international websites and a local company CUMA with four shareholders (two Kosovo nationals and two Danish citizens), registered in Kosovo and offering online tutorial packages for investing in the cryptocurrency OneCoin. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "one local entity, CUMA, which had four shareholders"</sub>

## Indicators

- **mandatory / context**: The report highlights that the Financial Action Task Force revised its standards in June 2019 to mitigate the money laundering and terrorism financing risks posed by virtual assets; virtual asset service providers must now implement a full range of preventive measures against these crimes. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Virtual asset service providers must now implement a full range of preventive measures"</sub>

## CASE

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The CUMA case was Kosovo's first successful stand-alone money laundering indictment and prosecution involving a global multibillion-dollar cryptocurrency fraud (OneCoin), producing the largest single confiscation ever (nearly EUR 1 million) upheld on appeal; it began two months after Kosovo joined Egmont, relied on cooperation with 17 Egmont members, and took a record 18 months from STR to court verdict. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "first successful stand-alone money laundering indictment and prosecution in Kosovo"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Police, taxation and Business Registration Agency databases gave no information tying those involved to money laundering; open-source searching showed the Danish citizen owed debts to Danish tax and other authorities and had a criminal record, which FIU-Denmark confirmed. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Data from police, taxation and Business Registration Agency databases did not provide information"</sub>

## The Investigation

- **descriptive / case example**: Preliminary analysis found OneCoin was not then considered a pyramid scheme though some authorities (BaFin in Germany, and Bulgaria, Italy and the United Kingdom) had warned customers; FIU-Kosovo nevertheless opened a case and scored it high risk priority. The case began from a bank STR, followed by qualitative analysis of the individuals and companies and tactical cooperation with the reporting entity. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIU-Kosovo opened a case and scored it with a prioritized high risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: FIU-Kosovo used open sources on websites provided by CUMA's owner to the bank (onelife.eu and nbh.ea), which with the STR revealed the link between OneLifeNetwork Ltd. and OneCoin. Countries: Kosovo, Denmark, Germany, Poland; sectors: banking, precious metals and stones trade. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "uncover the connection between OneLifeNetwork Ltd. and OneCoin"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: FIU-Kosovo found a Facebook profile of a local owner and analysed his fingernails in photos, suggesting a manual labourer (likely house painter or construction worker); with the bank it tested his knowledge of education packages or IT, and when he visited the bank he did not respond convincingly to due diligence and know-your-customer questions. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "analyzed his fingernails in photos, which indicated that he was a manual labourer"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: To meet the prosecutor's requirements FIU-Kosovo built a strategy on one suspect's account, whose balance was EUR 907,406 on July 21; if the suspect declared no financial activity by August 1 it would be tax fraud. On July 26 the suspect attempted an outgoing e-banking transaction; FIU-Kosovo let the bank allow e-banking transactions up to EUR 5,000, then pretend a cyber security problem, and keep in email contact to delay until August 1 so a tax evasion charge could apply. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIU-Kosovo gave permission to the bank for e-banking transactions up to EUR 5,000"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: FIU-Kosovo found the parent company OneLifeNetwork Ltd. registered in Belize, whose financial monitoring authority warned it was not registered to trade and that trading with it was an offence; requests to 17 FIUs via the Egmont Secure Web determined purchasers were being defrauded. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIU-Kosovo requested information from 17 FIUs through the Egmont Secure Web"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: From April to mid-July 2017 FIU-Kosovo gathered bank information, customs information, 17 international responses confirming CUMA was part of a global fraud, and tax and customs information, which was not enough to convince the Special Prosecution to issue a sequestration order. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This information was not enough to convince the Special Prosecution to issue a sequestration order."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: On July 28 the bank reported the suspect wanted to transfer EUR 858,000 to a Danish jewellery shop to buy precious metals; FIU-Kosovo had the bank request the shop's documents, and on July 31, coordinating in real time, the bank asked for an invoice; the invoice emailed by the Danish suspect listed OneLifeNetwork Ltd. Belize as beneficial owner, the missing piece of the puzzle. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This invoice was the missing piece of the puzzle"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: FIU-Kosovo determined CUMA was a cover company or mule used to funnel money via a Danish jewellery shop to OneCoin's beneficial owner; the fraudsters assumed poor bilateral cooperation tools because Kosovo is not a UN or INTERPOL member nor recognised by some jurisdictions, but Kosovo had just been admitted to Egmont. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "CUMA was being used as a cover company or mule to funnel money via a jewelry shop in Denmark"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: OneCoin was advertised as a cryptocurrency, attracted billions of euros of investment worldwide through education packages, and was featured in business magazines and conferences; OneLife was directly linked to OneCoin and CUMA sold identical packages developed by OneLife; the cryptocurrency was a global fraud and Kosovo was used because of perceived lack of bilateral cooperation. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "CUMA was selling identical education packages developed by OneLife"</sub>

## FIU Action

- **descriptive / case example**: FIU-Kosovo issued a 48-hour freezing order on the account, freezing EUR 946,000; the prosecution then issued a sequestration order and indictment, in close cooperation with FIU-Kosovo, explaining the stand-alone money laundering offence and use of multiple jurisdictions and corporate vehicles to hide it. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIU-Kosovo issued a 48-hour freezing order on the account involved in this activity"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: FIU-Kosovo coordinated with taxation and customs authorities, advised the Special Prosecutor on issuing a stand-alone money laundering charge that for the first time in Kosovo led to court convictions upheld on appeal; an analyst's database, open source and background checks showed the two nationals had no IT experience and one Dane had a prior conviction for tax evasion and illegal weapons possession, verified via the Egmont Secure Web; FIU-Kosovo requested information from all Kosovo banks on CUMA's transactions and from the Tax Administration on declared activity. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "one of whom had a previous convic- tion for tax evasion and illegal weapons possession in Denmark"</sub>

## Outcome/Contribution of the Case

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The CUMA case produced EUR 1 million in confiscated assets upheld on appeal; analysis involved open source investigation, tactical cooperation with the private sector, prosecution, taxation and customs, and information exchange with 17 Egmont members months after Kosovo's admission; Kosovo is working with Poland and Denmark on compensating citizens who were victims. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "excel- lent tactical cooperation with the private sector, the prosecution, and taxation and customs authorities"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The two Kosovo nationals received a 2.5-year probational sentence, nearly EUR 1 million was confiscated and arrest warrants were issued for the two Danish nationals; Kosovo's Ministry of Justice Department for International Legal Cooperation has been proactive in reaching out to victims' countries to compensate victims from confiscated funds. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "sentenced to 2.5 years probational sentence, nearly EUR 1 million was confis- cated"</sub>

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Indicators of the Kosovo case: movement of funds through multiple jurisdictions; suspected pyramid scheme. (p. 35)[^original]

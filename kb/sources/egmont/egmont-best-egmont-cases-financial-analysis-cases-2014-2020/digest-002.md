## BRIBERY AND CORRUPTION

- **descriptive / context**: The section introduction says that practically any society or state is vulnerable to corruption, which has taken on an international character largely through globalization; globalization creates economic transparency and free movement of capital, goods and labour but also invites criminalization of national economies. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it also invites the criminalization of national economies"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Bribery and corruption pursue personal enrichment and the concentration and retention of power; the 'vicious circle' principle means corrupt officials create their own structure, in which each official's knowledge of the others' involvement in bribery is the basis of the structure. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "corrupt officials create a structure of their own kind"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Corruption erodes trust in government and undermines the social contract; the World Bank Group considers it a major challenge in ending extreme poverty, and empirical studies have shown the poor pay the highest percentage of their income in bribes; some studies suggest the poor may be targeted as they are seen as powerless to complain. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Empirical studies have shown that the poor pay the highest percentage of their income in bribes."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The cases in the section show how FIU work and techniques are critical to exposing increasingly sophisticated schemes, such as profiting from the misfortune of bankrupts, using a complex array of offshore accounts to launder bribes, creating a bogus NGO to disguise a multimillion-dollar pork barrel scam, and leveraging corrupt officials to win government tenders and then embezzling public funds through the contracts. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "creating a bogus non-governmental organization to disguise a multimillion-dollar pork barrel scam"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: In the public sector corruption is not only officials taking bribes to award contracts or favouring friends and family; corrupt officials might also artificially slow bureaucratic processes, since longer queues or more complicated paperwork raise the incentive for citizens to offer a bribe. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Corrupt officials might also artificially slow down bureaucratic processes to increase their chances for personal enrichment"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Rooting out corruption means creating a strong, legal, democratic state; the world community is committed to fighting corruption, raising requirements for those hired or appointed by state bodies and regulating relations between state bodies and the population. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "To root out corruption means to create a strong, legal, democratic state"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Anti-money laundering efforts by international organizations and governments aim in particular to eliminate conditions facilitating legalization of dirty money, one of which is corruption among government officials, political representatives and business. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "One of these conditions is corruption in the ranks of government officials"</sub>

## Indicators

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Indicators of corruption-related laundering, suspicious cash transactions, include purchase of high-value items with cash, credit card debt paid off in cash, and large cash deposits to personal accounts subsequently transferred to overseas accounts. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "large cash deposits made to personal accounts and subsequently transferred to overseas accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Further indicators: payments made primarily in cash through dummies and intermediaries to obscure the audit trail; multiple financial transactions to blur the trail of the source of funds; use of currency exchanges to remit funds abroad. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "payments made primarily in cash through dummies and intermediaries to obscure the audit trail"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Further indicators: use of multiple offshore companies to blur the audit trail and hide the ultimate beneficial owner; use of fake contracts to justify receipt of large sums of money; embezzlement of state funds through fake NGOs; corrupt government officials receiving kickbacks or commission for facilitating transfer of state funds. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Use of multiple offshore companies to blur the audit trail and hide the ultimate beneficial owner"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In the bribery and corruption section the report observes that progress against corruption usually takes determined effort to overcome vested interests; transparency and open governance are normally one element of the story but seldom all of it; and once public dissatisfaction with corruption and cronyism peaks, the political gains from tackling corruption can outweigh the costs of antagonising those interests. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Transparency and open governance are typically part of the story, but rarely the whole story."</sub>

## Judges Stealing from Court-Protected Coffers (Brunei Darussalam, AMBD) 1

- **descriptive / case example**: Brunei Darussalam case 1: two corrupt court officers, Ms. RR and her husband Mr. NB, abused their positions at the State Judiciary Department to embezzle debtors' funds from Official Receiver accounts; the case involves 255 Official Receiver accounts relating to 234 victims and more than BND 15.7 million (USD 11.1 million) embezzled between 2004 and 2017. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This case involves 255 Official Receiver accounts related to 234 victims"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Between 2011 and 2017 the couple transferred the equivalent of BND 1,374,212 to their accounts in Country A and usually travelled there after the transfers; the funds were used for premium services and luxuries including at least 19 luxury cars, at least 456 high-value assets (watches, designer handbags, accessories, shoes) and long-term rental of homes outside Brunei Darussalam. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "transferred an equivalent of BND 1,374,212 to their accounts in Country A"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Open-source information, including social media, indicated the suspects had a significant presence in Country A, living at their own residence and driving their own vehicles; early feedback from Country A's FIU indicated the properties thought to be theirs were all rental properties. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Early feedback from Country A’s FIU indicated that the properties thought to be owned by the suspects were all rental"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Two key types of information helped the investigation: information on the suspects' overseas wire transfer activities, and cash transaction reports filed on the suspects. The Anti-Corruption Bureau approached AMBD (the FIU) on December 31, 2017 to share complaints against the two judicial officers and request financial information to determine how they afforded their lifestyle. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Two key types of information became useful in developing the investigation"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: AMBD also sought cooperation from countries B, C and D to refute the suspects' claims of legitimate sources of wealth and to trace funds; overseas wire transfer information proved that assets in their country of residence that could only have been purchased overseas were bought with embezzled funds and so were proceeds of crime. Countries involved: Brunei Darussalam, United Kingdom, Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand; sector: banking. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "refute the suspects’ claims made that their sources of wealth were legitimate"</sub>
- **mandatory / data or feature**: Brunei Darussalam has two types of cash transaction report (CTR). The first is a threshold-based report that financial institutions, designated non-financial businesses and professions, and motor vehicle dealers must complete for transactions above a certain amount. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The first is a threshold-based report that financial institutions"</sub>
- **mandatory / data or feature**: The second CTR type is a currency notes transaction report: banks are required to file reports on all transactions involving BND 10,000 (about USD 7,000) or SGD 10,000 currency notes, recording the transactor's details and the note serial number, intended to mitigate the risk of money laundering through large denomination notes. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "banks are required to file reports on all trans- actions involving BND 10,000"</sub>

## FIU Action

- **descriptive / case example**: From these records AMBD found transactions spread over several years involving large cash withdrawals from debtors' accounts at one bank; Ms. RR withdrew large amounts without arousing suspicion by using her status and authority to release funds. The couple was charged with criminal breach of trust by a public servant, money laundering and possession of unexplained property. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "using her status and position of authority to release funds"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: AMBD collaborated closely with the Anti-Corruption Bureau and banks, facilitated by early appointment of designated individuals at each agency preauthorized to exchange information without delay; this built trust and let authorities act quickly so embezzled funds were not further dissipated or hidden; AMBD also used Egmont Group connections to send information requests to four other countries. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "designated individuals at each agency who were preauthorized to exchange information without delay"</sub>

## Evolution of the Case

- **descriptive / case example**: Much of the cash withdrawn from the Official Receiver accounts was traced to deposits to the couple's joint savings account at another bank; currency notes CTRs linked serial numbers of withdrawn cash to cash deposited to that account. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "link the serial numbers of cash withdrawn from the Official Receiver accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The case took one week from the start of official investigation until seizure of suspected proceeds, and almost 24 months from intelligence gathering through prosecution; discovery of links through threshold-based and currency note CTRs was a key factor in speeding the process. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The case took only one week from the start of official investigations until seizure of the suspected proceeds of crime."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Some serial numbers of notes withdrawn from the Official Receiver accounts matched those reported by motor vehicle dealers for deposits to their corporate accounts; linking car dealers' CTRs with bank-filed CTRs identified the officers' cars as proceeds of crime, and serial number tracing gave concrete evidence of the officers moving money from debtors' accounts to personal accounts for personal gain. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "had the same serial numbers as those reported by motor vehicle dealers for deposit to their corporate accounts"</sub>

## Valuable indicators of the case

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Indicators from the Brunei case: car purchases paid in cash including BND 10,000 notes; credit card debt paid off in cash; ATM deposits at unusual hours (such as close to midnight) to avoid being noticed at bank counters; large cash deposits to a personal savings account subsequently transferred to overseas accounts under the senders' names. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Deposits via automated teller machines conducted at an unusual hours (such as close to midnight)"</sub>

## Outcome/Contribution of the Case

- **descriptive / finding or result**: On January 15, 2020 Ms. RR was found guilty on all charges of criminal breach of trust by a public servant and money laundering; Mr. NB, facing eight money laundering charges, was convicted of six and acquitted of two for insufficient evidence. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "convicted of six charges and acquitted of two due to insufficient evidence"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A further charge of possession of unexplained property was initially stayed, the stay was lifted after the initial trial, and prosecutors had not announced whether they would pursue it; appeals by both were scheduled for December 2020. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The proceedings for these charges were initially stayed but the stay was lifted"</sub>

## Exposing the Politically Exposed (Peru, FIU-Peru) 2

- **descriptive / case example**: Peru case 2: a former senior official of one of Peru's most important state-owned enterprises was a PEP at the time of events; the case was sparked by a news report that he received a bribe of about USD 1,300,000 from a Latin American transnational company through an account in Country A. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A former senior official in one of Peru’s most important state-owned enterprises was a politically exposed person"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The PEP allegedly received the bribes through an offshore company he set up just over a month before receiving the funds; funds were transferred to accounts in four countries belonging to his immediate family or linked companies (conversion and transfer acts) and used to buy real estate (acts of concealment and possession). (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "through an offshore company that had been set up by the PEP just over a month before receiving these funds"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: FIU-Peru requested information from counterpart FIUs in Country A and B, which identified first links and the initial money trail: money allegedly went to Countries B, C and D, mostly to accounts of the PEP's immediate family and offshore companies owned by them; Offshore 1, which received the bribes, was established in Country B, and Offshore 3 and 4 in Country E. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "(FIU-Peru) requested further information from counterpart Financial Intelligence Units (FIUs) in Country A and Country B"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: FIU-Peru then asked counterparts in Countries B to F and a Peruvian bank (Bank BL 1), requested information from the main Peruvian banks and researched open data sources and internal databases, identifying the economic activity and assets of the PEP and affiliates and allowing evaluation of the rationality of the funds. Country involved: Peru; sector: banking. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "information was requested from the main Peruvian banks; FIU-Peru also researched open data sources and internal databases"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Analysis showed the money mostly came to Peru and was used to acquire real estate and vehicles; one property was in Country G, prompting an information request there; Offshore 3 belonged to the PEP and his wife and Offshore 4 to a Uruguayan citizen and his wife, the former having worked in the same economic sector as the PEP. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Offshore 4 belonged to a Uruguayan citizen and his wife"</sub>

## DRUG TRAFFICKING

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The section states drug trafficking generates multibillion-dollar revenues annually and is the main revenue source for criminal organizations; it was identified as the single largest crime category tied to money laundering (about a third), followed by smuggling (about a fifth). (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Drug trafficking was identified as the single largest crime cat- egory tied to money laundering"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: According to UNODC, drug use is rising: in 2009 an estimated 210 million users represented 4.8 percent of the population aged 15-64, versus an estimated 269 million users (5.3 percent) in 2018. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In 2009, an estimated 210 million drug users represented 4.8 percent of global population aged 15–64"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Financial Action Task Force is quoted as saying drug trafficking can generate huge proceeds and those involved must find a way to control funds without attracting attention, by disguising sources, changing form or moving funds to where they attract less attention. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the sources, changing the form, or moving the funds to a place where they are less likely to attract attention"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The sale of synthetic drugs over the Internet is a growing industry; UNODC's 2020 report notes drug markets are becoming more complex, with plant-based substances joined by hundreds of synthetic drugs (many not under international control) and rapid rise in non-medical use of pharmaceutical drugs. Cases include a network of businesses hiding international web and parcel post drug trafficking, and an organized crime group headed by a prison inmate importing methamphetamine from his cell. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "drug mar- kets are becoming increasingly complex."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Payment for drugs is nearly always in large sums of cash that must be moved and/or integrated into the financial system so it appears legitimate and can support a lavish lifestyle or finance other businesses. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The actual payment for drugs is nearly always done with large sums of cash"</sub>

## Indicators

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Drug trafficking indicators: use of companies to provide a cover of legality; public contracts with a state company; use of family ties to hide money and confuse investigators; bribery of public officials; use of legal companies that receive profits from organized crime; use of multiple accounts to collect funds then transferred to the same foreign beneficiaries. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Use of family ties to hide money and confuse investigative services"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Criminal groups are increasingly creative in distribution and payment; one case in the section involves a family using state fuel transport contracts to move drugs and hide the money, and another a drug ring implicating lawyers, financial experts, a bank manager and dozens of individuals, with money laundered through company takeovers. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Criminal groups are becoming more and more creative with their distribution and payment methods."</sub>

## Cloaking Drug Trafficking and Money Laundering in State Contracts in Fuel Transportation (Bolivia, FIU-Bolivia) 9

- **descriptive / case example**: Bolivia case 9: an investigation into cross-border drug transport identified vast sums and many assets (e.g. fuel tanker trucks); several companies were founded mainly for fuel transport or sale through family-owned service stations, and all operations were used to clear illicit funds as in FATF typologies and warning signs. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "All these operations were used to clear illicit funds, as spelled out in the typologies and warning signs"</sub>

## CASE

- **descriptive / case example**: Financial intelligence identified links in a criminal organization that used contracts with a state organization to facilitate international trafficking of marijuana and cocaine in fuel trucks; the illegal money was laundered through legal companies and front companies related to fuel transport run by different family members. Countries: Argentina, Bolivia; sectors: transport, fuel tankers. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "used contracts with a state organization to facilitate international trafficking of controlled substances"</sub>

## The Investigation

- **descriptive / case example**: Analysis began with Company C, acquired in December 2007 by spouses X and Y, whose main activity was transport of national and international cargo; before purchase it earned about USD 4,500 annual profit over four years and afterwards an average of about USD 33,300. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "approximately USD 4,500 in the last four years"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In 2015 the Public Ministry asked FIU-Bolivia to analyse financial operations of family members investigated for drug trafficking; six obliged subjects filed seven STRs that year on spouses X and Y and family, mainly eldest son A. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Six obliged subjects also presented seven suspicious transaction reports to FIU-Bolivia that same year"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Under the family, Company C multiplied assets and earnings quickly, with family members operating under Company C and other front companies so a lot of money could be moved; X and Y used Company C to transport controlled substances freely through state fuel transportation contracts, fuel delivery being the cover; its success led the family to create other companies and recruit relatives. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The success of this methodology encouraged the family group to create other companies in the same category"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: All financial and equity information was classified and analysed to detect changes in transaction patterns and concentration of resources among beneficiaries; the analysis focused on the origin and use of money compared with the socioeconomic and financial profile of the people involved, including relationships among depositors or beneficiaries. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "in comparison with the socioeconomic and financial profile of the people involved"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: X and Y owned Company C and managed local and foreign currency accounts at different institutions with deposits exceeding USD 120 million; about 45% could be accounted for by state fuel transport contracts, 10% by presumably legal transactions with client companies, and 45% had unknown source and destination. Children A, B, C and D and companies C1 to C5 were involved in transactions with Company C and each other. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "exceeded deposits of USD 120 million"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Because 14 people were involved, FIU-Bolivia applied a new analysis technique focused on three people (X, Y and A), analysing the rest through links and transactions leading back to them; it showed funds moving among A's family members and personal accounts and links between A's corporate accounts without correlation between A's personal accounts and X-Y corporate earnings. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIU-Bolivia applied a new analysis technique to account for this large number"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Analysis with the Public Ministry identified illicit traffic in controlled substances as a previous crime and established legitimation of illicit profits as the main crime. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identified ILLICIT TRAFFIC IN CONTROLLED SUBSTANCES as a previous crime"</sub>

## FIU Action

- **descriptive / case example**: After completing financial and equity analysis, FIU-Bolivia sent the intelligence report to the Public Ministry; it gathered information from INTERPOL, the Directorate General of Traffic, Customs, the Minister of Industry and Commerce, the Central Bank of Bahrain (as written) and bodies such as the Egmont Group, seeking real estate and vehicle registrations, additional economic activities in the Registry of Commerce (FUNDAEMPRESA) or departmental governments, taxes and migration data. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "registration of real estate and vehicles;"</sub>

## Evolution of the Case

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The case involved a year of investigation before any suspects were arrested and identified a new legal interpretation of money laundering from drug trafficking via a fuel transport company; the family moved a sizable quantity of drugs across the Bolivia-Argentina border. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The case involved a year of investigation before any suspects were arrested."</sub>

## Outcome/Contribution of the Case

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Public Ministry coordinated effectively with FIU-Bolivia, and information from FIU-Argentina helped precisely identify links among family members; this led to arrests and extradition of the main defendant to face drug trafficking proceedings in Argentina; the case was at oral trial stage in Bolivia, where the Financial and Asset Intelligence Report will be useful. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the information provided by FIU-Argentina helped to precisely identify the links between the members of the family"</sub>

## Valuable Indicators of the Case

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Bolivia case indicators: transfer of significant funds to third-party bank accounts; obtaining credits that were paid in advance; clients or relatives with a history of investigation for illicit drug trafficking; a low-earning company suddenly generating significant income in a short time. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Low-earning company begins to generate a significant income in a short time"</sub>

## Internet Pharmacies Distributing Illegal Drugs Worldwide (India, FIUIND) 10

- **descriptive / case example**: India case 10: psychotropic substances were trafficked by misdeclaration using illegal Internet pharmacies, with remittances from abroad received to launder proceeds; those involved ran a software business maintaining websites, used post parcel/courier routes, received foreign remittances via fake bank accounts and money transfer service scheme (MTSS) franchises; besides the website company, five companies, 19 bank accounts and property over INR 220 million were identified; an STR disseminated by FIU-IND to law enforcement initiated the case. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Psychotropic substances were trafficked by misdeclaration using illegal Internet pharmacies"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In the India internet-pharmacies case (FIU-IND), the countries involved are Australia, Costa Rica, Denmark, France and the United States, and the sectors involved are pharmaceuticals and the money transfer industry. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Australia, Costa Rica, Denmark, France and United States"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Mr. A told the bank that the remittances were sent by his friends for his personal use, but the details of the remitters showed no apparent relationship with the receiver. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Mr. A had informed the bank that the remittances were sent by his friends for his personal use."</sub>

## The Investigation

- **descriptive / case example**: FIU-IND analysed a bank STR and disclosed it to LEA-A (agency dealing with money laundering and foreign exchange violations), which sent it to LEA-B (agency fighting drug trafficking and psychotropic substances abuse). Enquiries showed Mr. A had submitted a forged driver's licence to the MTSS franchisee, and when questioned said the remittances pertained to his brother Mr. B. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Mr. A had submitted a forged driver’s licence to the MTSS franchisee"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Mr. B had worked nearly three years in customer support and data entry at purported software company M/s ABC Softech and handled its MTSS work; surveillance showed the company, whose stated work was creating websites, sent drugs and medicines abroad and received MTSS payments in fake persons' names using forged identification; payments funded medicines including psychotropic drugs, distributed in post parcels through India Post. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "receive MTSS payments in the name of fake persons based on forged identi- fication documents"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The company created many websites with medicine-related names, set up customer support to follow orders, and collected payments through e-cheques and online e-payment portals. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The company cre- ated customer support to follow up on the orders."</sub>

## FIU Action

- **descriptive / case example**: The company received foreign payments through two MTSS franchises owned by Mr. X and wife Ms. X (also directors of M/s ABC Softech), operated from the company's office with records kept at drug store M/s Pharma ABC, also owned by Mr. X; the company procured drugs from Delhi, Mumbai and M/s Pharma ABC. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "which were owned by Mr. X and his wife Ms. X"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The case began with an STR for only INR 130,000; averaged over 11 payouts in three years that is just under INR 12,000 each, not significant in itself, but the STR arose because the remittances came from abroad to a person with no obvious reason to receive them, leading to an organized drug trafficking racket. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "which by itself is not very significant"</sub>

## Internet Pharmacies Distributing Illegal Drugs Worldwide (India, FIUIND) 10

- **descriptive / case example**: The STR analysed by FIU-IND stated that, over a three-year period starting January 2015, an MTSS branch made 11 pay-outs to Mr. A amounting to INR 130,000 in total. These amounts came from various persons in Costa Rica, the United States, Denmark, Australia and France. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These amounts were received from various persons from Costa Rica, the United States, Denmark, Australia and France."</sub>

## FIU Action

- **descriptive / case example**: M/s ABC Softech was smuggling pharmaceutical formulations containing narcotic or psychotropic substances to overseas buyers by international post; the business appeared large, with a call centre, drug store and MTSS franchises interconnected; after searches produced bank account details, FIU-IND linked six additional accounts and shared them with LEA-B. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIU-IND, which in turn was able to link six additional bank accounts and share that information with LEA-B"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Because psychotropic substances were exported to the U.S. and many remittances came from a U.S. company, Uncle Sam LLC, the U.S. DEA (through its New Delhi office) was asked to help; it found the company's overseas address was incorrect and it did not exist there, confirming money laundering suspicions. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the overseas address of Uncle Sam LLC was not correct, and the company did not exist at that address"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In early August 2018, intelligence indicated M/s Pharma ABC would send 44 parcels containing drugs, some with psychotropic formulations, to the US, UK and Hungary via the Foreign Post Office in New Delhi; they were intercepted with simultaneous searches at four locations including Mr. X's residence, recovering over 22,000 tablets of psychotropic formulations (Tramadol, Diazepam, Zolpidem, Clonazepam, Alprazolam, Nitrazepam), controlled under the NDPS Act 1985; two people including the head of the operation were arrested. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These searches recovered over 22,000 tablets that were formulations of psychotropic substances"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Dealing with the controlled substances requires appropriate licences and import and export needs a No Objection Certificate of the Narcotics Commissioner; the predicate offence is violation of NDPS Act section 8 (prohibition of certain operations) and section 21 (contravention relating to manufactured drugs and preparations). (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "requires a No Objection Certificate of the Narcotics Commissioner"</sub>

## Evolution of the Case

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Forfeiture of identified properties is proceeding under due process, two individuals have been arrested, and prosecution of all offenders will follow completion of the investigation; FIU-IND worked closely with LEA-A and LEA-B on tracing accounts, the drug trafficking chain and financial flows including from the U.S. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Action for forfeiture of the properties identified is being taken following due process."</sub>

## Valuable Indicators of the Case

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: India case indicators: multiple small foreign incoming remittances from multiple countries; unsatisfactory reasons for receipt of the money; several entities owned by the same person. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Multiple foreign incoming remittances of small amounts from multiple countries"</sub>

## Outcome/Contribution of the Case

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The matter is under active investigation; properties of about INR 220 million have been frozen; 19 bank accounts related to Mr. X or his companies held over INR 30 million accrued through foreign remittances; Mr. X used fake rubber stamps to prepare forged documents, leading to other criminal charges. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "having balance of over INR 30 million accrued through foreign remittances"</sub>

## Organized Crime Group Operating from Jail: The Methamphetamine Business Transactions (New Zealand, NZPFIU) 11

- **descriptive / case example**: New Zealand case 11: in April 2014 the NZPFIU began analysing suspicious transactions between Thailand and New Zealand and informed OFCANZ, which launched a criminal investigation the NZPFIU supported. Operation GANDOLF was a multi-agency methamphetamine importation investigation led by the Organised and Financial Crime Agency New Zealand (now the National Organised Crime Group), supported by NZ Customs, the Department of Corrections and the NZPFIU. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Operation GANDOLF was a multi-agency investigation into the importation of methamphetamine."</sub>

## Introduction

- **descriptive / case example**: Between February and October 2014 Atta was imprisoned at Rimutaka Prison for importation of cocaine and aggravated robbery; between January and July 2014 about NZD 1,000,000 was sent from New Zealand to Thailand via international money transfer, enough to buy about 5 kilograms of methamphetamine worth NZD 5,000,000 resold in New Zealand. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "approximately NZD 1,000,000 was sent to Thailand from New Zealand via international money transfer"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The primary target was Egyptian national Mohamed Soliman Hussain Atta, serving a 10.5-year term at Rimutaka Prison, at the centre of an organized crime group importing and distributing methamphetamine across New Zealand; he used several mobile phones in custody to facilitate this. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Atta was identified as having access to and using several mobile phones while in custody"</sub>

## The Investigation

- **descriptive / case example**: Police intercepted calls where Atta and other prisoners used the term 'back door', meaning a corrections officer who smuggles contraband into the prison for payment; in April 2013 a New Zealand group was identified importing crystal methamphetamine from a Thailand group, sending money and receiving parcels at an agreed New Zealand address. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "refers to a corrections officer who smuggles contraband into the prison in return for payment"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Atta was assisted by his wife Linda Olive Aldworth, who provided mobile top-up vouchers and did bank transactions at his request, and was jointly charged with him with conspiracy to import methamphetamine. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "She provided him with mobile phone top-up vouchers and completed bank transactions at his request."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Keywords: methamphetamines, drug trafficking, organized crime, Rimutaka; countries: New Zealand and Thailand; sectors: banking, pharmaceuticals, import/export, money transfer industry. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "banking, pharmaceuticals, import/ export, money transfer industry"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Atta's modus operandi from intercepted communications and interviews, steps 1-4: a customer wanting methamphetamine contacted Atta on unlawfully held cell phones; Atta obtained a name from his Thailand supplier; the customer remitted money through an international money remittance provider to the person named by the supplier; the customer gave Atta the money transfer control number (MTCN) with a name and delivery address. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Atta’s customer would remit money using an international money remittance service provider"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Steps 5-7: Atta forwarded the MTCN, name and address to the Thailand supplier who collected the money; the supplier then posted the methamphetamine to the New Zealand address; on receipt the customer paid Atta a commission deposited in Aldworth's bank account. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the customer would pay Atta a commission, which was deposited in Aldworth’s bank account"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: A production order revealed texts from Atta to Aldworth asking her to pay money to a bank account linked to Tuisamoa; in February 2015 police searched Tuisamoa's home finding handwritten notes, diary entries, mobile phones, SIM cards and chargers, and items supplied by Tuisamoa including a mobile phone were found in Atta's cell and used to facilitate importation. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In February 2015, police executed a search warrant at Tuisamoa’s home address"</sub>

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Initial NZPFIU analysis found that between February and April 2014 eight New Zealand-based persons in four regions facilitated 29 transactions to five Thai-based persons totalling NZD 178,451; the NZPFIU also found links to active Police and Customs operations, gangs and prisoners, and individuals with criminal convictions including drug offences. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "had facili- tated 29 separate transactions to five Thai-based persons, amounting to a total value of NZD 178,451"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Operation GANDOLF involved 69 STRs containing 486 individual transactions; during intelligence collection Corrections voiced concern about corrupt practices of various corrections officers at Rimutaka Prison. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Operation GANDOLF involved a total of 69 STRs containing 486 individual transactions."</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: NZPFIU analysis techniques (list truncated at the end of the excerpt) included collection of relevant information from police databases and open source information. (p. 56)[^original]

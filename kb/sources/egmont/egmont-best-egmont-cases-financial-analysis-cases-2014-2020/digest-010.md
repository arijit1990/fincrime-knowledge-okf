## TRADE-BASED MONEY LAUNDERING AND THIRD-PARTY MONEY LAUNDERING

- **descriptive / context**: Per FATF and the Egmont Group, interconnected world supply chains make trade inherently complex; organized crime, professional money launderers and terrorist financing networks exploit them to launder proceeds (e.g., drug trafficking), finance terrorism and evade sanctions. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Organized criminal groups, professional money launderers and terrorist financing networks exploit these supply chains"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Many competent authorities (customs, law enforcement, FIUs, tax authorities, banking supervisors) find trade-based money laundering harder to identify and combat than other ML/TF forms, and even those with advanced knowledge are impeded by the sophistication and evolving nature of the techniques. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "have a harder time identify- ing and combatting trade-based money laundering"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FIUs play a key role in strategic analyses of their databases to identify ML/TF typologies and emerging phenomena, in ongoing independent operative analyses for tracing suspicious targets, and in alerting competent authorities to illicit activity their analyses identify. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIUs play a key role in conducting strategic analyses of their databases"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Cases in this section: FIU-Bahrain unravelled an international ML scheme using swift investigative tools and analysis with domestic and international cooperation. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIU-Bahrain used its swift investigative tools and efficient analytical techniques"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FIU-Mexico worked with US FinCEN to dismantle international ML networks involving dozens of entities, transactions to dozens of countries and more than 1,500 beneficiaries. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "transactions to dozens of countries and more than 1,500 beneficiaries."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Israel's IMPA provided financial intelligence that led to investigating a cross-border professional ML scheme piggy-backing on the diamond trade, contributing to the EGMONT/FATF typologies report on Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing through Trade in Diamonds. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a cross-border professional money laundering scheme that piggy-backed on the diamond trade."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Korea's FIU gave the Korea Customs Service financial information on a fake export/import company, used to identify offences such as false export/import declarations, property concealment and money laundering costing Korean import agencies and commercial banks more than KRW 50 billion. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "that were costing Korean import agencies and commercial banks more than KRW 50 billion."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The cases show the international nature of these crimes demands cooperation among actors, agencies and FIUs and point to measures that can prevent trade-based and third-party money laundering. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These cases demonstrate how the international nature of these types of crimes demand cooperation"</sub>

## CASE

- **descriptive / context**: Bahrain (FID) case 'Unravelling an International Money Laundering Scheme Tied to Cyberfraud': keywords third-party and trade-based money laundering; countries Bahrain, Belgium, India, Denmark, Sweden, Germany, UK, Norway, US, France, Canada, Netherlands; sector financial. (p. 88)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Kingdom of Bahrain, Belgium, India, Denmark, Sweden, Germany, United Kingdom, Norway"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Bahrain's Financial Intelligence Directorate (FID) unravelled the scheme after a Bahrain money exchange institution filed an STR on an individual making several overseas cash transfers whose amounts did not match his profile or have a convincing legitimate source; domestic and international cooperation and other techniques brought perpetrators to justice. (p. 88)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "for amounts that did not match the individual’s profile or have a convincing legitimate source"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: All 14 beneficiaries used eight exchange institutions for 451 transfers totalling BHD 157,208 (USD 418,107) between January 27, 2014 and May 4, 2017; eight individuals in India received them under orders from Subject 1, who managed the transfers for his friend Subject 2, and 192 transfers totalling BHD 108,907 (USD 289,647) went to India between March 26, 2015 and July 27, 2016 via seven exchange institutions. (p. 88)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "All 14 beneficiaries used eight exchange institutions to make 451 transfers"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Subject 1's organization in Bahrain received 179 transfers totalling BHD 105,638 (USD 280,181) from September 7, 2015 to July 24, 2016; these were proceeds of cyber fraud in 10 countries affecting 118 victims and were passed to 14 beneficiaries in Bahrain. (p. 88)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "proceeds of CYBER FRAUD crimes in 10 different countries affecting 118 victims"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Suspects confirmed they received a 2% commission on amounts received; some handed commissions in cash to Subject 1, who sent them to his Indian bank account via exchange companies and to Indian beneficiaries; Subject 1's Bahrain account showed only his monthly salary (about BHD 350, USD 931), part of which he sent to his accounts in India. (p. 88)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "received a commission of 2% of the amounts received"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: A meeting of competent authorities in Bahrain's Ministry of Interior, which regularly discusses interlinked trends, linked a similar case with the same pattern and a parallel investigation involving FID and INTERPOL was launched. (p. 88)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a similar case was linked that used the same pattern and a parallel investigation was launched"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: FID used its authority to question suspects, which helped transfer the case to the Public Prosecution Office. (p. 88)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FID used its authority to question the suspects"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Further investigation of the TalkTalk scam found two Bahrain suspects (subjects 3 and 4) received 50 fraudulent electronic transfers from UK persons between February 17 and May 21, 2016 totalling BHD 82,223 (USD 218,637): Subject 3 35 transfers of BHD 55,672 (USD 148,064) and Subject 4 15 of BHD 26,596 (USD 70,774); these went on to three individuals in India via 39 transfers totalling BHD 87,178 (USD 231,856). (p. 88)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "received 50 fraudulent electronic transfers between February 17, 2016, and May 21, 2016"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Public Prosecution investigations found the suspects' operations were controlled by Subject 1 while the others were forced to conduct the transactions. (p. 88)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "while the others were forced to conduct these transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: FID's further actions: searched its database for criminal history, STR records and travel history of all known senders and receivers; summoned Subject 2's accounts; obtained the phone call registers of Subjects 1 and 2; and retrieved records of all transactions in Bahrain where Subjects 1 and 2 were main beneficiaries. (p. 89)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FID database was searched for criminal history, STR records and travel history"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The case began with an STR from a money exchange institution on Subject 1, an Indian expat working as a chef in Bahrain, flagged because he transferred funds from different branches of the same exchange house in amounts below the reporting threshold and the total did not fit his financial profile. (p. 89)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "amounts not exceeding the reporting threshold; in addition, the total amount"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: FID followed protocol, rating the STR as medium risk, meaning it needed further investigation, and summoned the accounts and records of all Subject 1's transactions in Bahrain. (p. 89)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FID followed protocol, rating the STR as medium risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Subject 1 received several small transfers from individuals in three countries and shortly after sent similar amounts to three main individuals in India, his country of origin, and also transferred funds with Subject 2, another person of the same nationality in Bahrain. (p. 89)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "received several small-transaction transfers from several other individuals from three countries"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: FID used the Egmont Secure Web to send requests to five FIUs of the senders' countries; one FIU replied that multiple senders had complained to local police of an Internet scam hacking personal computers and hoaxing victims into sending funds to a Bahrain subject who forwarded them to the original perpetrator in India; FID notified India that the crime originated within its jurisdiction. (p. 89)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FID sent requests to five FIUs for the countries of the senders of the transfers"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Egmont Group's smooth, swift channels for exchanging intelligence were described as the crucial factor in untangling the case, given the size of the cyber fraud network. (p. 90)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "effectiveness in facilitating smooth and swift channels through which FID could communicate"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: FID located and traced all Bahrain suspects, who were captured, and BHD 105,638 (USD 280,952) of illegally obtained funds was seized; the work with foreign FIUs identified a criminal organization run by Subject 1 and associates. (p. 90)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "BHD 105,638 (USD 280,952) in illegally obtained funds was seized"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Under Articles 64 and 111 of Bahrain Decree-Law No. 4 of 2001 on prohibition and combatting of money laundering, subjects 1, 2 and 3 were found guilty of money laundering and cyber fraud and sentenced to five years in prison and fined BHD 5,000 (USD 13,298). (p. 90)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Article 64 and 111 from Decree-Law No. 4 of the year 2001"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Foreign FIUs' response times and sufficiently detailed intelligence let FID unravel the web of fraudulent transfers; linking over 10 countries was possible only through Egmont Secure channels and each FIU's commitment to thorough reports. (p. 90)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Linking over 10 countries was a group effort that could be achieved only through Egmont Secured Channels"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The case shows the importance of cooperation; financial institutions are essential, particularly for raising red flags, and it was the money exchange institution's vigilance that first alerted FID. (p. 90)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial institutions are an essential factor to such cases, particularly for raising red flags."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Valuable indicator: an individual performing several transactions below reporting thresholds that combined amount to much more than expected for that individual's standing. (p. 90)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Individual performing several transactions for amounts below reporting thresholds but, when combined"</sub>

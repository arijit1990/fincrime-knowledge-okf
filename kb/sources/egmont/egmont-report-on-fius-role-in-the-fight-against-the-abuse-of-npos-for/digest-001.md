## Abstract

- **descriptive / context**: The Egmont Group IEWG developed this Public Summary (April 2024) to support efforts against abuse of NPOs for TF by identifying, updating and expanding existing information to develop new knowledge on typologies and financial threats. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identifying, updating, and expanding on existing information to develop new knowledge on typologies and financial threats"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Egmont Group is described as a united body of 174 FIUs providing a platform for secure exchange of expertise and financial intelligence to combat ML/TF. The Project leads were FIU Israel (IMPA) and FIU Nigeria (NFIU); team members were Australia (AUSTRAC), Canada (FINTRAC), Finland (RAP), Greece (Hellenic FIU), Mauritius (FIU-Mauritius), the Netherlands (FIU-NL), South Africa (FIC) and the UK (UK-FIU). (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Egmont Group is a united body of 174 Financial Intelligence Units (FIUs)."</sub>

## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

- **descriptive / context**: The Project aims to support efforts against NPO abuse for TF and to give an overview of how FIUs make best use of international cooperation by sharing financial information and intelligence to enhance detection and disrupt NPO abuse to finance terrorism. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "how FIUs make the best use of international cooperation by sharing financial information and intelligence"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The NPO sector poses great challenges for FIUs due to its scale, distinctive structures and nature of work. NPOs often work internationally, in or adjacent to conflict-afflicted areas, and those on humanitarian missions operate in the same environments and among the same vulnerable populations as terrorist entities, which can make NPOs susceptible to exploitation and even complicit with terrorist organizations. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "NPOs often work internationally, in or adjacent to conflict-afflicted areas"</sub>

## INTRODUCTION

- **descriptive / context**: The FATF has established Recommendation 8 specifically for NPOs, outlining measures to prevent NPO misuse for TF. Concerns were raised that these measures may have unintended consequences, including de-risking and financial exclusion, so the FATF initiated a project to update its Best Practices Paper on Combatting the Abuse of NPOs, which led to amendments to Recommendation 8 and its interpretative note. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FATF initiated a project to update the Best Practices Paper on Combatting the Abuse of NPOs"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report supplements FATF work with a refined understanding of how NPOs are exploited for TF and, by giving a more nuanced view of NPO risk, should contribute to initiatives to prevent unintended consequences for NPOs at low or no risk of abuse. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "preventing unintended consequences for NPOs that are at low or no risk of abuse"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Understanding of NPO-abuse threats is uneven among FIUs; some threats persist and others evolve. Terrorist organizations have evolved TF typologies partly in response to countermeasures, including abusing NPOs through modern fundraising methods such as DeFi, virtual assets and FinTech platforms to raise and transfer funds. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The level of understanding of threats targeting NPO abuse for TF purposes is uneven among FIUs."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Given the high risk and considerable global growth of the NPO sector, FIUs play a vital role in identifying and detecting TF-related transactions, and the international nature of NPO activity highlights the importance of international cooperation and timely information sharing between FIUs. This requires effective long-term CTF strategies and preventive measures by FIUs and partner agencies, focusing on operational exchanges, strategic intelligence and analysis. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "requires the establishment of effective long-term CTF strategies and preventive measures by FIUs and their partner agencies"</sub>

## METHODOLOGY

- **descriptive / method**: The Project was implemented in five stages: (i) assessment of existing knowledge from international organizations; (ii) a questionnaire on abuse of the non-profit sector for TF sent to all Egmont Group members; (iii) a call for sanitized operational cases, which are not included in this sanitised version; (iv) analysis of FIU responses; and (v) compilation of a report on NPO abuse for TF and the role of FIUs. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A questionnaire about the abuse of the non-profit sector for TF was sent to all Egmont Group members."</sub>

## LITERATURE REVIEW

- **descriptive / context**: Since 2001 the FATF has prioritized combatting TF including in the non-profit sector. In June 2014 it published a typologies report on terrorist abuse of NPOs with red flag indicators to help stakeholders identify and investigate possible abuse. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In June 2014, the FATF published a typologies reportii on the risk of terrorist abuse in NPOs"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In 2016 the FATF revised its standard on protecting NPOs from misuse, moving from a blanket approach to a targeted focus on the 'subset' of NPOs most at risk, recognising that not all NPOs are exposed to risk and that some or many pose little or no TF risk. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "shifting from a blanket approach to a targeted focus on the ‘subset’ of NPOs at most risk of misuse"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In November 2023 the FATF published updated standards in which wording of sections of Recommendation 8 and its interpretive notes was revised to address excessive use of preventive measures on the NPO sector in some countries; also in November 2023 it published ongoing updates to its Best Practices Paper on Combatting the Terrorist Financing Abuse of Non-Profit Organisations. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to address the issue of excessive use of preventive measures on the NPO sector in some countriesiii"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report concludes from available literature that NPO abuse for TF is a global concern with many information sources at international and regional level. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the phenomenon is a global concern with many sources of information found at the international and regional level"</sub>

## NPO DEFINITION

- **descriptive / definition**: Given the variety of legal forms, the FATF adopted a functional definition of NPO based on the activities and characteristics that put an organization at risk of TF abuse, rather than simply on operating on a non-profit basis. The FATF defines an NPO as a legal person, arrangement or organization that primarily engages in raising or disbursing funds for charitable, religious, cultural, educational, social or fraternal purposes, or for other types of "good works". (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "primarily engages in raising or disbursing funds for purposes such as charitable, religious, cultural, educational, social or fraternal purposes"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: NPO and NGO are often used interchangeably because both are non-profit oriented and often engaged in charitable work, but differ slightly in scope: NGOs tend to have a broader operational and outreach footprint. The UN defines an NGO as any non-profit, voluntary citizens' group organized at local, national or international level. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "NGOs tend to have a broader operational and outreach footprint than NPOsvi"</sub>

## PROMINENT TYPOLOGIES

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The literature identifies six methods prevalent in abuse of NPOs for TF: diversion of funds; affiliation with a terrorist entity; abuse of NPO programs; providing support for recruitment; false representation; and fundraising through social media. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This literature identifies six methods prevalent in the abuse of NPOs for TF purposes"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Diversion of funds is the most commonly observed typology: actors within or outside an NPO divert funds raised for humanitarian purposes toward terrorist activities, with or without the NPO's knowledge. Transfers can also use third parties such as sham organizations and trade-based terrorist financing (TBTF), as highlighted in Section 2 of the 2020 FATF-Egmont Group publication on the subject. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "divert the funds raised for humanitarian purposes towards terrorist activities with or without the NPO's knowledge"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Affiliation with a terrorist entity is the second most commonly observed method: it means providing financial and/or operational support for a terrorist group, ranging from NPO officials and employees having suspected or established ties to a terrorist entity to more formalized relationships between the parties. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The second most commonly observed method is NPO’s affiliation with a terrorist entity"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Terrorist entities can abuse NPO programs at the point of delivery by 'skimming' charitable donations and sending or using them to support terrorist activities. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "at the point of delivery to support terrorism by 'skimming'x off charitable donations"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: NPO-funded programs or facilities can be exploited to create an environment that knowingly or unknowingly supports recruitment by terrorist organizations. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "NPO-funded programs or facilities can be exploited to create an environment"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: False representation means organizations or individuals falsely raising money for charitable purposes while using it to support terrorism, in two categories: (1) groups or individuals falsely claiming to act for existing NPOs, for example a 2011 Birmingham bomb plot partly financed by terrorist cell members posing as Muslim Aid volunteers, which UK intelligence and security services thwarted; and (2) NPOs created as a front to support terrorist activities. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "terrorist cell members posing as Muslim Aid volunteersxi"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The rapid expansion of social media, particularly encrypted messaging platforms, is increasingly exploited by terrorist entities to raise funds from sympathetic individuals globally. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "particularly encrypted messaging platforms, are being increasingly exploited by terrorist entities to raise funds"</sub>

## PROMINENT RISKS AND THREATS

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Globalization and technological advances have made it relatively easy to transfer funds or goods globally, which enabled NPOs to expand fundraising and outreach and drew them into unstable high-risk regions where the integrity of their operations is at risk. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "drawing NPOs into unstable high-risk regions where the integrity of their operations is at risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Emergency response situations such as pandemics or natural disasters have increased the risk of NPO-related TF: they prompt more fundraising by legitimate and sham NPOs, and terrorist entities can exploit them to raise funds via sham NPOs or abuse legitimate NPOs to divert resources. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Emergency response situations, such as pandemics or natural disasters, have increased the risk of NPO-related TF."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Cash couriers pose a significant risk for NPOs as a recognized method of moving funds for terrorist purposes; cash disguised as legitimate transactions under an NPO and charitable cause may be less likely to be questioned. Where financial infrastructure is limited or not functioning, cash becomes one of few options for moving funds to beneficiaries, and de-risking and financial institutions restricting wire transfers are reasons some NPOs resort to cash couriering. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "De-risking and financial institutions restricting wire transfers are reasons some NPOs resort to cash couriering."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report notes that political considerations, especially during periods of election campaigning, may influence the monitoring of elements affiliated with or sympathetic to extremist causes. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "political considerations, especially during periods of election campaigning, may influence the monitoring of elements"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The report says this threat should be monitored closely in the coming years, given the ever-increasing rise in popularity of the far-right. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This threat should be monitored closely in the coming years due to the ever-increasing rise in popularity"</sub>

## PROMINENT VULNERABILITIES

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Terrorist organizations pose a higher risk to NPOs in regions plagued by war, weak governance (including financial controls, risk management policies and procedures, and appropriate due diligence), corruption and poor infrastructure. Areas where humanitarian relief is most needed are often where terrorism risk is greatest, because terrorist networks and NPOs often rely on similar logistical capabilities. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "terrorist networks and NPOs often rely on similar logistical capabilities that are consistent through domestic or foreign operations"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: With more individuals, wider ranges of activities and possibly large geographic distances, it can be difficult for NPOs to maintain adequate control of resources, scrutinize staff actions, perform due diligence and beneficiary verifications and identify suspicious activity, making it challenging to distinguish charitable activity from terrorist support. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "difficult for NPOs to maintain adequate control of resources, scrutinize staff actions, perform due diligence and beneficiary verifications"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Under current legislation and reporting requirements, authorities address misuse after it has occurred. NPO awareness of TF risk varies, limiting their ability to identify and protect themselves from financial misuse, and limited visibility of the funding cycle (fundraising through to sending or spending domestically then abroad) compounds the vulnerabilities of the cash-intensive nature of most NPOs. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Current legislation and reporting requirements mean that relevant authorities are addressing misuse after it has already occurred."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The key NPO vulnerabilities relate to their social purpose, cash-intensive nature, generally minimal regulatory oversight, and the social role and inherent trust associated with NPOs in the community; embedding TF operations in the regular activities of a trusted organization is described as ideal cover. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "social purpose, cash-intensive nature, generally minimal regulatory oversight applied to their operations"</sub>

## ISSUES CONCERNING SPECIFIC NPO TYPES/CATEGORIES

- **descriptive / finding or result**: NPOs providing humanitarian services and/or operating in unstable high-risk environments are the most vulnerable to abuse. NPOs operating abroad risk abuse of funds or goods at the point of distribution by the charity or partner organizations. Domestic NPOs within a population actively targeted by a terrorist movement for support and cover are also exposed, since locally generated resources may be transferred internationally if the organization does not direct and control end-use. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "NPOs that are engaged in providing humanitarian services and/or operating in unstable high-risk environments are the most vulnerable to abusexxii"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Unregistered NPOs are highly vulnerable to TF activity because they are subject to minimal to no oversight and little is known about their operations. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Unregistered NPOs are highly vulnerable to TF activity as they are subject to minimal to no oversight"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: There is heightened ML/TF risk with religious organizations, particularly religious NPOs operating in environments or among populations that terrorist entities actively target. Sunni terrorist groups such as al Qaeda and ISIS exploit the Islamic principle of Zakat, under which a person is required to give 2.5% of annual excess wealth to charity, to raise finance from Islamic communities. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "exploit the Islamic principle of Zakat to raise finance from Islamic communities around the globe"</sub>

## UNINTENDED CONSEQUENCES

- **descriptive / finding or result**: De-risking, which the FATF describes as financial institutions terminating or restricting business relationships with clients or categories of clients to avoid rather than manage risk, may push NPOs to use high-risk, unsupervised informal value transfer systems (IVTS), creating visibility gaps. Undue targeting of NPOs may also prevent delivery of genuine relief aid, curtail human rights and cause wrongful financial exclusion. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may push NPOs to use high-risk, unsupervised informal value transfer systems (IVTS)"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Measures to mitigate NPO exploitation for TF should be balanced against NPOs' continued ability to undertake legitimate activities such as humanitarian aid; given potential inadvertent harm, a risk-based approach should be implemented. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a risk-based approach should be implemented"</sub>

## PROMINENT INDICATORS

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The prominent indicators of which NPOs are at higher risk of abuse are the value of their resources or activities to terrorist entities and their proximity to an active terrorist threat with capacity and intent to exploit NPOs. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the value of their resources or activities to terrorist entities and the proximity to an active terrorist threat"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: 'Service NPOs' primarily delivering humanitarian services are the most desirable targets for terrorist entities because of the resources involved, the geographic scope (supply chain and logistics networks) and access to vulnerable populations. NPOs in locations or among populations terrorists target are at higher risk, which does not always relate to conflict areas or poor governance, since populations in relatively stable environments may be targeted due to affiliation with a conflict, ideology, or religious or ethnic group. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "'service NPOs' primarily involved in the delivery of humanitarian services are the most desirable targets for terrorist entities"</sub>

## SECTION I – THE NPO SECTOR

- **descriptive / context**: The NPO sector provides essential financial assistance and services to vulnerable populations. Addressing the threat requires understanding NPOs' environment, sector vulnerabilities, how terrorists exploit them, and how threats have been detected and managed, which lets FIUs update typologies and improve detection and prevention. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The NPO sector plays a vital societal role by providing essential financial assistance and services"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Information was collected on the number of NPOs per jurisdiction and on registration requirements and supervision or monitoring. Based on respondents' feedback, no significant correlation was found between the number of supervision measures and the risk perception of NPO abuse for TF. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "no significant correlation was found between the number of supervision measures and the risk perception"</sub>

## THE REGULATION AND SCALE OF NPO SECTORS

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Figure 1 shows 90% of respondents require registration of NPOs and 10% do not. In most jurisdictions NPOs must also keep information over time and submit financial statements. Registered NPO numbers are generally high, with some jurisdictions having over 100,000, and 40% of jurisdictions that do not register NPOs report sectors with over 100,000 known entities. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "noting 40% of jurisdictions that do not register NPOs, report sectors with over 100,000 known entities"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: NPO sectors are economically significant, with annual turnover of almost USD50 to USD196 billion in jurisdictions that collect and report the data. Lack of understanding of the NPO landscape and limited visibility and transparency of NPO funds create vulnerabilities to abuse for TF. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "annual turnover of almost USD50 to USD196 billion in jurisdictions that collect and report this data"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Some jurisdictions that do not register or license NPOs use other tools, such as taxation systems where NPOs enrol for tax concessions or where legal entities or bank accounts are mandatory to receive government benefits, but these are not equivalent to an effective registration regime. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these measures are not equivalent to an effective registration regime"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A significant number of jurisdictions use a range of regulatory methods to monitor and supervise NPOs. Less commonly used tools are requirements to confirm the identity, credentials and good standing of beneficiaries and to disclose donor identity, possibly because of strict data protection laws and lack of proper database management systems; this likely creates vulnerabilities TF actors can exploit. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Less commonly used tools include requirements to confirm the identity, credentials and good standing of beneficiaries"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Figure 2 (use of supervision/monitoring tools applied to NPOs; the chart values are not given in the text) is stated to show that some FIUs use a variety of tools, so percentages exceed 100%. NPO registration and licensing are common but not without problems. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "thereby resulting in the percentage total exceeding 100%"</sub>

## NPOS FINANCIAL ACTIVITY

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Terrorist organizations exploit NPOs in various ways to raise, move and store funds. Formerly money was distributed from high command to regional branches and cells through the formal banking system, which was fairly easy for law enforcement to identify and stop; sanctions and enforcement reduced this ability, forcing terrorist organizations to decentralize and diversify fundraising. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Terrorist organizations exploit NPOs in various ways to raise, move and store funds."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The shift from centralized structure to smaller self-sufficient affiliates means terrorist operations now need less funding. Where funds must still move, terrorists usually use VASPs, MSBs and/or informal hawala networks, along with trade-based terrorism financing (TBTF) and modern methods such as DeFi, cryptocurrency, virtual assets and FinTech platforms and applications. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they will usually use VASPs, MSBs and/or informal _hawala_ transfer networks"</sub>

## NPOS TURNOVER

- **descriptive / data or feature**: Figure 3 gives the share of jurisdictions in which each NPO category has the highest turnover: Health 26%, Social services 26%, Religion 23%, Education and research 19%, Environment 19%, Culture and recreation 16%, International activities 16%, Other 12%, Philanthropic intermediaries and voluntarism promotion 12%, Development and housing 9%, Law, advocacy and politics 9%, Business and professional associations, unions 5%. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Health 26%Social services 26%Religion 23%Education and research 19%Environment 19%"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: NPOs were categorized using the UNSD's ISIC, and information was collated on yearly turnover, source of funding and changes in financial activity over five years. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The NPOs were categorized based on the UNSD's ISIC"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FATF risk-based approach requires other factors, primarily the subset of NPOs (regardless of sector or turnover) that jurisdictions identify as most exposed to misuse. Presence of domestic and foreign terrorist groups in or near a jurisdiction, and NPOs operating in or near conflict zones, are the key risks, but abuse can occur elsewhere, e.g. the British charity Palestinian Relief and Development Fund (Interpal) was designated by the US government in 2003 for funding and supporting terrorism. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "designated by the US government in 2003 for funding and supporting terrorism"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: TF does not only occur near terrorist organizations; funds can be collected in areas less associated with terrorism, and NPOs and terrorist actors can use online payment systems to reach donors worldwide. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "NPOs and terrorist actors can utilize online payment systems, to gain access to donors"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Financial data viewed as standalone figures is not sufficient to indicate risk or vulnerability of each NPO category; 60% of respondents said the total yearly turnover of their NPO sector was unknown, and the small values in much TF activity make (sub)sector turnover a questionable risk indicator. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the majority of respondents (60%) stated that the total yearly turnover of the NPO sector in their country was unknown"</sub>

## NPOS SOURCES OF FUNDING

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Per FIU responses, government funding and domestic donations are the most popular NPO funding sources, and appear to be channelled into the highest-turnover categories (Health, Social Services and Religion). (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Government funding and domestic donations are the most popular sources of funding for NPOs"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: In theory government funding can reduce TF risk since governments are expected to conduct sufficient due diligence, but in practice governments often use private companies to examine NPOs; audits are often done by parties with limited knowledge of terrorism/TF, not properly certified and without access to government or intelligence information. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Audits are often performed by parties with limited knowledge and understanding of terrorism/terrorist financing."</sub>

## NPOS TURNOVER

- **expected / expectation**: Because the ability to raise and move funds is one of the biggest vulnerabilities abused by terrorist organizations, and Health, Social Services and Religion are among the NPO categories with the highest turnover, the report says this implies FIUs and competent authorities should pay more attention to NPOs in these categories to prevent their potential abuse for terrorism financing. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIUs and competent authorities should pay more attention to NPOs within these categories"</sub>

## CHANGES IN FINANCIAL ACTIVITY VOLUME OVER THE LAST 5 YEARS

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Most responding FIUs said NPO financial activity volume in their jurisdictions increased or stayed unchanged over five years, but only 27% responded to the question, so no definitive conclusions can be drawn. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the number of respondents to this question was particularly low (only 27%)"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The lack of data is concerning and may stem from non-implementation of supervisory measures, lack of knowledge or cooperation from the NPO sector, or insufficient expertise; it casts doubt on understanding of how the sector operates and its risks, and can lead to incorrect or ineffective supervisory and preventive measures. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the lack of information can lead to the implementation of incorrect or ineffective supervisory and preventive measures"</sub>

## NPOS SOURCES OF FUNDING

- **expected / expectation**: The report says that, to prevent TF, it is necessary for NPOs to examine their sources of funding more thoroughly, with an emphasis on donations. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is necessary for NPOs to conduct a more thorough examination of their sources of funding"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The report says that, to ensure this more thorough examination of funding sources, there is a need for increased NPO engagement and training and for appropriate supervisory measures to be implemented on NPOs. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is a need for increased NPO"</sub>

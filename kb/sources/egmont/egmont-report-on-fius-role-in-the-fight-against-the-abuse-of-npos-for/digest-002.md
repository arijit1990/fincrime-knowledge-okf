## NPOS SOURCES OF FUNDING

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Risks from the NPO sector can be linked to terrorist organizations abroad or in the jurisdiction. Internationally the primary risk is exploitation of NPOs in the region or neighboring countries, particularly high-risk areas or conflict zones; locally there are risks from domestic terrorist groups, and at domestic level from radicals and lone wolves. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the primary risk is the exploitation of NPOs in the region or neighboring countries"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Figure 4: 98% of FIU respondents said TF risks from the NPO sector were considered in their recent National Risk Assessments (NRAs) and 2% said no. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "98% of FIU respondents indicated that TF risks emanating from the NPO sector were taken into consideration"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In their NRAs nearly all participants assessed NPO-related TF risk as low to medium, with only 16% rating it high; the report explains this by the responding FIUs coming from jurisdictions with a low global terrorism financing index. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "nearly all participants assessed TF risks relating to NPOs as low to medium level, with only 16% rating it high"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Per the 2023 Global Terrorism Index, FIUs in jurisdictions most affected by terrorism and TF did not respond to the questionnaire. This may not overly alter broad findings, but further examination of TF abuse of NPOs in or near conflict zones is needed for a holistic picture. (A chart comparing the Global Terrorism Index of responding jurisdictions and all countries is present but its values are not legible in the text.) (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FIUs in jurisdictions mostly affected by Terrorism and Terrorism Financing activities did not respond to the questionnaire"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Abuse of NPOs for TF may be a less prominent funding source for terrorist groups than other typologies such as kidnapping for ransom, looting, extortion and drug trafficking, and limited NPO-related TF has likely shaped respondents' low to medium threat perception. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "such as kidnapping for ransom, looting, extortion, and drug trafficking"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: As seen in some mutual evaluation reports, some jurisdictions view TF risk too narrowly, linking it only to terrorist presence and acts in that jurisdiction, which can lead to incomplete and inaccurate risk assessments. TF can be significant for jurisdictions relatively immune to terrorism owing to financial and trade factors, migrant communities and geopolitical location. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the risk of TF is viewed too narrowly and linked to only the presence of terrorist risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Terrorist organizations exploit the high trust placed in NPOs and lax governmental supervision to operate in areas considered low terrorism risk. FIUs may fail to find suspicious transactions outside their designated focus areas and strategic activity, and insufficient information on NPO financial activity limits state risk assessment. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Terrorist organizations exploit the high trust that NPOs have in the country and lax governmental supervision"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: To gauge overall risk perception, FIU responses were summarized and normalized into a uniform measure. Risk perception was lower in jurisdictions with mandatory NPO registration than in those without; higher where FIUs sent more information requests to other FIUs involving NPO and TF, and where FIUs disseminated more NPO/TF reports; and lower where regulatory requirements were more stringent and where FIUs collaborated more with DNFBs. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "risk perception of jurisdictions where mandatory registration of NPOs is required is lower"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: For most FIUs, activities of terrorist groups in their jurisdiction or neighbouring countries or region are the key threats to NPOs. Other concerns are sham NPOs set up after natural disasters or epidemics, exploitation of charities in or near conflict zones, the ease of establishing and closing NPOs, and use of online platforms to solicit funds. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the establishment of sham NPOs following natural disasters or epidemics"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The NPO sector enjoys a high level of trust from governments and the public, but characteristics such as field of activity, operation among certain populations, geographic area, source of financing and others expose NPOs to TF abuse by terrorist organizations. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "certain sector characteristics expose NPOs to abuse by terrorist organizations for TF purposes"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: FIUs rated nine NPO characteristics for TF vulnerability; most respondents saw all characteristics as low to medium vulnerability, in line with their low-to-medium risk view. An operation involving a high-risk jurisdiction was seen as most vulnerable, followed by international activity and exemption from supervision requirements. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an operation involving a high-risk jurisdiction is the most vulnerable to TF abuse, followed by international activity"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Figure 5 is partly legible. Using the scale Very high / High / Medium / Low / Not at all, it gives these percentages in order of appearance (some rows show only four numbers and the text does not say which category is missing): Multiple beneficial owners 10, 5, 20, 50, 15; Source of financing 6, 13, 35, 46; Unclear objectives and mission 8, 7, 26, 44, 15; Operation involving high risk jurisdiction 14, 34, 17, 29, 6; International activity 14, 23, 23, 40; Volume of financial turnover 3, 11, 25, 57, 4; Operation among specific population or sects 9, 18, 27, 33, 13; Exemption from supervision requirements 21, 11, 14, 25, 29; Affiliation with certain classification 8, 4, 29, 38, 21. The text refers to nine characteristics but only these nine labels are legible. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Multiple beneficial owners 10% 5% 20% 50% 15%Source of financing 6% 13% 35% 46%"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: NPOs, particularly humanitarian service providers in economically and geopolitically unstable jurisdictions, are vulnerable to terrorist exploitation because NPO and terrorist target populations are typically intertwined, making it hard to tell terrorist support from charity. Limited NPO regulation in both low and high-risk jurisdictions increases abuse risk. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the target population/s for NPOs and terror organizations in such jurisdictions are typically intertwined"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Lack of adequate oversight lets terrorist groups set up sham NPOs or infiltrate legitimate non-profits to siphon off funds, lets NPOs obscure funding sources and gives donors relative anonymity; radicalized individuals or supporters of radical ideologies can exploit the lack of transparency to donate to terrorist organizations. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "set up sham NPOs or infiltrate legitimate non-profits with the aim of siphoning off funds"</sub>

## SECTION III – MEASURES

- **descriptive / limitation**: The project is limited to the FIU participant perspective and does not account for other government bodies that may provide regulatory oversight of NPOs in some jurisdictions. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "does not account for perspectives from other government bodies that might render regulatory oversight of NPOs"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: FIU participants rated mechanisms for international cooperation as the element contributing most to effectiveness against NPO-for-TF risks, followed by investigation and information gathering, supervision or monitoring and sustained outreach. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIU participants rated mechanisms for international cooperation as the element most contributing to the effectiveness"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Responding FIUs listed educating NPOs and other sectors about TF risks, typologies and mitigating factors as the main measure to ensure NPOs are not misused, followed by regular monitoring and supervision, consistent risk assessments, NPO-related legislation, and collaboration with other FIUs. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "educating NPOs and other sectors about TF risks, typologies and mitigating factors as the main measure"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In the responding FIUs' views, collaboration with financial institutions was most effective in mitigating NPO-for-TF risks, followed by collaboration with domestic LEAs, security agencies and prosecution authorities. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Collaboration with the financial institutions was most effective in mitigating the risks"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: To mitigate NPO-for-TF risks, it is recommended that jurisdictions mandate NPO registration and appoint a supervising authority to oversee the NPO sector. This is outside most FIUs' responsibility, but they can collaborate with the relevant authorities to raise awareness of NPO abuse risks. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is recommended that jurisdictions mandate NPO registration and appoint a supervising authority"</sub>

## SECTION IV – FIU'S ROLE IN IDENTIFICATION AND DETECTION

- **descriptive / finding or result**: STRs general trends: many participants reported overall STR increases in 2019-2021, with STRs on ML suspicions up 350% and STRs on TF suspicions up 82.6%. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "considerable increase in the number of reports concerning suspicions of TF activity (an increase of 82.6%)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: There was a jump in STRs between 2020 and 2021. Likely reasons given are greater reporting-entity awareness of ML/TF risk and indicators, and the COVID-19 pandemic, which changed financial behaviours; the public and criminals found alternative methods such as online financial services and virtual assets, and concern that terrorist groups use these methods may explain the increase. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Increased awareness among reporting entities of ML/TF risk and associated indicators is one likely reason"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: STRs linking NPOs and TF rose by a relative increase of about 310%, and the 2021 figure was more than double that for 2019. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a relative increase of about 310% identified"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Possible explanations for rising NPO-TF STRs are changes in the terrorism landscape and loss of some revenue sources, leading terrorist groups and financiers to alternative ways of raising or moving funds, including misuse of NPOs. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "terrorist groups and their financiers turning to alternative methods of raising/moving funds, including the misuse of NPOs"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Despite the consistent increase, absolute numbers of NPO-TF STRs are low. A possible reason is that more than half of participants' jurisdictions do not require NPOs to disclose donor identity; lack of awareness, inadequate training and resources can also leave suspicious transactions unnoticed and unreported. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "more than half of the participant's jurisdictions do not require NPOs to disclose information about the identity of donors"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Designation lists differ: for example Hezbollah in its entirety is listed by OFAC as a terrorist entity but is not on the UNSC list. Reporting entities often do not consider actions of undesignated entities suspicious and do not report; given high societal trust in NPOs, they may attribute suspicious activity to another entity rather than an NPO, or not report at all. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Hezbollah in its entirety is listed by OFAC as a terrorist entity"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: To potentially rectify these issues, red flags and risk indicators should be sent to NPOs. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "red flags and risk indicators should be sent to NPOs"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Terrorists commonly use cash, informal value transfer systems such as hawala and trade-based methods (TBTF), which the report says makes identification and reporting impossible. Detection is also harder because the sums needed to fund small cells, lone wolves and modern attacks are usually low, so terrorist actors can infiltrate or manipulate legitimate NPOs to transfer lawfully acquired funds without suspicion. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "terrorist actors can infiltrate or manipulate legitimate NPOs to transfer lawfully acquired funds"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Overall, detecting NPO abuse for TF is a great challenge because of low amounts of money, informal transfer methods, high public trust in NPOs and the terrorists' use of cash. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "detecting the abuse of NPOs for TF is a great challenge due to the use of the low amounts"</sub>

## STRS INVOLVING NPOS AND UNSC OR OTHER DESIGNATION LISTS

- **descriptive / context**: Obliged entities check persons and entities against designation lists (UNSCR lists, domestic lists and other jurisdictions' lists), which name countries, organizations and individuals subject to economic, trade or diplomatic sanctions for criminal or terrorist activity. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Obliged entities check persons and entities against a variety of designation lists"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Per participants, lists most commonly used by reporting entities are UNSCR 1267 and successor resolutions, then UNSCR 1373 and domestic lists; other lists in common use include the Dow Jones Watch List, UNSCR 1988, the EU sanction list, the OFAC List and more. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "UNSCR 1267 and its successor resolutions', followed by UNSCR 1373 and domestic designation lists"</sub>

## REPORTS DISSEMINATED BY FIUS CONCERNING NPOS

- **descriptive / finding or result**: In 2019-2021 participants reported an overall annual increase of about 29% in total disseminated reports on ML and TF (domestic and international combined); the relative increase in TF reports was only 12% to 14% each year. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "overall annual increase of about 29% in the total number of disseminated reports"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In 2019-2020, disseminated reports involving NPOs rose 310% in general and 393% for TF. In 2021 the increase continued at lower volumes: 28% for NPOs in general and 69% for NPOs involving TF. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "with reference to TF (an increase of 393%)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: NPO disseminations as a share of total TF reports rose from 8% in 2019 to 28% in 2020 and 32% in 2021, in line with increased STRs on NPOs. Figure 6 (jurisdictional breakdown of NPO-related TF disseminations) is referenced but has no legible data in the text. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "from 8% in 2019 to 28% in 2020"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Increased domestic disseminations might reflect general growth in NPO-related STRs and authorities focusing on domestic NPOs to disrupt TF at source; a decline in terrorist attack planning in some countries may have shifted focus to domestic TF supporting extremist ideology, propaganda and recruitment. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "authorities focusing on investigating domestic NPOs to disrupt TF at its source"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Another reason for more domestic disseminations is terrorist organizations' shift in fundraising: groups such as Al Qaeda and ISIL adopted decentralized structures with more financially and operationally self-sufficient affiliates, enabling operation on a smaller budget. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "have adopted a decentralized organizational structure whereby their various affiliates have been empowered"</sub>

## CONCLUSION

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Most respondents assess the threat of NPO abuse for TF as low or medium. Risks include operating in or near conflict zones, terrorist presence in the jurisdiction or region, and religious or humanitarian activities providing cover for TF. The report says it is incorrect to read lack of terrorism activity as no TF threat, since non-profits also fundraise in jurisdictions less associated with terrorism. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is incorrect to perceive a lack of terrorism activity as meaning no TF threat"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Terror groups decentralized and diversified fundraising because law enforcement thwarted TF in the formal banking sector. Emerging technologies such as cryptocurrency, virtual assets, IVTS and FinTech make TF harder for FIUs to detect and investigate, particularly when the payment system is in a jurisdiction with weak AML or CTF measures; limited funds needed for modern attacks let actors exploit genuine NPOs without suspicion. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "particularly when the payment system is based in a jurisdiction with weak AML or CTF measures"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: While NPO registration and licensing are common, they are not without flaws. Limited regulation in low and high-risk jurisdictions raises abuse risk, and insufficient information on NPO financial activity limits risk evaluation and may indicate misunderstanding of how the sector functions. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "While NPO registration and licensing are common, they are not without flaws."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Investigating NPO exploitation for TF is also challenging because parties involved often have no criminal records or suggested prior TF involvement; even accessible financial data is often not considered suspicious without a direct link to terrorist acts, and showing charitable donations finance terrorism can be difficult. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the parties involved in TF in many cases do not have criminal records"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: That nearly all FIUs considered NPO abuse in their NRAs shows general awareness, but the lack of NPO financial-activity information is worrisome and may have led to incorrect threat assessment and incorrect or ineffective supervisory and preventative measures. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the lack of sufficient information available about NPO financial activity is worrisome"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The report says FIUs need to be proactive in their engagement with the NPO sector, regulators, reporting entities, domestic partners and LEAs, and foreign FIUs and LEAs, given their critical role in detecting TF activities. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIUs need to be proactive in their engagement with the NPO sector, regulators, reporting entities"</sub>

## RECOMMENDATIONS

- **descriptive / context**: The report's recommendations aim to raise awareness and mitigate NPO abuse for TF based on project findings; they are not binding, and jurisdictions with different models may warrant different approaches. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The recommendations are not binding, and jurisdictions may have different models in place"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Recommendation 1: jurisdictions are encouraged to create and promote publicly accessible online NPO databases covering status, activities, finances and trustees, to promote transparency and public trust and facilitate international information sharing about NPOs operating beyond their own jurisdiction. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Jurisdictions are encouraged to create and promote publicly accessible online databases for NPOs."</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Recommendation 3: increased engagement with prominent social media companies is advisable; platform moderators should be educated on NPO abuse dangers, methods, red flag indicators and whom to report to, which may improve identification of suspicious NPO fundraising and raise NPO-related TF STRs received by FIUs. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Increased engagement with prominent social media companies is advisable."</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Recommendation 4: it is advisable that NPOs be required to maintain registered bank accounts, create annual financial statements and use regulated channels or systems for financial transactions, bringing NPO accounts under financial system controls or regulations. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is advisable that NPOs be required to maintain registered bank accounts, create annual financial statements"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Recommendation 5: results from strategic analysis should lead to intelligence-based policies implemented to disrupt and prevent TF through NPOs. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The results from strategic analysis should lead to intelligence-based policies"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Recommendation 6: use of multiple designation lists by the relevant stockholders (as written) is advisable when examining activity and investigating legitimacy of NPOs, their trustees, donors and beneficiaries. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The use of multiple designation lists by the relevant stockholders is advisable"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Recommendation 2: FIUs, supervisors and relevant authorities are advised to educate reporting entities about the various designation lists in existence and the importance of checking as many of these lists as possible when investigating NPO trustees and activities. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIUs, supervisors and relevant authorities are advised to educate reporting entities about the various designation lists"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Recommendation 2 adds that training sessions with reporting bodies should be arranged to ensure the reporting entities know how to properly check designation lists. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "training sessions with reporting bodies should be arranged to ensure the reporting entities know how to properly check designation lists"</sub>

## Public Summary

- **descriptive / context**: This is a July 2019 public summary of the Egmont Information Exchange Working Group (IEWG) Report on FIU Tools and Practices for Investigating Laundering of the Proceeds of Corruption. The Report is aimed at Egmont member FIUs and their law enforcement counterparts with anti-corruption competences, and examines tools, methods, techniques and indicators used by FIUs to collect, analyze and disseminate financial intelligence on corruption-related cases. (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to examine tools, methods, techniques, and indicators associated with laundering the proceeds of corruption"</sub>

## OVERVIEW

- **descriptive / limitation**: The Report also aims to support private sector reporting entities in identifying and reporting laundering of corruption proceeds, which is a main goal of its set of indicators. It cautions that detection is specific and differs case by case, so it cannot be achieved by simply applying one or two indicators from the list. (p. 2)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "cannot be achieved by simply applying one or two indicators from the list"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The project was endorsed by the IEWG at the Egmont Group 25th Plenary Meeting in Sydney in September 2018. The project team comprised FIU Israel (IMPA), FIU Russian Federation (Rosfinmonitoring), FIU-The Netherlands and FIU Ukraine (SFMS), with support from other IEWG member FIUs. (p. 2)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIU Israel (IMPA), FIU Russian Federation (Rosfinmonitoring), FIU-The Netherlands, and FIU Ukraine (SFMS)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Report treats corruption as a pervasive crime affecting all countries: it hinders economic growth, erodes trust in governments, diverts government resources and affects delivery of services such as health care, infrastructure and education. Because corruption is motivated by profit and greed, financial intelligence plays a vital role in detecting and prosecuting it. (p. 2)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "financial intelligence plays a vital role in detecting and prosecuting corrupt activities"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Report states that FIUs are in many instances well equipped to detect, analyse and disseminate intelligence on laundering of corruption proceeds. It seeks to identify the most efficient tools and practices and the main challenges FIUs identified, with examples of best practices, areas for improvement and challenges. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identify the most efficient tools and practices that support these FIU activities"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Report assesses techniques that proved successful in building financial intelligence in cases leading to convictions and/or confiscation, and treats risk identification and assessment and strategic analysis development as key starting points for detecting potential corruption-related transactions. Its FIU Toolkit of effective tools, practices and powers is dedicated to FIUs and is not included in the Public Summary. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "As this toolkit is dedicated to FIUs, it will not be included in this Public Summary."</sub>

## METHODOLOGY AND DEFINITIONS

- **descriptive / definition**: Transparency International generally defines corruption as the abuse of entrusted power for private gain and classifies it as grand, petty and political depending on the amounts lost and the sector where it occurs. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "generally defines corruption as the abuse of entrusted power for private gain"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Transparency International types of corruption: Political Corruption is manipulation of policies, institutions and procedure rules in allocating resources and financing by political decision makers who abuse their position to sustain power, status and wealth; Grand Corruption is acts at a high level of government that distort policies or the central functioning of the state, enabling leaders to benefit at the public's expense; Petty Corruption is everyday abuse of entrusted power by low- and mid-level officials in dealings with ordinary citizens seeking basic goods or services in places such as hospitals, schools and police departments. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Petty Corruption – everyday abuse of entrusted power by low- and mid-level public officials"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The Report says there is no unified definition of corruption; it could generally be considered abuse of public resources or public power for personal gain, with many levels depending on the scale of the corruption and who perpetrates it. For the Report, the IEWG agreed the main focus would be corruption within the public sector rather than private sector corruption. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There is no unified definition of corruption."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The Report states that public sector corruption can be considered as abuse of power by government officials for illegitimate private gain. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Public sector corruption can be considered as abuse of power by government officials for illegitimate private gain."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The UNODC defines Grand Corruption as corruption at the highest levels of government requiring significant subversion of political, legal and economic systems, commonly found in countries with authoritarian or dictatorial governments or without adequate policing of corruption. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "corruption occurring at the highest levels of government in a way that requires significant subversion"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Per the same UNODC source, Grand Corruption occurs when a public official or other person deprives a social group or substantial part of a state's population of a fundamental right, or causes the state or its people a loss greater than 100 times the annual minimum subsistence income through bribery, embezzlement or other corruption offences. The term is therefore predominantly used for bribery, embezzlement or abuse of power cases. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a loss greater than 100 times the annual minimum subsistence income of its people"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The United Nations Convention against Corruption (UNCAC) is described as the only legally binding universal anticorruption instrument, covering five areas: preventive measures, criminalization and law enforcement, international cooperation, asset recovery, and technical assistance and information exchange. It also supplies legal definitions of Public Official, Foreign Public Official and Official of a Public International Organization. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is the only legally binding universal anticorruption instrument"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: UNCAC article 2(a) defines Public Official as (i) any person holding a legislative, executive, administrative or judicial office of a State Party, appointed or elected, permanent or temporary, paid or unpaid, irrespective of seniority; (ii) any other person performing a public function, including for a public agency or public enterprise, or providing a public service as defined in the State Party's domestic law; (iii) any other person defined as a public official in domestic law. For some specific measures in chapter II, the term may mean any person performing a public function or providing a public service as defined in domestic law. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "any person holding a legislative, executive, administrative or judicial office of a State Party"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: UNCAC article 2(b) defines Foreign Public Official as any person holding a legislative, executive, administrative or judicial office of a foreign country, appointed or elected, and any person exercising a public function for a foreign country, including for a public agency or public enterprise. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "any person holding a legislative, executive, administrative or judicial office of a foreign country"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: UNCAC article 2(c) defines Official of a Public International Organization as an international civil servant or any person authorized by such an organization to act on its behalf. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an international civil servant or any person who is authorized by such an organization"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: For the purposes of the Report, Public Official also covers the official's family members and close associates as defined in FATF Guidance on politically exposed persons (Recommendations 12 and 22), Chapter II p. 11: family members are individuals related to a PEP directly (consanguinity) or through marriage or similar civil partnership; close associates are individuals closely connected to a PEP socially or professionally. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Family members are individuals who are related to a [PEP] either directly (consanguinity)"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: For the Report, Accounts covers accounts held with any reporting entities, including all types of Financial Institutions and Virtual Asset Service Providers. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Accounts shall cover accounts held with any reporting entities"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: UNCAC article 14(b), as described, provides that parties shall give their administrative, regulatory, law enforcement and other anti-money-laundering authorities the ability to cooperate and exchange information nationally and internationally within domestic-law conditions, and to that end shall consider establishing a financial intelligence unit as a national centre for collecting, analysing and disseminating information on potential money laundering. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "shall consider the establishment of a financial intelligence unit to serve as a national center"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: UNCAC article 58, as described, provides that States Parties shall cooperate to prevent and combat transfer of proceeds of Convention offences and promote ways of recovering them, and to that end shall consider establishing an FIU responsible for receiving, analyzing and disseminating to competent authorities reports of suspicious financial transactions. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "shall consider establishing a financial intelligence unit to be responsible for receiving, analyzing and disseminating"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The project team ran two case collection phases using a template tailored to the purpose, gathering 59 sanitized cases from 41 FIUs in total, plus extra information on tools, best practices and challenges. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the project team collected 59 sanitized cases in total, from 41 FIUs"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Report considers UNCAC a major tool against corruption and a solid base for FIUs to obtain, analyze and exchange information on laundering of corruption proceeds. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Convention is considered by this Report as a major tool in the fight against corruption"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FATF Recommendations require measures on Politically Exposed Persons; the FATF General Glossary defines foreign and domestic PEPs and persons entrusted with a prominent function by an international organization, explicitly excluding middle ranking or more junior individuals. The project team used the UNCAC definitions instead because they better matched the project objectives. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These FATF definitions explicitly exclude middle ranking or more junior individuals"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The set of indicators at the end of the Report was supported by feedback from a number of Egmont Group FIUs and the Wolfsberg Group. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supported by feedback received from a number of Egmont Group FIUs, as well as the Wolfsberg Group"</sub>

## KEY FINDINGS OF THE SURVEY

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Report states that laundering corruption proceeds, and corruption itself, pose a significant threat to the national security of all countries and corrode public trust that authorities can deliver fair, transparent and effective services. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "pose a significant threat to the national security of all countries and jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Corruption and its laundering are most destructive when LEAs, FIUs, Anti-Corruption Agencies, Prosecutor's Offices and other authorities cannot show effectiveness, especially in asset recovery. Preventing corrupt officials from using ill-gotten assets is considered a main priority, so the FIU power to postpone suspicious transactions is considered an effective tool for disrupting laundering schemes. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FIU power to postpone suspicious transactions is considered an effective tool"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: One of the most important requirements for effective collection and disclosure of financial intelligence on laundering of corruption proceeds is FIU operational independence and autonomy. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "effective collection and disclosure of financial intelligence related to money laundering derived from corruption is FIU operational independence and autonomy"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: To identify corruption elements, FIU analysts predominantly rely on lists of PEPs, their family members or close associates. The public procurement sector is mostly known as vulnerable to bribery, abuse of power or embezzlement of public funds, so transactions on accounts of state-owned companies or related to them are subject to stricter monitoring. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "analysts predominantly rely on lists of PEPs, their family members or close associates"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The surveyed cases describe laundering of corruption proceeds with political and non-political involvement in the private and public sectors; the analysis focuses on public sector cases, most described as very large and highly influential for their countries. Footnote 6 notes the charts present statistics only for cases gathered from Egmont Group FIU members. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This analysis focuses on the cases related to the public sector"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Two main typologies of corruption emerged from the cases: corruption related to procurement fraud and corruption related to unexplained wealth or income. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "corruption related to procurement fraud and corruption related to unexplained wealth or income"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Unexplained wealth or income cases describe Public Officials using their position to gain funds or assets illegally, usually from bribe-taking or embezzlement committed directly or with help from family members or close associates. Procurement fraud cases describe officials using influence in favour of certain parties over public tenders, contracts or suppliers in exchange for cash, checks, loan payments, assets or other benefits. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "use their influence in favor of certain parties concerning public tenders, contracts, suppliers"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: One of the most widespread methods of laundering corruption proceeds is transferring them through several foreign jurisdictions, divided into origin, transit and destination locations. This effectively separates funds from the source and helps conceal beneficial ownership because of difficulties controlling foreign Public Officials. Origin countries are where illicit funds come from; transit countries are financial centers mostly used for layering; destination countries are used for investment at the final stage. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The transfer of funds can be divided along three location types: origin, transit and destination."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In most cases funds were eventually used by the Public Officials, family members and associates to buy assets or were transferred abroad. Public Officials' positions varied widely, and third parties such as family members, business partners, employers, nominees and companies (including offshore and shell companies) were used to disguise the real beneficial owners through complex ownership structures. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to disguise, via a complex ownership structure, the real beneficial owners of accounts and assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The survey shows financial analysis of wire transfers and other transactions is crucial for exposing suspicious activity and the real beneficial owners of accounts, assets, legal entities and real estate, and that FIUs' ability to exchange information informally and promptly with foreign counterparts allows them to obtain further information from overseas. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "financial analysis of wire transfers and other financial transactions is crucial"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Timely and accurate provision of information from transit countries to origin and destination countries is described as one of the most crucial parts of the fight against corruption; transit-country FIUs cannot discern details of the corruption crime themselves but in some cases are the only FIUs that can see funds of unknown nature in one jurisdiction turn into assets in another via several transactions. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "in some cases they are the only FIUs that can see how the funds of the unknown nature"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Destination countries can sometimes be the same as origin countries, but criminals often want to move money far from the illegal source. FIUs of destination countries are most dependent on external information but can identify assets with concealed beneficial ownership. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIUs of destination countries are most dependent on external information"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Proceeds in the surveyed crimes were mostly received as cash and in-kind assets. Instruments included wire and physical transfers of funds, checks, credit and debit cards, real estate, loans and trusts, and the funds were used to buy real estate, cars, luxury goods, shares and other assets in the official's jurisdiction and abroad. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The proceeds of the crimes described in the survey were mostly received in the form of cash and inkind assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Because FIUs have access to diverse databases and tools, the more extensive their involvement in the financial aspects of investigations, the better the results appear to be; FIU financial analysis can both initiate and support corruption investigations. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial analysis carried out by the FIUs can both initiate and support investigations of corruption"</sub>

## 1. Detection

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Several surveyed cases were triggered by information from reporting entities, which stresses the importance of financial institutions identifying and reporting suspicious activity and of FIU IT tools able to find significant information in their databases. Other cases were triggered by a whistle-blower, spontaneous dissemination, requests from national authorities or foreign FIUs, and in one international situation open source information about a foreign Public Official holding accounts and assets in another jurisdiction. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Several cases identified information from reporting entities as the trigger for initiating the investigation"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: Specialized IT alerting systems can let an FIU take a proactive role in discovering potential corruption cases by automatically searching for corruption-related keywords in STRs/SARs, and by actively monitoring existing intelligence, external databases and open-source information to detect corruption-related patterns identified through strategic analysis exercises. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "automatically searching for corruption-related keywords in STRs/SARs"</sub>

## 2. Investigation

- **descriptive / finding or result**: FIUs use various IT tools for analysis and investigation: off-the-shelf products adjusted to FIU needs (e.g. the i2 platform), tools developed for FIUs (e.g. goAML), and proprietary tools built by FIU IT departments, all used similarly to flag higher-risk Public Officials and detect suspicious activity related to corruption. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Some of such tools are off-the-shelf products, adjusted to the needs of FIUs and financial investigations"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: A spreadsheet of transaction data is the basic, flexible analysis tool, while graphical display by more sophisticated tools helps portray transaction flows and links between transactions and entities. Such tools can visualize on a single graph bank account data, funds flow, links between legal entities and their shareholders and directors, relatives' connections, real estate, property, financial assets, contracts and other networks. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "graphical display by more sophisticated IT tools was helpful in better portraying and schematizing the transactions flows"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Open source intelligence such as search engines, social networks and news media provided information on persons and entities involved, source of wealth, apparent destination of funds, country of origin or residence and other affected jurisdictions; examples cited were LEXIS NEXIS and World-Check. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Examples for such sources included LEXIS NEXIS and World-Check."</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Additional databases used to enhance the intelligence picture: PEP databases to identify domestic and foreign PEPs; criminal records databases; the Central Bank system to identify cross border transactions; commercial databases and TCSP reports to identify beneficial owners, directors, capital and registration addresses of legal entities; tax authorities' databases to identify links to assets, affiliated entities and individuals and occupational links to public funds and state contracts; population registries for details on officials and relatives; cross border control registries for travel information that may indicate hidden assets abroad; and vehicle registration databases to identify owners of luxury vehicles, a common use of laundered proceeds in the survey. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Vehicle registration databases were used to identify owners of luxury vehicles"</sub>

## 3. Domestic cooperation

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Most participating FIUs said complementary information was essential to get clarifications and reveal hidden funds and other persons involved. Requests for further disclosure usually went to private-sector reporting entities such as banks, credit institutions, insurance companies, notaries, accountants, auction houses, MVTS, credit card services and precious stones and metals dealers, showing high private-sector involvement in the national AML/CFT system. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "requests for further disclosures of information were sent to reporting entities within the private sector"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Collaboration with domestic and international counterparts was a key factor in successful investigations; information requests went through exchange platforms and face-to-face meetings were held at key stages. Cooperation and coordination with LEAs, particularly police and prosecution, up to indictment was emphasized in all cases, bringing quick results and helping focus the investigation, freeze and seize assets and obtain judicial orders. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The importance of cooperation and coordination with law enforcement agencies, and in particular the police services and the prosecution"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Some cases stress close FIU-LEA partnership, which aligns priorities and gives holistic understanding and awareness of each agency's roles and capabilities. Designated joint taskforces on corruption cases are mentioned as beneficial for trust, mutual understanding and efficient information exchange. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Designated joint taskforces targeted at corruption cases are mentioned as beneficial"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Survey responses name outreach to reporting entities, and an ongoing relationship with them, as key to improving the quality of the information received and to raising awareness of red flags and relevant typologies; keeping close relations with the private sector lets FIUs obtain valuable information, formally and informally, promptly and confidentially. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Outreach to reporting entities and an ongoing relationship with them is mentioned as key"</sub>

## 4. International cooperation

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The vast majority of analysed cases involved one or more foreign jurisdictions and the survey shows the indispensable contribution of information exchange with other FIUs, sometimes with multiple counterparts at once via the Egmont Secure Web; face-to-face meetings and phone calls enhanced cooperation when possible. Information provided was comprehensive and precise, and when limited or unavailable it helped narrow the investigation. Some investigations were triggered by spontaneous disseminations from other FIUs. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The vast majority of the cases that were analysed involved one or more foreign jurisdictions."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Information from other FIUs helped discover concealment methods, identify persons involved and identify beneficial owners of overseas legal entities, financial assets and real estate. The ability of FIUs to freeze accounts or transactions was shown to be important because it allows time for further analysis and for law enforcement to obtain court orders. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the importance of FIUs’ ability to freeze accounts or transactions was demonstrated"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The quality and timeliness of investigations depends on completeness and speed of information exchange between FIUs of the jurisdictions involved; supply can be delayed by clarification requests from the receiving FIU or by a counterpart FIU awaiting results of third-party queries. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "depends on the completeness and speed of information exchange between the FIUs of the jurisdictions involved"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The Report says it is important to ensure cooperation between the FIUs of all countries through which the funds were transferred. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is important to ensure the cooperation between FIUs of all countries through which the funds were transferred."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In the cases studied, one transit country was not an Egmont member and another had no FIU at all, which makes the investigation process much harder. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "one of the countries of transit was not an Egmont member and another did not have an FIU at all"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The Report states that, even where a scheme has been revealed and assets frozen, further cooperation of the competent authorities must be secured within the mutual legal assistance process; otherwise the assets could be unfrozen and the case will come to nothing. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "further cooperation of the competent authorities must be secured within the mutual legal assistance process"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Report says several problems can be mitigated by controlling the information exchange process on a regular basis and by training FIU, LEA and ACA staff. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Several problems can be mitigated by controlling the information exchange process on a regular basis"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: The Report says it is useful to monitor other FIUs' powers and to regularly update relevant methodological documents for the FIU's own analysts and third parties. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is useful to monitor other FIUs’ powers and regularly update relevant methodological documents"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The Report states that national counterparts must be duly informed of the FIU's information exchange capabilities and features. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "national counterparts must be duly informed of the FIUs information exchange capabilities and features"</sub>

## IDENTIFIED CHALLENGES AND CONCLUSIONS

- **descriptive / limitation**: Although the surveyed investigations mostly succeeded, FIUs faced challenges. In some countries investigating and prosecuting high-ranking politicians is difficult, which may illustrate the actual level of FIU autonomy and operational independence. Cash use in residual corruption cases created information gaps on cash flows, particularly between jurisdictions, with various forms of cash use letting perpetrators avoid customs declarations and the legitimate financial system. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is difficult to investigate and prosecute high-ranking politicians"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Information exchange between FIUs was mostly productive but also a challenge, since not all counterpart FIUs can access the specific information needed and time limits were not always met; collecting information abroad is also problematic when the relevant country has an FIU that is not an Egmont member or has no FIU. It appears not all corruption-related requests are answered, or answered fully. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "not all requests for information concerning corruption cases are answered or are not answered in their entirety"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: A technical challenge was gathering information received in different data formats into one system or database for analysis. The main difference between surveyed FIUs is availability of information types and databases, including the FIU database and designated PEP databases, so there is room for mutual exchange of ideas on creating and using relevant databases. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "gathering all of the obtained information, received in different data formats, into one system or database"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Cooperation between the FIU and other domestic authorities (such as national police and prosecutor's office) and public-private partnerships were key to success, and the Egmont Secure Web was of high importance; efficiency and effectiveness of information exchange can be improved by setting a specific timeline and focusing the request. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "can be improved by setting a specific timeline and focusing the request"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: One reported challenge was the involvement of multiple third parties, such as relatives and associates, which made tracing the funds difficult. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "involvement of multiple third parties, such as relatives and associates, which made tracing the funds difficult"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: A major challenge mentioned in the survey was ensuring confidentiality, especially when disseminating information to foreign FIU counterparts; in some cases bank secrecy law obstructed retrieval of SWIFT documents. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "bank secrecy law presented obstacles in the way of retrieval of SWIFT documents"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The Report says identifying the persons involved in laundering the proceeds of corruption is complicated because offshore and shell companies are used. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Identification of persons involved in laundering the proceeds of corruption is complicated since offshore and shell companies are used."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In most cases the corrupt activity was not overt and further domestic and foreign investigation was needed to establish which institutions were involved, the source of funds, financial flows, and assets (such as real estate, bank accounts and vehicles) and their beneficial owners. This was complicated because Public Officials usually create convoluted ownership structures using families, relatives and business partners as owners, frontmen and active executors. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Public Officials usually create convoluted ownership structures by positioning their families"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: The Report describes as one of the best practices applying a reversed burden of proof concerning corruption or money laundering, which allows assets to be recognized as criminally acquired and confiscated without a person's conviction, and calls such a mechanism crucial for timely reaction where access to information is limited by criminals using different jurisdictions. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "applying the reversed burden of proof concerning corruption or money laundering"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Where assets of doubtful nature, suspicious transactions fitting the described schemes or any red flag of laundering corruption proceeds are identified, the Report says it is highly important to inform all involved jurisdictions, enabling multilateral information exchange and informing the countries where the corruption occurred and those used in laundering. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is highly important to inform all involved jurisdictions"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: To identify multi-jurisdiction schemes at the very first step, when the subject attempts to transfer funds to the transit country, the Report recommends applying all appropriate measures immediately, for example suspending transactions and freezing funds, which helps prevent laundering and reduces the time to find and recover assets worldwide. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is recommended to apply all appropriate measures immediately"</sub>

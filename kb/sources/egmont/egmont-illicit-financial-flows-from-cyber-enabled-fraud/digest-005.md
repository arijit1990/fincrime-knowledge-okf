## 5. International co-operation and asset recovery

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 81: the jurisdiction where cyber-enabled fraud (CEF) occurs (generally where the victim is) tends to differ from where proceeds are laundered. This can create challenges for cross-border investigations and international co-operation to obtain information and evidence, dismantle CEF syndicates and recover proceeds. For example, a jurisdiction where proceeds were laundered may struggle to identify every victim tied to a ML account, as victims can be spread across multiple jurisdictions. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the jurisdiction where the CEF occurs (i.e., where the victim is generally located) tends to be different"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 82: the de-centralised nature of CEF adds complexity. Jurisdictions' international co-operation priorities may be mismatched, e.g. Jurisdiction A's victims send money to B but B's victims are in C, so A may prioritise B while B prioritises C. Involving multiple public and private partners abroad also makes identifying and tracing illegal funds challenging. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There may be mismatches in jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: CEF syndicates use various financial services and asset classes, and transactions can be made almost instantaneously across borders between different providers and sectors, making funds transfers difficult to trace and attribute. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Transactions can be made almost instantaneously across borders between different providers and sectors"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Relevant digital forensic evidence is likely distributed across jurisdictions, making it hard to piece together how syndicates operate and launder proceeds; this is complicated by its volatile characteristics, as it can be easily dissipated if not quickly preserved. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Relevant digital forensic evidence is also likely to be distributed across different jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 83: formal co-operation, including mutual legal assistance, typically takes a long time, so given the rapid nature of digital crime and ML relying on it may be significantly less effective. To stay nimble, competent authorities increasingly rely on informal co-operation, sharing information directly with foreign counterparts at law enforcement or FIU level through channels including the Egmont Secure Web, INTERPOL's I-24/7 and networks such as the Camden Asset Recovery Inter-Agency Network (CARIN) and regional ARINs. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Formal co-operation, including mutual legal assistance, typically takes a long time"</sub>

## Box 39. Interception of CEF proceeds through informal multi-lateral networks

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 39 (France): French investigative authorities actively use informal networks, including the European Asset Recovery Offices (AROs) sub-network of CARIN. The French ARO works with members of these networks to exchange information rapidly between LEA and FIU counterparts specialised in tracing, seizing and confiscating assets, with emergency requests answered within 8 hours. This lets funds be quickly preserved in the first destination account and all subsequent layered accounts. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "especially in cases of emergencies where requests are answered within 8 hours"</sub>

## 5.1. Asset recovery

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 85 names the rapid pace of laundering as a key challenge in recovering CEF assets. Several bodies run multi-lateral 'rapid response' programmes to trace and recover CEF proceeds, including INTERPOL's Global Rapid Intervention of Payments (I-GRIP), the Egmont Group's BEC Project and the U.S. Financial Fraud Kill Chain. Their experience generally shows that intervening within 24 to 72 hours of a fraudulent transaction is most effective, which limits the risk of funds spreading into many later layers that sharply narrow the scope of the ML investigation and make recovery easier. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "intervention is most effective within 24 to 72 hours of a fraudulent transaction"</sub>

## Box 39. Interception of CEF proceeds through informal multi-lateral networks

- **descriptive / case example**: In 2022 in Box 39, the French ARO contacted the Slovak ARO about a fraudulent bank transfer of EUR 1 875 000 against a French victim company and asked that funds be frozen in the Slovak beneficiary account. Exchanges between the two AROs led to the freeze and gave Slovak authorities the information to draw up and execute a judicial freeze request. In the end GBP 1 874,907 (as written in the source) was frozen and returned to the victim company. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the French ARO contacted the Slovak ARO involving a fraudulent bank transfer"</sub>

## 5. International co-operation and asset recovery

- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 84: to maximise effectiveness in investigating CEF-related ML and recovering proceeds, co-operation should have a multi-lateral focus rather than a bi-lateral one. The section covers challenges and good practices through two outcomes: asset recovery, and enforcement and prosecution. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "co-operation should have a multi-lateral focus rather than bi-lateral"</sub>

## Box 40. Financial Fraud Kill Chain and Recovery Asset Team

- **descriptive / method**: Box 40: the Financial Fraud Kill Chain (FFKC) was created by the FBI and FinCEN (U.S. FIU) in 2016 in response to business e-mail compromise schemes, to aid recovery of international wire transfers sent under fraud schemes by leveraging FinCEN's relationships with the Egmont Group. It can only be used if: (1) the wire is USD 50 000 or above; (2) the wire is international; (3) a SWIFT recall notice has been initiated; and (4) the wire occurred within the last 72 hours. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "(1) the wire transfer is USD 50 000 or above; (2) the wire transfer is international"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Box 40: in 2018 the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) established the Recovery Asset Team (RAT) to address vulnerabilities in domestic wire transfers, streamlining communication with financial institutions and assisting FBI field offices in freezing funds for domestic transfers made under fraudulent pretences. The RAT has frozen 73% of funds reported as fraudulent to IC3 (USD 433.3 million of USD 590.62 million) to date, and in some instances can quickly identify second-hop accounts and freeze funds, making full recovery possible. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "freezing 73% of funds reported to be fraudulent to the IC3 (USD 433.3 million of USD 590.62 million) to-date"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 86: these multi-lateral programmes primarily aim to collect the minimum information required for law enforcement action and pass it into the 'correct hands'. All nodes of the networks also agree on governance rules and procedures to ensure effective cross-border response. Such networks are usually global, but regional initiatives may also help by building on established regional collaboration. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "collect the minimum level of information required for law enforcement action and pass that information"</sub>

## Box 41. Multi-jurisdictional Anti-Fraud Project

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 41 (Malaysia): the regional Multijurisdictional Anti-Fraud Project of the Financial Intelligence Consultative Group (FICG, a regional body of FIUs from Southeast Asia, New Zealand and Australia) is co-led by the FIUs of Malaysia, Indonesia and Singapore and aims to detect, trace and recover funds for victims. A response mechanism for cross-border transactions between FICG members lets them share financial intelligence quickly and easily, supporting swift action against fraud and recovery of stolen money. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "co-led by the FIUs of Malaysia, Indonesia and Singapore"</sub>

## _Cross-border information collection and exchange: “collect minimum level of information”_

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 87 adds that, unlike traditional fraud between acquaintances (where fraud is hard to distinguish from civil debtor-creditor disputes), prima facie criminality is relatively easier to establish in CEF, where fraud is typically between non-acquaintances. This reduces the need for a lengthy request for assistance to articulate the criminal nexus, as is typically required for crimes not universally recognised as predicate offences. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is relatively easier to establish prima facie criminality in CEF cases"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 88: as good practice, rapid response programmes use templates to speed collection and exchange of information. Templates allow quick collection of the minimum information needed to establish criminality, focus ground response units on vital evidence at the initial stages of a criminal complaint, mitigate quality problems in exchanged information and improve cross-border law enforcement response. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the various rapid response programmes use templates to accelerate the collection and exchange of information"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 89: besides a summary of the CEF crime, templates generally seek basic data needed to advance funds tracing. Standardising requests allows requested jurisdictions to process incoming requests quickly, accelerating law enforcement's ability to intercept illicit funds that have entered their jurisdiction. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The standardisation of requests allows requested jurisdictions to quickly process any incoming requests"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Paragraph 90: template data fields may include originator and beneficiary account information and transaction information (date, time, amounts transferred). Templates could also include the next destination of funds if already transferred out of the beneficiary account, and it may be useful to minimise restrictions on jurisdictions disseminating exchanged information domestically to relevant competent authorities on receipt. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "templates could also include information on the next destination of funds"</sub>

## Cross-border information collection and exchange: “collect minimum level of information”

- **mandatory / requirement**: Paragraph 87 states that where a country treats CEF as a serious crime under its domestic law, FATF Recommendation 3 requires it to be criminalised as a predicate offence for ML. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is required to be criminalised as a predicate offence for ML under FATF Recommendation 3"</sub>

## Box 42. INTERPOL I-GRIP

- **descriptive / method**: Box 42: INTERPOL's Global Rapid Intervention of Payments (I-GRIP) is a global stop-payment mechanism letting member countries submit and handle requests to follow, intercept or provisionally freeze CEF proceeds. It was piloted in 2022 as the Anti-Money Laundering Rapid Response Protocol (ARRP) and officially launched in November 2022 after many stop-payment successes in the pilot. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "originally piloted as the Anti-Money Laundering Rapid Response Protocol (ARRP) in 2022"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Box 42: I-GRIP facilitates rapid communication between INTERPOL National Central Bureaus (NCBs) to prevent suspected illicit assets moving between member countries. Requests should include enough detail for the receiving NCB to act, such as date of transaction, currency and amount, and account numbers and financial institution names of the beneficiary and remitter accounts. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Requests submitted via I-GRIP should include sufficient details upon which the receiving NCB can act"</sub>

## _Cross-border information collection and exchange: “collect minimum level of information”_

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 91: standardised template data fields let international organisations with centralised capabilities analyse data easily and maximise investigative and asset recovery efforts. INTERPOL uses information exchanged through its channels to build an internal database, the Financial Criminal Analytical File (FINCAF), to analyse transnational financial crime intelligence and identify links between cross-border cases, threats, crime trends and criminal networks. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Financial Criminal Analytical File (FINCAF)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 92: to speed asset recovery, some jurisdictions let foreign victims file a CEF complaint directly with their LEAs, including via an online reporting platform capturing the data fields needed for enforcement action. This removes a layer of communication and lets competent authorities swiftly act against suspicious transactions to beneficiary accounts in their jurisdictions. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "some jurisdictions have enabled foreign victims to file a CEF complaint directly with their LEAs"</sub>

## _Necessary powers to act: “the Correct Hands”_

- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 93: as speed is of the essence, collected information should ideally be handed directly to authorities already equipped with the power and expertise for asset tracing and recovery. This allows provisional measures to be taken immediately on receipt of a request to prevent further laundering or dissipation, giving law enforcement time to continue investigations, gather evidence and follow up with formal MLA requests. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "any information collected should ideally be directly handed to authorities that are already equipped"</sub>

## Box 43. Request of postponement from obliged entity

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 43 (Italy): FIU Italy received a postponement request from an obliged entity about four suspicious wire transfers totalling EUR 490 000, ordered by an Italian clothing wholesale company in favour of firms in a far eastern Asian country. The entity found them suspicious because the funds came from incoming transfers being recalled by the ordering bank due to a 'CEO fraud' on a western European victim company, and FIU Italy had also received a spontaneous exchange from that country's FIU. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIU Italy received a request of postponement from an obliged entity about four suspicious wire transfers"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In Box 43 the Italian company was also reported to the FIU for possible connection to VAT fraud schemes involving the same Asian country through a separate eastern European country, indicating links between CEF and other organised crime. The transactions were successfully postponed, allowing foreign authorities to issue a foreign seizure order to recover the funds in Italy. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This allowed for the foreign authorities to issue a foreign order of seizure to recover the funds in Italy"</sub>

## _Necessary powers to act: “the Correct Hands”_

- **descriptive / recommendation**: Paragraph 94: direct interfacing may face challenges from differences in legislative and enforcement frameworks across jurisdictions. Good practices include establishing domestic co-ordination mechanisms to route requests to the correct authorities, leveraging public-private collaboration channels, and FIs' ability to voluntarily take provisional measures once informed of suspicious transactions by competent authorities. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "establishing domestic co-ordination mechanisms to facilitate requests transmission to the correct authorities"</sub>

## _Governance & Rules: “the Collective Agreement”_

- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 95: governance and rules for multi-lateral frameworks give assurance and commitment to mutually recognise criminal activity and act quickly on receipt of information, helping overcome mismatched priorities because conditions to accede and assist are agreed in advance. As a good practice these rules and criteria should be clear and easily understood. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these rules and criteria should be clear and easily understood"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 96: these principles apply to informal and formal mechanisms. As an example, Regulation (EU) 2018/1805 of the European Parliament and of the Council allows mutual recognition of foreign freezing and confiscation orders, a direct enforcement mechanism that allows swift cross-border intervention. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Regulation (EU) 2018/1805 of the European Parliament and of the Council, allows the mutual recognition"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 97: accelerated information sharing should not be at the expense of data protection and confidentiality. Multi-lateral frameworks usually use existing secure channels such as those provided by INTERPOL, Europol and the Egmont Group, which also lets them expand easily since bilateral channels need not be developed. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Accelerated information sharing should not be at the expense of data protection and confidentiality"</sub>

## Box 44. The Egmont BEC Project Team

- **descriptive / method**: Box 44: 11 FIUs launched the 'Egmont BEC Project Team' to analyse BEC trends, indicators and methodologies and share findings with FIUs. Common BEC typologies and cases show a prompt reaction to stop and follow wire transfers is the most effective response. The Project Team establishes protocols between law enforcement and FIUs, and between international FIUs, to follow and freeze BEC proceeds. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "serious threat posed by BEC to financial institutions and their customers, 11 FIUs launched"</sub>
- **expected / method**: Box 44 process: on receipt of an STR on suspected cross-border BEC flows, the originator FIU develops a 'rapid response' request to the destination FIU; the request should contain agreed basic data and information needed for enforcement action; and the destination FIU is requested to take immediate action where possible to suspend and recover proceeds, ideally within 72 hours after the crime. Requests are exchanged over the Egmont Group's secured platform. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The request should contain agreed upon basic data and information needed to be exchanged for enforcement action"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 1 to Box 44 lists Project Team members as AUSTRAC (Australia), BFIU (Bangladesh), CTIF-CFI (Belgium), TRACFIN (France), GHFIU (Ghana), HFIU (Hungary), IMPA (Israel), SIC (Lebanon), FIU Luxembourg, UPWBNM (Malaysia), FinCEN (USA) and Europol. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AUSTRAC (Australia), BFIU (Bangladesh), CTIF-CFI (Belgium), TRACFIN (France), GHFIU (Ghana)"</sub>

## 5.2. Enforcement and prosecution

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 98: beyond asset recovery, the transnational nature of CEF creates difficulties throughout enforcement, from intelligence gathering and investigation to evidence collection for prosecution. Technology has increased transaction speed and enabled fragmented cross-border operations, increasing the time and effort law enforcement needs to trace and identify them. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the transnational nature of CEF has also resulted in difficulties throughout the enforcement process"</sub>

## Digital evidence collection

- **descriptive / recommendation**: Among the good practices listed in the digital evidence section is working directly with foreign service providers to obtain forensic evidence such as subscriber information without using the MLA process; one jurisdiction considers direct voluntary co-operation from a foreign service provider the most effective way to gather relevant digital evidence. Footnote 22 refers readers to the Council of Europe's July 2020 report on the benefits and impact of the Budapest Convention for more on voluntary co-operation with foreign service providers. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Direct co-operation with foreign service providers to obtain the necessary forensic evidence"</sub>

## Box 45. The Budapest Convention

- **descriptive / method**: Box 45 says the Budapest Convention provides procedural powers covering: expedited preservation of stored data; expedited preservation and partial disclosure of traffic data; production orders; search and seizure of computer data; real-time collection of traffic data; and interception of content data. It adds that the Convention also offers a fast and effective regime of international cooperation. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "expedited preservation of stored data, expedited preservation and partial disclosure of traffic data"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Box 45 says the Second Additional Protocol to the Cybercrime Convention, on enhanced co-operation and disclosure of electronic evidence, gives a legal basis for disclosing domain name registration information and for direct co-operation with service providers on subscriber information; it also provides effective means to obtain subscriber information and traffic data, immediate co-operation in emergencies, mutual assistance tools and personal data protection safeguards. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "provides a legal basis for disclosure of domain name registration information"</sub>

## _Digital evidence collection_

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 99: while not exclusively related to ML, digital forensic evidence can give critical clues for ML investigations, but the wide availability and ease of use of identity-concealment services such as VPNs complicates locating the ultimate CEF perpetrators. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "digital forensic evidence can provide critical clues to direct law enforcement to further their ML investigations"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 100: there is currently no single global regime governing the duration of digital data retention, including by technical service providers. Several jurisdictions highlighted the significant risk of digital evidence dissipation, and delays in formal co-operation also challenge swift securing of digital evidence. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is currently no single global regime that governs the duration of digital data retention"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: Paragraph 101 good practices: leverage informal channels first to gather and secure intelligence, then use formal channels to obtain evidence and statements for judicial proceedings; and use conventions and tools such as the Budapest Convention on Cybercrime, which allows expeditious preservation of electronic data and spontaneous information transmission, and establishes a 24/7 network for immediate investigative assistance (technical advice, evidence collection, data preservation etc.). (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Leveraging informal channels to first gather and secure intelligence"</sub>

## _Joint Enforcement Action_

- **descriptive / definition**: Paragraph 102: cross-border Joint Investigation Teams (JITs) involve a legal agreement between competent authorities of two or more jurisdictions to carry out criminal investigations. They may facilitate information sharing and cross-border financial tracing, typically through frameworks and agreements such as Eurojust and the Europol-supported Joint Cybercrime Action Taskforce. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Cross-border Joint Investigation Teams (JITs) involve a legal agreement between competent authorities of two or more jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 103: JITs are an important co-ordination point for multi-lateral enforcement against CEF given its transnational, de-centralised operations. Because barriers to criminal operations are low, CEF syndicates can easily relocate and set up new digital centres remotely, so co-ordinated action is necessary to uproot sub-groups working across multiple jurisdictions simultaneously. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "CEF syndicates can easily relocate and set up new digital centres of operations remotely"</sub>

## Box 47. INTERPOL Operation HAECHI

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Box 47: since 2020 INTERPOL runs a yearly operation, HAECHI, targeting cyber-enabled financial crimes and associated ML. Under HAECHI III (2022), with 30 jurisdictions, almost 1 000 suspects were arrested and 2 800 bank and virtual-asset accounts linked to illicit proceeds of USD 130 million were blocked. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "participated by 30 jurisdictions, almost 1 000 suspects were arrested and 2 800 bank and virtual-asset accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Box 47: Operation HAECHI served as platform for FINCAF, which gathers information from different sources, identifies links between ongoing investigations in member countries, and is structured to include data on any financial crime with a transnational dimension. INTERPOL uses it to strengthen tactical response to international organised crime such as CEF, giving insight into cross-border criminal activity, organisations, group structures, individual roles and key persons, modi operandi and fraudulent financial transactions. (p. 61)[^original]

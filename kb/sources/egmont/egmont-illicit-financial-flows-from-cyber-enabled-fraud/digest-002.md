## 2. Risk environment: Cyber-enabled Fraud

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The report notes a rising link between CEF and human trafficking: victims lured by fake job ads to online call centres are forced to commit CEF on an industrial scale, which widens the geographic diversity of targeted victims (trafficked people exploited for language and cultural insight) and can raise sophistication by trafficking skilled professionals such as IT workers or 'digital sales executives'. Call centres sometimes operate in victims' time zones and use rental properties for temporary operations, letting them re-locate and change IP addresses quickly to avoid detection. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "victims are lured through fake job ads to online call centres and forced to commit CEF on an industrial scale"</sub>

## Box 5. Operational Storm Makers

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 5 (source INTERPOL): Operation Storm Makers targeted organised crime groups facilitating cross-border movement of Asian men, women and children for exploitation, triggering 121 arrests across 25 countries and 193 new investigations. Police in Malaysia and Cambodia worked on a case of 15 men and one woman lured to Cambodia with promise of a lucrative call centre salary, then locked up and forced to work 14-hour days as scammers. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The operation triggered 121 arrests across 25 countries, prompting 193 new investigations."</sub>

## 2. Risk environment: Cyber-enabled Fraud

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Most jurisdictions have not seen substantial evidence of terrorist financing linked to CEF, but some observations exist; STRs from one jurisdiction suggest CEF proceeds were in some cases transferred to specific conflict areas or jurisdictions known for terror-related activities. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Most jurisdictions have not seen substantial evidence of terrorist financing activities linked to CEF."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report links CEF to proliferation financing: cybercrime is reported as a major source of illicit income for the DPRK, including sale of harvested personal information and provision of hacking and phishing tools and services that other criminals may use to commit CEF. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "cybercrime reported as a major source of illicit income generation for the Democratic People’s Republic of Korea"</sub>

## Box 6. Use of DRPK phishing tools for CEF to fund weapons programmes

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 6 (UN Panel of Experts, South Korea): DPRK IT workers linked to the Munitions Industry Department have earned foreign currency by selling voice phishing hacking applications and operating overseas servers and IP addresses. In July 2020 four ROK nationals were arrested in China and extradited to the ROK; one testified that criminal groups bought ROK nationals' personal information and voice phishing applications from a DPRK IT worker, fooled victims into downloading tools to steal information, then posed as FI employees to trick victims into sending money. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "criminal groups had purchased personal information of ROK nationals as well as voice phishing hacking applications"</sub>

## 2.3. ML techniques and typologies

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Because criminals need to launder CEF proceeds quickly and efficiently, jurisdictions have seen professional ML groups and third-party professional enablers including lawyers, accountants, tax advisors, company secretaries and bankers. Professional ML groups may be part of the CEF syndicate or a separate de-centralised organisation providing ML services under a 'crime-as-a-service' model (professional ML networks). (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "third-party professional enablers including lawyers, accountants, tax advisors, company secretaries and bankers"</sub>

## Box 7. QQAAZZ Network

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 7 (Portugal, Europol): QQAAZZ advertised a 'global, complicit bank drops service' on Russian-speaking cybercriminal forums, opened and maintained hundreds of shell company and personal bank accounts worldwide to receive CEF cybercriminals' money, moved funds to other QQAAZZ-controlled accounts and sometimes converted them to cryptocurrency via 'tumbling' services, and after taking a fee of up to 50 percent returned the balance to clients. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "After taking a fee of up to 50 percent, QQAAZZ returned the balance of the stolen funds"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Box 7 continued: in November 2020 an international operation involving 16 countries led to 20 arrests of suspected QQAAZZ members, who attempted to launder tens of millions of euros; about 40 house searches took place in Latvia, Bulgaria, the UK, Spain and Italy, and criminal proceedings were started by the US, Portugal, the UK and Spain. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "resulted in the arrest of 20 individuals suspected of belonging to the QQAAZZ criminal network"</sub>

## 2.3. ML techniques and typologies

- **descriptive / finding or result**: CEF proceeds are typically laundered rapidly through a network of accounts; case studies show networks can be complex, spanning borders and institutions, though this varies with the group's sophistication. These networks typically involve individuals as well as legal entities. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Typically, CEF-proceeds are rapidly laundered through a network of accounts."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Individual money mules are often recruited through job offers and advertisements and online social media interactions; recruiters are known as mule 'herders'. Mules may be knowingly complicit, unwitting (deceived) or negligent, and may be offered incentives or fees. It is challenging to identify the herder or the origin of funds, and some jurisdictions noted recruitment of foreign nationals with no apparent link to the jurisdiction who are directed to set up mule accounts by physical travel or virtual account opening. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Money mule recruiters are also known as mule ‘herders.’"</sub>

## Box 8. Mule recruitment: Job Offer

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 8 (Philippines): a sari-sari store owner, Ms. RS, was recruited by Mr. O, a Nigerian national arrested in 2019 for allegedly running a multimillion online romance scam (losses over PHP 8 million, about EUR 129 000), into what she thought was a legitimate job offer with a portion promised per bank transaction. She handled 83 cash-based transactions (cash deposits, ATM and over-the-counter withdrawals) totalling PHP 3.6 million (about EUR 58 000) over six months, and Mr. O was arrested via an entrapment operation with her collaboration. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Ms. RS handled 83 transactions amounting to PHP 3.6 million"</sub>

## 2.3. ML techniques and typologies

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Shell companies are under CEF criminals' control, typically via strawmen or nominee directors; recruited mules may be told to act as strawmen and open corporate accounts to further obscure ownership. Some shell companies used virtual business addresses (real physical addresses offered by service providers to receive post and packages), and in online trading fraud criminals may use shell companies to open virtual point-of-sale accounts with merchant services companies to process victim payments. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Shell companies are under the control of CEF criminals, typically through strawmen or nominee directors."</sub>

## Box 9. Shell companies in online trading platform fraud

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 9 (Turkiye): STRs concerned an online trading platform fraud in which victims were approached by phone or social media for foreign exchange investments; a network of 209 companies laundered proceeds among themselves, had mutual accountants, mostly were established on the same date and were liquidated after a short period. Analysis showed three sub-groups and about TRY 10 billion (about EUR 336.7 million) fraudulently acquired and laundered. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a network of 209 companies, that had laundered proceeds amongst each other"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Box 9 sub-groups: 135 companies received TRY 9.6 billion (about EUR 323.2 million) via payment companies using virtual point-of-sale accounts, with TRY 100 million (EUR 3.4 million) withdrawn in cash and about TRY 6 billion (EUR 202 million) sent to a gold company; 59 companies received TRY 700 million (EUR 23.6 million), TRY 200 million (EUR 6.7 million) withdrawn in cash and the rest sent to VASPs after passing through accounts of third-party individual accomplices; 23 companies received TRY 875 million (EUR 29.5 million), TRY 220 million (EUR 7.4 million) cash withdrawn and the rest to VASPs via third-party accomplice accounts. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "One hundred thirty-five companies received TRY 9.6 billion"</sub>

## 2.3. ML techniques and typologies

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Legitimate companies, like individual mules, may be tricked into receiving CEF proceeds (e.g. as an investment or business opportunity) and asked to redirect the funds or refund them to a separate criminally controlled account; some willingly accepted such opportunities, particularly in economic distress. Their involvement gives an additional façade masking illicit activity. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Legitimate companies , similar to individual money mules, may also be tricked into receiving CEF-proceeds"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Differences for CEF-related mules: they are more likely to be recruited online (fake-company job adverts, spam emails), criminals exploit economic conditions and present 'easy money' jobs, CEF victims (e.g. of romance fraud) can be tricked into acting as mules, and in some instances human trafficking victims such as illegal migrants or workers are used to open accounts. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "CEF money mules are more likely to be recruited online"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: CEF mules are used for their financial institution accounts because fraudulent funds can be received and sent quickly by electronic payment rather than physical transfers or cash deposits, likely reflecting how victims are defrauded (by funds transfers); given digital banking's convenience, individuals targeted as CEF mules likely have some basic computer and technology proficiency. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Money mules linked to CEF are used for their accounts with financial institutions"</sub>

## Box 10. Romance fraud victim turned mule

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 10 (Slovakia): between April and May 2022 an elderly woman whose account was opened to receive her pension received two payments of higher amounts, one from a domestic account and one from a reported foreign victim. Investigation found she fell prey to a romance fraud via social media, gave the fraudster her internet banking credentials, and her account was used to launder other proceeds, part of which was exchanged to cryptocurrency through a foreign VASP. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The elderly woman provided her internet banking credentials to the fraudster"</sub>

## ML typologies and techniques

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The location of the CEF (victim) frequently differs from where laundering occurs, and mule networks may span multiple jurisdictions. Syndicates know FIs or authorities may already have flagged accounts, risking interception, so criminals may perform 'tests' with small-value transactions to change the funds' destination if the tests fail. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "criminals may perform “tests” by carrying out small value transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The type of first layer account receiving CEF proceeds typically depends on the CEF type to keep up a façade of legitimacy, and has changed over time; in BEC fraud, syndicates have shifted from individuals' accounts to corporate accounts to reduce detection risk. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "CEF syndicates have shifted from the use of accounts of individual persons to the use of accounts of corporates"</sub>

The table note says it distils general trends and may not apply to all cases. (p. 21)[^original]

## ML typologies and techniques

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Once a syndicate establishes an account, funds quickly enter the ML network and are rapidly layered through 'pass-through' transactions via domestic or foreign accounts controlled by mules/strawmen or by the syndicate. In the latter case mules surrender banking credentials, cards and tokens or give power of attorney so the syndicate controls accounts directly; professional enablers, e.g. when creating a power of attorney, lend transactions an air of legitimacy and help obfuscation. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "money mules would surrender banking credentials, cards, and tokens, or provide power of attorney"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: To evade detection syndicates use smurfing, hopping through accounts across different financial, remittance or payment service providers, and conversion to other assets (e-money, pre-paid cards, VAs), which may lengthen the time FIUs and law enforcement need to obtain financial data across borders, sectors and institutions. Some mules allow accounts to be used only for a limited period, which together with legitimate onboarding makes abnormal activity relatively hard for institutions to detect. Footnote 13 defines e-money as a digital representation of fiat currency used to electronically transfer value denominated in fiat currency. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The limited time period, together with legitimate onboarding procedures, make it relatively difficult"</sub>

## Box 11. Shell companies, bank accounts and virtual assets

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 11 (India): complaints concerned a mobile app posing as a crypto-mining investment platform that promised profit shares, lured victims to invest more, then stopped withdrawals and became inaccessible. The Indian FIU identified two entities operating the app on Google Play (subsequently deleted) and 34 linked entities, 28 of the 36 having foreign nationals as directors. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Out of the 36 entities, 28 entities had foreign nationals as directors."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Box 11 continued: India's Enforcement Directorate found a large-scale conspiracy with shell entities operating similar fraudulent apps and websites, entities not found at registered addresses and some also running illegal betting and loan apps; victim money moved to shell entity accounts and partly converted to virtual assets, and balances of INR 865 million (EUR 9.9 million) were found and frozen. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "INR 865 million (EUR 9.9 million) were found and frozen"</sub>

## ML typologies and techniques

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Cash technique: case studies feature cash withdrawal by mules and syndicates; cash movement outside FIs is hard to track, cash may be withdrawn at ATMs after laundering through the network (avoiding face-to-face contact with FIs), couriered across borders and deposited for further laundering, and proceeds may buy valuables that can be resold for cash, such as prepaid cards or precious metals. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Cash may be withdrawn via ATMs after being laundered across a ML network"</sub>

## Box 12. Withdrawal of cash and purchasing of gold and fuel cards

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 12 (China): in March 2023 an accountant of a Chinese company fell for a bank impersonation fraud after being added to a messaging app group for a supposed annual account inspection; criminals impersonating legal representatives and shareholders had him transfer RMB 7.8 million (about EUR 996 000) to two corporate accounts they controlled. Funds went into 26 secondary accounts and were withdrawn in cash at counters or ATMs, sent to third-party payment platforms, and used to buy gold and fuel cards. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "transferred into 26 secondary bank accounts, and then withdrawn in cash"</sub>

## ML typologies and techniques

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Trade/service-based ML: some jurisdictions observed TBML techniques such as fictitious or false invoicing and use of illicit proceeds to buy high value or readily marketable goods (e.g. vehicle parts, tickets, household items), including fraudulent wire transfers to legitimate businesses from luxury or electronics brands to small local businesses; goods are moved across borders and converted to cash. Commercial businesses outside the AML/CFT regime may lack awareness to perform identity verification or transaction monitoring, and overpriced or fictitious invoices for IT or consultancy services may also be used. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "criminals use trade-based money laundering (TBML) techniques such as fictitious or false invoicing"</sub>

## Box 13. CEF, mules and TBML

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 13 (Ireland): authorities arrested Person MS in a scheme laundering romance and BEC proceeds from Ireland to Nigeria through TBML (investigation ongoing); the scheme involves at least 60 names and 64 bank accounts. Proceeds go to Irish mules' accounts, are withdrawn in cash and moved to Irish accounts linked to or owned by MS, many opened under false identities. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the laundering scheme involves at least 60 names and 64 bank accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Box 13 continued: a Nigerian company (controlled by a Nigerian believed to be based in the US) orders goods such as alcohol, clothing, electronics and pharmaceuticals from legitimate European or Chinese companies, and MS's Irish accounts pay the invoices with goods shipped to the accomplice company in Nigeria. In one instance a German pharmaceutical company received over EUR 1.7 million traced to BEC and romance fraud proceeds across Europe and the US. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a German pharmaceutical company received funds of more than EUR 1.7 million"</sub>

## ML typologies and techniques

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Unlicensed or unregistered remitters and VASPs: criminal proceeds may be sent out of the jurisdiction via underground remitters or hawala services with little or no AML/CFT controls, and where VAs are involved syndicates may exploit VASPs based in jurisdictions with no or weak AML/CFT controls. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Criminal proceeds may be transferred out of jurisdiction using underground money remitters or hawala services"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: VA anonymity-enhancing techniques: unhosted wallets, peer-to-peer transactions, peel chains and high-risk exchanges are preferred, often combined, to quickly launder VA-related CEF proceeds out of a jurisdiction. Criminals increasingly use Bitcoin ATMs, giving falsified or altered identification details (identifiers, phone numbers, dates of birth), and use mixers or tumblers, anonymity-enhanced VAs (privacy coins, e.g. Monero) and DeFi services. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The use of unhosted wallets, peerto-peer transactions, peel chains, and high-risk exchanges are the preferred methods"</sub>

## Box 14. Complex ML across multiple sectors

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 14 (Japan): a foreign romance scam syndicate targeted about 70 Japanese victims, with USD 3 million sent to mule accounts in Japan. A Japanese man acting as local mule herder laundered funds to Ghana, where the syndicate was based, and was arrested via Ghana's cooperation through INTERPOL. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A foreign romance scam syndicate targeted approximately 70 Japanese victims."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Box 14 channels identified by STR analysis: wire transfers to the herder's Ghana bank account, then cash withdrawal in Ghana hand-delivered to the still-at-large syndicate leader, supported by fictitious invoices presented to his Japanese bank and falsely declared as a legitimate purchase of cacao beans; some funds exchanged into VAs through a VASP in Japan; and funds sent to Ghana through an underground bank linked to the Ghanaian community in Japan. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Japanese man presented fictitious invoices to his Japanese bank"</sub>

## Impact of digitalisation and new technologies on ML

- **descriptive / context**: Financial services are shifting profoundly to digital, accelerated by COVID-19, with reduced cash usage and more online activity producing new tools; the payments chain is increasingly dynamic and fragmented with more diverse service providers. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The financial payments chain is also growing increasingly dynamic and fragmented"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Technology can advantage criminal groups, who use it to improve ML techniques: transactions execute at near-instantaneous speed, driven partly by consumer expectations of frictionless experience, and with anonymising techniques such as VPNs this makes it hard to identify the ultimate criminals performing rapid-succession ML transactions. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial transactions are increasingly executed at near-instantaneous speeds"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Digitalisation has increased the ease and speed of creating accounts for ML and expands syndicates' cross-border reach; some jurisdictions noted more remote virtual processes in account opening and company creation, which remove the need to travel and which criminals can exploit. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "increase in remote virtual processes in two areas: opening of accounts and the creation of companies"</sub>

## Box 15. Scaling through digitalisation

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 15 (Singapore): FIU analysis found a network of 147 individuals and 276 bank accounts at eight banks; the individuals had relinquished their national digital identity to criminal syndicates, who used it to open accounts remotely and directly control the mule accounts to launder CEF proceeds. The FIU detected the network via commonalities in banking transactions, data points (foreign contact information, device ID) and contact details (mailing, email, telephone); the Anti-Scam Command (ASCom) investigations led to 6 arrests and prosecution of 3 individuals. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These individuals had relinquished their national digital identity"</sub>

## Impact of digitalisation and new technologies on ML

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Criminals can rapidly scale cross-border mule networks with digital tools; social media and VoIP applications are preferred recruitment media, and instant messaging has significantly reduced the time lag traditionally needed for mules to receive and follow syndicate instructions. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Social media and Voice over Internet Protocol (VoIP) applications have also been identified to be preferred mediums"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Criminals may steal identities through phishing, purchase, or deceiving someone into handing over identity, and may use falsified or synthetic identities (combining real and fake identity information) to fraudulently create accounts they directly set up and control, making ML harder to trace as account holders may not know of their involvement. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "synthetic identities, which involve the combination of real and fake identity information"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: One delegation flagged risks of deepfakes for account takeover fraud: using machine learning, a fraudster might fake someone's voice or video to impersonate them by phone or in biometric authentication systems, or combine deepfakes with social engineering to obtain credentials. The technology is relatively new so current risk may be somewhat limited, but it may pose a significant risk if the technology develops and becomes more widely available. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "One delegation flagged the risks of deepfakes being potentially used for account takeover fraud."</sub>

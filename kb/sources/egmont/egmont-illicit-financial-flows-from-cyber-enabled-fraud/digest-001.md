## Executive Summary

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report describes cyber-enabled fraud (CEF) as a growing transnational organised crime. CEF syndicates are often well structured into sub-groups with specialised expertise including money laundering, but may also be loosely organised and de-centralised across jurisdictions, which complicates investigation. They are also linked to other criminality, notably human trafficking and forced labour in CEF call centres, and to proliferation financing linked to illicit cyber activities from the DPRK. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "CEF criminal syndicates are often well structured into distinct sub-groups with specialised areas of criminal expertise"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Money laundering groups and professional enablers are involved in the CEF-ML process. The ML network of accounts typically involves money mules but can include shell companies or legitimate businesses, and features banks, payment and remittance providers and VASPs. Criminals combine ML techniques such as cash, trade-based money laundering (TBML) and unlicensed services to conceal the financial trail. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The ML network of accounts typically involves money mules but can also include shell companies"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Digitalisation has let CEF criminals increase the scale, scope and speed of their activity, using tools to deceive victims or prey on their psychological state. Remote online account opening lets criminals set up foreign accounts and launder abroad at near-instantaneous speeds; criminals use social media and messaging platforms to recruit money mules across borders at scale and quickly exploit new digital financial institutions and products and non-traditional sectors such as e-commerce and social media and streaming platforms. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Criminals are taking advantage of social media and messaging platforms to recruit money mules across borders at scale"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: The executive summary says jurisdictions need to respond more effectively by: employing initiatives to increase victim reporting and enhance suspicious transaction reporting; effectively analysing voluminous information inflows to tackle CEF; and, given the cross-cutting nature of CEF, having strong domestic co-ordination mechanisms to holistically combat and prevent CEF and related ML. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "strong domestic co-ordination mechanisms are required to holistically combat and prevent CEF and related ML"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The location of CEF predicate offences tends to differ from where laundering occurs, and proceeds can be laundered quickly through networks of accounts often spanning multiple jurisdictions and institutions. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The location where CEF predicate offences occur tends to be different from where the ML process occurs"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The report states that, because CEF predicate offences usually occur in a different place from the ML process and proceeds can be quickly laundered through networks of accounts across jurisdictions and financial institutions, jurisdictions must work together multilaterally to intercept cross-border CEF proceeds effectively and expeditiously. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Jurisdictions must collaborate multi-laterally to effectively and expeditiously intercept CEF proceeds"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: To combat CEF more effectively, the report says jurisdictions should make use of and back existing and future multilateral mechanisms for rapid international co-operation and information exchange, naming INTERPOL's I-GRIP and the Egmont Group BEC Project as examples. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions should leverage and support existing (and any future) multi-lateral mechanisms"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report includes a list of risk indicators and useful anti-fraud requirements and controls for public and private sector entities to detect and prevent CEF and related ML. It adds that online fraud and scams have dominated the cyber-enabled crime landscape and, left unchecked, will grow in sophistication and threat as more organised crime groups engage and exploit new technologies such as generative artificial intelligence. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Online fraud and scams have dominated the cyber-enabled crime landscape."</sub>

## 1. Introduction

- **descriptive / context**: Under the Singaporean Presidency the FATF started an initiative on countering illicit financial flows from cyber-enabled fraud; the report is a joint project of the Egmont Group, FATF and INTERPOL, the first such joint project by the three organisations. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the first project that these three organisations have undertaken jointly"</sub>

## 1.1. Focus and scope

- **descriptive / definition**: The report focuses on illicit financing arising from fraud enabled through or conducted in the cyber environment that (i) involves transnational criminality such as transnational actors and funds flows and (ii) involves deceptive social engineering (manipulating victims to obtain confidential or personal information); activity covered is referred to collectively as cyber-enabled fraud (CEF). (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "involves deceptive social engineering techniques (i.e., manipulating victims to obtain access to confidential or personal information)"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: CEF types covered: Business Email Compromise (BEC) fraud, where victims receive email instructions purporting to come from clients or suppliers asking them to pay new payment accounts; and phishing fraud, where victims are deceived into revealing personal data, banking details or login credentials which criminals use to drain accounts, open new payment accounts or make fraudulent transactions. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Victims receive email instructions that purport to be from their clients or suppliers"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: CEF types covered (continued): social media and telecommunication impersonation fraud, where criminals contact victims by mobile or social media pretending to be government officials, relatives or friends and prey on emotions to induce payment, hand over control of payments accounts, or carry out activities such as a loan application or account opening to receive criminal proceeds; and online trading/trading platform fraud, where fake advertisements or advisors lure victims to non-existent or fraudulent trading or investment platforms for fiat and virtual assets. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "criminals pretending to be government officials, relatives or friends, and prey on the victims’ emotions"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: CEF types covered (continued): online romance fraud, where victims are duped into sending money after being convinced they are in a romantic relationship; and employment scams, where fake job offers on social media trick victims into paying scammers on excuses such as advance payment for purchasing commodities to boost a trading platform's sales or a guarantee fee to secure employment. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Victims are duped into sending money to criminals after being convinced that they are in a romantic relationship."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Illicit financing related to ransomware and other malware-enabled crimes is outside the scope of the report; readers are referred to the FATF report Countering Ransomware Financing (March 2023), which is relevant because VAs and VASPs are sometimes exploited to launder CEF proceeds. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Illicit financing related to ransomware and other malware-enabled crimes are not within the scope of this report"</sub>

## 1.2. Objectives and structure

- **descriptive / context**: The report aims to enhance competent authorities' risk understanding of the CEF threat, building on work by the FATF, Egmont Group, Europol and INTERPOL. Chapters 2 and 3 cover the operating risk environment, risks, techniques and trends including the impact of digitalisation; chapters 4 and 5 identify good practices and operational solutions, including international co-operation and asset recovery mechanisms. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This report aims to enhance competent authorities’ risk understanding of the threat posed by CEF."</sub>

## 1.3. Methodology

- **descriptive / method**: The project was co-led by experts from Singapore (for the FATF), FIU Hong Kong China (for the Egmont Group) and INTERPOL, with a project team including Azerbaijan, Brazil, Belgium, Canada, China, the Council of Europe, the European Commission, Europol, Germany, GIABA, India, Italy, Israel, Japan, Malaysia, Mexico, MONEYVAL, Pakistan, Portugal, Saudi Arabia, Togo, the United Kingdom and the United States. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Experts from Singapore (on behalf of the FATF), FIU Hong Kong China (on behalf of the Egmont Group) and INTERPOL"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Findings rest on a literature and open-source review (including Egmont Group and INTERPOL data); a request to the FATF Global Network (over 200 jurisdictions) and the Egmont Group (170 FIUs) on risks, enforcement frameworks and strategies and co-operation mechanisms, yielding inputs from more than 80 delegations; and discussions at the FATF Joint Experts Meeting (April 2023) and Private Sector Consultative Forum (May 2023), including targeted private sector engagement. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the project team received inputs from more than 80 delegations"</sub>

## 2.1. Rising money laundering (ML) threat

- **descriptive / finding or result**: CEF has increased significantly internationally; there is no complete estimate of global magnitude but many jurisdictions report consistent growth. Illicit CEF proceeds are often sent to foreign jurisdictions and may be further laundered through third-party jurisdictions' financial systems. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "While there is no complete estimate of the global magnitude and scale of CEF"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The INTERPOL Global Crime Trend Report 2022 lists online scams among cybercrime trends most frequently perceived as 'high' or 'very high' threats globally. Most contributing jurisdictions recognise ML risks from CEF in national risk assessments; highly cashless, digital regions are expectedly more vulnerable, although CEF's transnational nature lets criminals target victims regardless of borders. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "online scams are one of the cybercrime trends most frequently perceived as posing"</sub>

## Box 1. Increased ML threats: regional CEF trends

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Box 1 regional trends: in Africa, rapid digitalisation has opened opportunities for CEF, causing a sharp rise in online banking fraud including phishing, identity theft and virtual asset scams, and CEF is reportedly a major source of proceeds of crime in West Africa. In the Americas, CEF is an increasing or emerging risk; one jurisdiction reported CEF reports rising year-on-year and another reported virtual asset investment fraud up over 180 percent between 2021 and 2022. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "investment fraud in virtual assets increased over 180 percent between 2021 and 2022"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Box 1 regional trends: in Asia-Pacific, jurisdictions cite CEF as a high or significant ML risk; one found the majority of fraud reports contain some form of CEF with increased CEF-linked ML, another highlighted transnational actors defrauding victims through illegal investment apps, and COVID-19 accelerated digitalisation so CEF and associated ML have escalated and are expected to keep escalating. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Another jurisdiction highlighted the role of transnational actors in defrauding victims"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Box 1 regional trends: the Caribbean is highly susceptible to CEF with rising fraud over five years, and its growing VA sector (including VASPs and mixers) creates vulnerabilities to laundering back to organised crime. In Europe CEF is generally assessed as a ML risk, many jurisdictions note large increases, and VA use to launder CEF proceeds is commonly observed, particularly for VA online trading fraud such as fraudulent initial coin offerings. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The use of VA is commonly observed to launder CEF-proceeds"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Box 1 regional trends: in the Middle East and North Africa, digitalisation accelerated during the pandemic, online financial frauds including phishing, impersonation fraud and online scams are ranked as high threats, and the region is vulnerable to ML because GCC member countries in particular serve as transhipment hubs for global trade and financial activities. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "GCC member countries, in particular, serve as important transhipment hubs for global trade"</sub>

## 2.1. Rising money laundering (ML) threat

- **descriptive / context**: Digitalisation and new technologies are key drivers of CEF growth: more citizens, including vulnerable groups, are online and information and funds move swiftly across borders, which together have fundamentally altered the criminal landscape. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Digitalisation and the development of new technologies serve as key drivers underpinning the growth of CEF"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated the move from in-person financial activity to online account opening, payments and lending; telephone and email scams, bank, elder and healthcare fraud (e.g. relating to PPE) and fraudulent investment scams significantly increased online, and increased use of digital banking, payment platforms and remote transactions also affected the ML landscape. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "accelerated the transition from in-person financial activities to online account opening, payments and lending"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Widespread smartphone use, evolving tools and remote transactions have increased user vulnerability, and anonymity-enhancing technology such as VPNs and The Onion Router (TOR, open-source software allowing anonymous internet use) gives criminals a cloak of anonymity. Criminals increasingly adopt a 'Crime-as-a-Service' model (division of labour in which groups offer niche capabilities to others), lowering barriers to entry for CEF syndicates and spreading specialisation across sub-groups. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "which also significantly lowers the barriers to entry for CEF syndicates"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In many instances organised criminal groups have expanded or adapted their activities to incorporate CEF, using existing techniques to launder their other unlawfully obtained funds. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "organised criminal groups have expanded or adapted their activities to incorporate CEF"</sub>

## Box 2. Common criminal ML network used for CEF and other crimes

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 2 (source: UNODC 2022): a ML network runs online gambling and CEF operations from a company building in Country A's Special Economic Zone housing about ten companies, with purported legitimate businesses in border regions of Country B. Country B nationals lead the network and use Country B currency bank accounts to move money from the SEZ to Country C where major investors are based; US dollars from the SEZ are laundered through Country B money exchanges, converted to Country B currency, transported to Country C and then transferred to investors. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "U.S. dollars from the SEZ are laundered through money exchanges in Country B"</sub>

## 2.2. Criminal characteristics of CEF

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: CEF criminals may rely on one or more elements to deceive victims into a fraudulent transfer, combined differently across variants: information extraction (e.g. phishing); social deception or engineering preying on vulnerable emotions (e.g. pretending to be another person or entity to generate urgency, fear or trust, or offering false claims of easy money); and an online medium or platform used for communication or on which victims transact in online trading fraud. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "CEF criminals may rely on one or more of the following elements to successfully deceive victims"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: A victim may not fall for just one type of CEF: the goal is to induce a funds transfer, and criminals may transition to other CEF types if the initial deception fails, for example steering a phishing or social media impersonation victim to an investment fraud by leveraging the trust already built. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Criminals are creative and may engage or transition to other types of CEF"</sub>

## Box 3. Same victims, multiple offences

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 3 (Europol IOCTA 2023): pig butchering combines romance scam and investment fraud, with criminals building trust and convincing victims to invest savings in fraudulent cryptocurrency trading platforms over time, causing large losses. After the fraud is realised, criminals often contact victims posing as lawyers or law enforcement offering to retrieve funds for a fee. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Pig butchering is a combination of romance scam and investment fraud."</sub>

## Organised criminal structure

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Figure 1, sourced to FATF, gives an example of a hierarchical CEF criminal structure. The chart text is garbled; legible parts show a leadership layer of senior members who do not take an active part in operations, a directors layer in direct contact with leadership that discusses and plans CEF, laundering and ML operations and the percentages payable to each layer, a facilitators layer with roles labelled mule herders, hacker and catchers, and funds transfers running to money mules and CEF victims. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Figure 1. Example of CEF criminal structure"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: CEF and related ML are often executed by transnational organised criminal groups or syndicates, which often operate as hierarchical organisations but may be loosely organised with members joining and leaving. They may be organised in sub-groups around information extraction, social deception, or technical expertise such as platform creation or ML, and are often de-centralised and have never communicated in person (e.g. via encrypted channels), making investigation difficult. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these CEF syndicates are de-centralised and have never communicated in person"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: CEF syndicates are regularly composed of well-educated and technically competent professionals, and jurisdictions noted they may intentionally recruit people working in professional sectors, including FIs, as sources of data and information to execute CEF and facilitate ML. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "CEF syndicates are regularly composed of well-educated and technically competent professionals"</sub>

## Links to other criminality

- **descriptive / finding or result**: CEF syndicates may be linked to other crimes associated with or necessary for CEF, including cybercrime such as hacking to obtain personal information, development and sale of criminal software, and document forgery. Part of the proceeds can be self-laundered into new equipment and more advanced technological tools. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Part of the criminal proceeds can be self-laundered by CEF syndicates"</sub>

## Box 4. Operation Falcon

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 4 (source: Nigeria): three Nigerian suspects were arrested in Lagos in 2020 after a joint INTERPOL-Group-IB and Nigeria Police Force investigation; they were believed to belong to a wider group distributing malware, running phishing campaigns and extensive BEC scams, and allegedly developed phishing links, domains and mass mailing campaigns impersonating organisations to spread 26 malware programmes, spyware and remote access tools. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The suspects were alleged to have developed phishing links, domains, and mass mailing campaigns"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Box 4 continued: the malware infiltrated and monitored victims' systems before scams and siphoning funds; Group-IB believed the gang compromised government and private companies in more than 150 countries since 2017 and is divided into subgroups with individuals still at large. Parallel ML investigations found the suspects used foreign bank and VA accounts in the UK, US and Thailand to receive victim payments; they were charged with fraud and money laundering, a luxury vehicle was confiscated and accounts frozen and undergoing forfeiture. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Parallel ML investigations revealed that the suspects also used foreign bank and VA accounts"</sub>

## Box 29. Using technology to delineate investigative responsibility

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 29 (Hong Kong China): the Hong Kong Police Force established the e-Crime Processing and Analysis Hub (e-Hub) in September 2022; it uses an enhanced computer system for correlation analysis on common CEF case types and identifies case clusters. In 2022 deception cases rose 45.1% to 27 923 cases, almost 40% of all crimes, with nearly 80% CEF-related; correlated e-reported cases (e.g. same criminal group) are assigned to a single investigation team for consolidated investigation. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the number of deception cases increased by 45.1% to 27 923 cases"</sub>

## Dedicated anti-CEF and related ML units

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Several jurisdictions indicated that the large volume of CEF reports makes it necessary to delineate who is responsible for investigating. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "makes delineation of investigative responsibility a necessity"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: As good practice, jurisdictions that have several anti-fraud or cybercrime agencies with oversight of CEF cases have tried to name the competent authority or authorities that will handle them. Other jurisdictions legislated so that complex investigations covering many victims of one syndicate fall under a single competent authority overseeing the whole investigation. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions with various anti-fraud or cybercrime agencies with oversight over CEF cases have sought to identify the competent authority"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report says these initiatives stop different competent authorities duplicating effort, stop cases from 'falling through the cracks', and respond to the transnational nature of the crime. (p. 42)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These initiatives prevent duplication of efforts by different competent authorities"</sub>

## Box 29. Using technology to delineate investigative responsibility

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 29 continued: using clustering algorithms, e-Hub identifies patterns and similarities not immediately apparent in the data to understand scope and nature of cases, including common criminal digital tools and money mule accounts used and how CEF is planned, executed and concealed. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "By using clustering algorithms, e-HUB can identify patterns and similarities in the data"</sub>

## Dedicated anti-CEF and related ML units

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Many jurisdictions set up a specific unit or taskforce to investigate CEF and related ML, allocating extra resources for financial investigation, intelligence gathering, LEA training and private sector capacity building. Such centralised units consolidate anti-CEF expertise and improve ability to disrupt CEF operations, trace laundered funds and recover proceeds. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "many jurisdictions set up a specific unit or taskforce to investigate CEF and related ML"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Reported benefits: consolidating all CEF cases in a single enforcement unit enables better analysis, data analytics and network link analysis to identify syndicates, provides a single point of contact for private sector and foreign counterparts, and helps build longer-run strategic relationships; this improves interventions such as disrupting phone lines and removing suspicious online monikers and advertisements, and improves asset recovery outcomes. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "serve as a singular point of contact for private sector stakeholders and foreign counterparts"</sub>

## Box 30. National Scam Response Centre

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 30 (Malaysia): the National Scam Response Centre (NSRC) brings together the National Anti-Financial Crime Centre, Royal Malaysia Police (RMP), the Central Bank and other public and private entities as a hub for fraud information, using network analysis to identify mule and laundering networks. Private sector entities including financial institutions trace funds layer to layer and withhold mule accounts, and the RMP investigates and takes enforcement action such as freezing orders. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "leverages network analysis to identify mule and laundering networks"</sub>

## Enhancing access to financial information

- **permissive / recommendation**: Technology-enabled information retrieval: so that financial institutions can quickly supply information to law enforcement, it can be useful for the competent authorities of a jurisdiction to agree on the data fields relevant to their investigations, because differing requests each needing a tailored reply are time-consuming for the private sector. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it can be useful for competent authorities within a jurisdiction to agree on data fields"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: As a good practice, law enforcement in some jurisdictions created a standard template of pre-agreed data fields required from financial institutions, so requests can be bundled, sent to financial institutions in batches and made machine-readable; financial institutions may also answer lawful requests digitally, which makes data analysis more efficient. (p. 43)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "law enforcement in some jurisdictions have developed a standardised template comprising pre-agreed data fields"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Given the volume and speed of CEF, timely access to financial and banking information is crucial to accelerate investigation and tracing. Some jurisdictions use technology, often with the private sector, or rely on central registers or developed databases; these good practices usually rely on a centralised platform bringing together multiple stakeholders for faster information exchange. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "timely access to financial and banking information is crucial in accelerating the investigation and tracing of CEF proceeds"</sub>

## Box 31. Leveraging robotic process automation to speed up access to financial records held by financial institutions

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 31 (Singapore): Singapore uses robotic process automation (RPA) to get banking information far faster: orders are served electronically on banks via a standardised template, banks automate retrieval and return data electronically for immediate LEA analysis. Turnaround improved by up to 97%, and banks saved costs by eliminating manual workflows and gained data mining capability that can detect hidden ML networks. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The process has improved turnaround time by up to 97%"</sub>

## Enhancing access to financial information

- **descriptive / recommendation**: Facilitating asset tracing across FIs: pass-through transactions and account hopping across many FIs add to tracing time, as information must be gathered from each FI to peel through layers and find the origin and ultimate destination of funds, which is hard given transaction speed. Good practices include platforms enabling rapid tracing and information exchange across FIs to intercept illicit proceeds. (p. 44)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Good practices include developing platforms to facilitate rapid tracing and information exchange across different FIs"</sub>

## Box 32. Citizen Financial Cyber Fraud Reporting and Management System (CFCFRMS)

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 32 (India): the CFCFRMS, built by the Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre, is an online system for quick reporting of financial cyber frauds and stopping fraud proceeds flowing across the financial sector; all State and Union Territory LEAs and 243 Financial Entities (banks, wallets, payment aggregators, gateways, e-commerce platforms etc.) are onboarded. After a victim reports to the LEA, the beneficiary details are submitted as a ticket to the concerned Financial Entity, which checks whether the money is still in the account and holds it; if dissipated, the ticket escalates to the next entity until the money is intercepted, and withdrawal details are filled by FIs for LEA action. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Entity will check if the defrauded monies are still in the account and puts it on hold."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Box 32 result: since inception in April 2021 the CFCFRMS has intercepted more than INR 6.02 billion (about EUR 66.1 million). (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "able to intercept more than INR 6.02 billion (about EUR 66.1 million)"</sub>

## Enhancing access to financial information

- **descriptive / recommendation**: Leveraging central registers: central bank registers give law enforcement quick access to basic bank information, letting them verify which banks hold a suspect's accounts or the account holder's identity, so they can scope investigations early and focus only on the relevant financial institutions. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Central bank registers allow law enforcement agencies quick access to basic bank information"</sub>

## Box 33. Identifying hidden mule accounts

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 33 (Malta): an STR was filed on a suspected mule after suspicious transactions to different beneficiaries, with funds going to local and international banks linked to a suspected romance fraud. Searches of the national Central Bank Account Registry let the FIU immediately identify another active account of the mule at a different bank, quickly establish a holistic picture, scope further analysis and identify commonalities of laundering to other foreign individuals. (p. 45)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Searches through the national Central Bank Account Registry allowed the FIU to immediately identify another active account"</sub>

## Enhancing access to financial information

- **descriptive / recommendation**: Developing databases for private-private information sharing: in professional ML networks many mule accounts may be known or suspected from previous scams (romance, lottery, employment) or identity takeover, and data and processes for identifying fraud overlap with those for mule networks. As a good practice some jurisdictions centralised data across anti-fraud and AML databases to identify deeper ML networks across FIs, prevent fraud and foster asset recovery. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "some jurisdictions have sought to centralise data that cuts across anti-fraud and AML databases"</sub>

## Box 34. Centralised private-private database

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 34 (Brazil): Brazil approved a Resolution making mandatory a database centralising information on fraud (including attempts) by all financial and payment institutions, enforced by Banco Central do Brasil and forecast to start operating in November 2023. Sharing is compulsory and the Resolution sets minimum information, including identification of persons involved (including money mules), the financial institution(s) involved and the account(s) used, to prevent and combat fraud and recover proceeds. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "sharing information about frauds (including attempts) are compulsory for institutions"</sub>

## Deterring money mules

- **descriptive / limitation**: Mules are recruited through many techniques and, depending on whether unwittingly tricked or exploited, have varying knowledge of and involvement in the CEF scheme, so authorities may struggle to bring ML charges because it can be difficult to prove the mule's criminal intent (awareness of participation in laundering). Some jurisdictions have legislated to lower the mens rea for the ML offence, such as from 'knowledge' to 'suspicion'. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to lower the _mens rea_ required in the ML offence, such as from “knowledge” to “suspicion”"</sub>

## Box 35. Article 9(3) of the Council of Europe Warsaw Convention

- **descriptive / context**: Box 35 (MONEYVAL): proving mens rea (that the launderer knew the proceeds were from crime) is a key difficulty in prosecuting ML, as defendants in complex professional cases commonly deny firm knowledge. Article 9(3) of the Council of Europe Warsaw Convention goes beyond the Vienna and Palermo Conventions' elements by establishing that the ML offence occurs even when the offender only suspected or ought to have assumed that the proceeds were generated by crime. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the ML offence occurs even when the offender only suspected or ought to have assumed"</sub>

## 4.4. Prevention and disruption

- **descriptive / context**: Given how fast funds dissipate, many jurisdictions explore initiatives to prevent CEF and related ML; prevention reduces syndicates' overall profitability and mitigates downstream resources from investigation to victim management. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Such an approach reduces the overall profitability of CEF syndicates"</sub>

## Box 36. Leveraging technology for public education on CEF

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 36 (Hong Kong China): the Hong Kong Police Force launched the 'Scameter' scam and pitfall search engine in September 2022; the public can enter a suspected fraudster's account name or number, payment account number, phone number, email address or URL to assess fraud and cyber security risk. Its data and ratings come from public reports to the police, information from organisations, a suspicious phone number database, and databases and real-time analysis from information security companies. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they can enter on Scameter the account name or number, payment account number, phone number, email address"</sub>

## Deterring money mules

- **permissive / recommendation**: Authorities may also use existing detection mechanisms (STRs and victim reports) to spot potential money mules who may have handled CEF proceeds; targeted outreach and warnings can tell such potential mules not to repeat the behaviour. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Authorities may also leverage existing detection mechanisms (STRs and victim reports)"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Records of past outreach or warnings can be used as helpful evidence when deciding whether criminal ML intent exists, if a mule reoffends. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Records of past outreach or warnings can be leveraged as useful evidence in determining criminal ML intent"</sub>

## Public education and outreach

- **permissive / recommendation**: A preventive approach can be taken by educating the public and raising vigilance against exploitation, including national awareness campaigns promoting cyber literacy. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A preventive approach can be adopted by educating the public and increasing vigilance against exploitation"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: To support this objective, some jurisdictions have used technology to run information campaigns that help citizens detect fraudulent operations, make them aware of tell-tale signs and encourage victims to report. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "some jurisdictions have leveraged technology to roll out information campaigns for citizens"</sub>

## Anti-fraud security and controls for AML/CFT outcomes

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Public and private sector experience shows anti-fraud and AML processes are complementary, including technology helping users automatically reject fraudulent messages, horizon scanning with the private sector to mitigate emerging fraud trends, account security features, controls and rules, and anti-virus warnings for potential phishing sites; Annex B compiles examples of financial regulators adopting anti-fraud requirements alongside AML/CFT controls. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "anti-fraud and AML processes are complementary"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Real-time transaction monitoring, using sophisticated software and algorithms, is considered useful for detecting and preventing CEF; given digitalisation's information overflow CEF may be hard to detect manually, and real-time monitoring can help FIs find patterns of suspicious activity across multiple accounts or transactions even if not directly linked. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "CEF might be difficult to detect through manual processes"</sub>

## Removing criminal instrumentalities

- **descriptive / recommendation**: Because CEF can be perpetrated through non-traditional sectors, some jurisdictions strengthened anti-fraud controls there, including targeting CEF instrumentalities such as shutting down mobile lines and fraudulent webpages used by criminals and filtering phishing messages and malicious weblinks. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "some jurisdictions have strengthened anti-fraud prevention and controls in such non-traditional sectors"</sub>

## Anti-fraud security and controls for AML/CFT outcomes

- **recommended / recommendation**: The report describes it as another good practice to encourage FIs to adopt real-time transaction monitoring so as to identify and prevent fraudulent or illicit activity as it happens. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "encouraging FIs to adopt real-time transaction monitoring to identify and prevent fraudulent or illicit activities in real-time"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: By monitoring abnormal account holder information (for example physical, IP and email addresses and mobile numbers) and transactions in real time, FIs can quickly identify, investigate and report unusual or suspicious activity. (p. 48)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs can quickly identify, investigate, and report any unusual or suspicious activity"</sub>

## Box 37. Removing suspicious websites and phishing campaigns

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 37 (Saudi Arabia): law enforcement and regulators work with telecom providers to predict, prevent, detect and respond to fraud; the National Cyber Security Authority imposed stringent brand protection requirements on countering clone websites and phishing messages on social platforms, and the Saudi Central Bank (SAMA) set cyber security and counter-fraud frameworks outlining mandatory baseline control requirements for regulated entities. Organisations are expected under these requirements to proactively monitor criminal instrumentalities such as suspicious websites and phishing campaigns and promptly report detections to authorities for swift investigation and shutdown. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "outlining mandatory baseline control requirements for regulated entities"</sub>

## Preventing asset dissipation

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Many jurisdictions find the rapid speed of laundering CEF proceeds one of the most challenging aspects of investigations; there is consensus that it is crucial for competent authorities to intervene swiftly before proceeds dissipate from bank accounts, and jurisdictions have implemented varied asset recovery measures (see section 5.1). (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is crucial for competent authorities to be able to intervene swiftly to reach the CEF proceeds"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: The report says there may be benefits in engaging key private-sector financial representatives so that they proactively intercept illicit funds once a victimised client notifies them of fraud, before competent authorities have contacted them; this includes information exchanges between domestic and foreign FIs or VASPs. (p. 49)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "proactive interception of illicit funds once a notice of fraud is received from a victimised client"</sub>

## Box 38. Egmont Group Bulletin on BEC fraud

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 38 (Egmont Group): in July 2019 the Egmont Group issued a bulletin alerting member FIUs to the rising BEC threat with key scenarios and risk indicators, noting FIs can play an important role in identifying, preventing and reporting BEC by promoting communication and collaboration among internal AML, business, fraud prevention and cybersecurity units. To help investigate BEC and recover funds, beneficiary FIs that learn a fraudulent transfer reached a customer's account (e.g. a SWIFT recall message) were advised not to carry out any transactions that could lead to loss of funds and to contact law enforcement or the FIU to assess the transaction's validity. (p. 50)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "were advised to not carry out any transactions that could lead to the loss of funds"</sub>

## 6. Conclusion and priority areas

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 107: CEF is perpetrated by transnational organised crime syndicates, and its scale and magnitude are expected to grow with rising digitalisation and virtual services globally. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "CEF is perpetrated by transnational, organised crime syndicates"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 107 says jurisdictions should be aware of additional vulnerabilities across sectors, including digital financial institutions and non-traditional sectors, that criminals may exploit to enhance CEF and ML techniques as digitalisation grows. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Jurisdictions should also be aware of the additional vulnerabilities across various sectors"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 108: jurisdictions need to focus on breaking down siloes to accelerate and enhance collaboration between sectors and entities, domestically and internationally. Because CEF and its laundering are de-centralised, vital financial information and evidence are often fragmented across locations, complicating investigation, dismantling of syndicates, and tracing and recovery of proceeds. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Jurisdictions need to focus on breaking down siloes to accelerate and enhance collaboration"</sub>
- **expected / finding or result**: Paragraph 109: CEF can have significant and crippling financial impact on victims, and beyond monetary losses can have devastating social and economic implications. The report identifies three priority areas in which jurisdictions should act: enhancing domestic co-ordination; supporting multi-lateral collaboration; and strengthening detection and prevention. (p. 63)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "three priority areas in which jurisdictions should act to tackle CEF and related ML more effectively"</sub>

## Enhancing domestic co-ordination across public and private sectors

- **expected / expectation**: Jurisdictions should develop co-ordination mechanisms bringing together relevant competent authorities to tackle CEF and laundering of its proceeds holistically, including technical cybercrime experts and non-traditional sectors such as social media platforms, e-commerce, telecommunication and internet service providers. They should also leverage public-private partnerships to improve detection and investigations and accelerate operational asset recovery responses. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Jurisdictions should develop co-ordination mechanisms to bring together relevant competent authorities"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: A good practice is creating a dedicated centralised unit that can harness relevant information and co-ordinate actions across public and private sectors, including investigations, asset recovery and fraud prevention. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "creation of a dedicated centralised unit that can harness relevant information"</sub>

## Supporting multi-lateral international collaboration

- **expected / expectation**: To improve asset recovery and avoid dissipation of CEF proceeds, jurisdictions should work together to intercept proceeds expeditiously; operational experience shows intervention is generally most effective within 24 to 72 hours of a CEF incident. A global united approach is required to trace and recover proceeds laundered and distributed across multiple jurisdictions. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Operational experience shows that intervention is generally most effective within 24 to 72 hours of a CEF incident"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Jurisdictions should leverage and support existing and future multi-lateral mechanisms (such as INTERPOL's I-GRIP and the Egmont Group BEC Project) for rapid international co-operation and information exchange; these also let jurisdictions collaborate to dismantle transnational CEF syndicates. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions should leverage and support existing (and any future) multi-lateral mechanisms"</sub>

## Strengthening detection and prevention

- **expected / expectation**: To enhance detection, jurisdictions should ensure ease of victim reporting, for example through dedicated platforms for streamlined reporting, and should work with the private sector to improve suspicious transaction reporting. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions should ensure ease of victim reporting, for example, through dedicated platforms"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Jurisdictions should promote awareness and vigilance against CEF through public education, including sharing tell-tale signs and enhancing cyber literacy; prevention plays a key role in reducing syndicates' profitability. Jurisdictions can also collaborate with the private sector on prevention strategies such as consumer protection and removal of criminal instrumentalities. (p. 64)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Jurisdictions should promote awareness and vigilance against CEF through public education"</sub>

## Annex A: Risk indicators for CEF

- **descriptive / context**: The Annex A risk indicators draw on experience and data from jurisdictions across the FATF Global Network, the Egmont Group and the private sector, aim to enhance detection of suspicious CEF transactions, and are categorised from account opening to transaction monitoring. They can be relevant to regulated entities including FIs, VASPs, DNFBPs and other financial and payment institutions. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These indicators aim to enhance the detection of suspicious transactions relating to CEF"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: A single indicator for a customer or transaction may not alone warrant suspicion of CEF, nor necessarily give a clear indication of it, but it could prompt further monitoring and examination as appropriate. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The existence of a single indicator in relation to a customer or transaction may not alone warrant suspicion"</sub>

## _Transaction patterns_

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Transaction pattern indicators (first part): rapid or immediate high or low value transactions after account opening that are inconsistent with the account's purpose; rapid or immediate cash withdrawals or transfers of large amounts after receiving a funds transfer to empty the account; and frequent large transactions inconsistent with the account holder's economic profile (e.g. sudden international transfers, cash withdrawals via payment cards at foreign ATMs, large purchases of VA or goods to be exported, or payments to unlicensed foreign MVTS). (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Rapid or immediate cash withdrawals or transfers of large amounts following the receipt of a funds transfer"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Transaction pattern indicators (second part): transfers to and from high-risk money laundering jurisdictions; large frequent transactions with recently established companies and/or whose main activities are inconsistent with the beneficiary's activities or have a general purpose; a small payment to a beneficiary, once completed, rapidly followed by larger payments to the same beneficiary; and frequent and/or large round-value purchases, which can indicate gift card purchases. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Small payment to a beneficiary, which once successfully completed, is rapidly followed by larger value payments"</sub>

## _Customer transaction instructions and remarks_

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Instruction and remark indicators (first part): a customer asks for additional payments immediately after a successful payment to an account not previously used to pay its suppliers/vendors, which may be consistent with a criminal trying to issue more unauthorised payments after learning a fraudulent payment succeeded; seemingly legitimate instructions with different language vernacular, timing and amounts from previously verified instructions; and instructions marked 'Urgent', 'Secret' or 'Confidential'. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A customer transaction requests for additional payments immediately following a successful payment to an account"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Instruction and remark indicators (second part): a customer justifying a transaction with poorly formatted messages/emails (spelling or grammar mistakes); instructions directing payment to a known beneficiary but with account information different from before; inconsistency between the intended beneficiary in the transaction description and the account holder name known to the beneficiary bank; and transfers by natural persons (alleged investors) with no financial experience to companies (often in high-risk jurisdictions) citing investments and financial products. (p. 65)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Transfers ordered by natural persons (alleged investors) with no financial experience and expertise"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Instruction and remark indicators (final items): counterparties incommensurate with the account's business/company name that might provide cover for moving large sums internationally (e.g. a reported furniture company making multiple large transfers to a company named as a petroleum trading company); and transactions conducted with a device time zone mismatch. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the company reported as a furniture company made multiple large transfer to a company named as petroleum trading company"</sub>

## _Suspicion in account holder’s profile_

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Account holder profile indicators: unwilling or unable to pass CDD checks; unfamiliar with the source of funds moving through the account or claiming to transact for someone else; frequent changes of legal entities'/sole proprietorships' names using foreign expressions and terminology; and inadequate knowledge of the nature, object, amount or purpose of transactions or relationship, or unrealistic, confusing or inconsistent explanations that suggest the customer is acting as a mule. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Account holder is unwilling or unable to pass CDD checks"</sub>

## _Suspicion in account user’s identity_

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Account user identity indicators (first part): attempting to conceal identity using shared, falsified, stolen or altered identification (address, telephone number, email); frequent changes of contact details after account opening; email addresses incompatible with the account holder's name or a pattern of similar addresses across multiple accounts; and shared credentials with other accounts (shared by two or more users). (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The user is attempting to conceal their identity by using shared, falsified, stolen or altered identification"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Account user identity indicators (second part): abnormal online behaviour such as hesitation inputting data, keystroke delays, signs of automation or multiple failed logins; accounts of entities expected to be no longer active in the jurisdiction (e.g. overseas students' accounts sold after finishing study); and IP addresses or GPS coordinates from high-risk money laundering jurisdictions. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Abnormalities identified via online behaviour, such as hesitation inputting data, keystroke delays, signs of automation"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Account user identity indicators (final items): use of VPNs, compromised devices (such as IOT devices) and hosting companies that may mask IP addresses; multiple IP addresses or devices tied to a single online account; a single static IP address or device tied to multiple accounts of various holders; remote desktop access through ports used by applications such as TeamViewer, which prevents the true device and location being seen; and accounts operated with excessively quick keystrokes or navigation suggesting possible bot control. (p. 66)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Single static IP address or electronic device associated with multiple accounts of various account holders"</sub>

## _Adverse information on the account holder_

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Adverse information indicators: material, relevant and verifiable negative news on the customer or counterparties (e.g. an account held by a known or suspected previous victim of scam, mule or identity takeover activity); a fraud report or recall from a correspondent institution or other third-party fraud databases; wire transfer recall requests; and adverse information about persons involved in a transaction provided by FIUs or LEAs. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Presence of adverse information provided by FIUs or LEAs about persons involved in a transaction"</sub>

## VA transactions

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Annex A VA transaction risk indicator: sending or receiving either large volumes of virtual assets, or many small-value transfers at high frequency, to unhosted wallet addresses or to addresses associated with darknet marketplaces, child sexual abuse material platforms, cyber exploit marketplaces, ransomware groups, mixing or tumbling services, high-risk jurisdictions, gambling sites and scammers. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Sending/receiving large volumes or high frequency low amounts worth of VAs to unhosted wallet addresses"</sub>

## _VA transactions_

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: VA transaction indicators (second part): no documents proving the origin of the VA or of money converted into cryptoassets; transfers of VAs to wallets linked to illegal dark web activity (e.g. terrorism, child pornography, narcotics); transactions involving more than one type of VA, particularly those giving higher anonymity; and abnormal VA activity from peer-to-peer platform wallets with no logical business explanation. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "No documents proving the origin of VA or of the money converted in cryptoassets"</sub>

## _Other_

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Other indicators: mismatch of account number and account holder name; the user seen on CCTV on the phone or accompanied by an individual and being instructed or coached during the transaction; and beneficiary companies running websites offering trading/investment services, in many cases not authorised or listed by the domestic supervisory authority. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The user is seen on the phone or accompanied by an individual through Closed Circuit Television (CCTV)"</sub>

## Annex B: Harnessing synergies between anti-fraud and AML/CFT controls

- **descriptive / context**: Annex B compiles good examples of financial regulators adopting anti-fraud requirements alongside AML/CFT controls, some targeting criminals' ability to register, access and control mule accounts remotely, including measures on customer verification and transaction monitoring. These controls may be useful for FIs, VASPs and other financial and payment institutions. (p. 69)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "good examples of how financial regulators have adopted anti-fraud requirements alongside AML/CFT controls"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Annex B control examples: rigorous Know-Your-Customer or Know-Your-Business processes, biometric features in digital on-boarding, and identification of one mobile or secure device to authenticate online banking transactions (others blocked or subject to enhanced risk mitigation); and a cooling-off period for first-time enrolment in online banking or secure devices (full suite of services not immediately available), limiting the number or value of transactions. (p. 69)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A cooling-off period for first time enrolment of online banking services or secure devices"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Annex B control examples: developing a definition of expected transactions (number, amounts, types of counterparties, countries involved) to help detect suspicious transactions, with tighter fraud detection rules and triggers to pre-emptively block illicit transactions; and 'verification of payee' services that let the payer check the named beneficiary matches the account holder's name. (p. 69)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Developing a definition of expected transactions (number of transactions, amounts, types of counterparties, countries involved)"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Annex B control examples: reducing email and social media communication with clients to general information only, explicitly stating no identification or personal data should be exchanged with the FI/VASP by email; adding voice recognition software and artificial intelligence support in client communication to ensure true identity; and requiring multi-factor authentication for customer verification, performing transactions, and adding or activating beneficiaries using different channels. (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Adding voice recognition software and artificial intelligence support in the communication with the clients"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Annex B control examples for authenticating the user during remote set-up and preventing criminals accessing multiple accounts via mules' or victims' account information: enhancing client identification reliability through liveness tests (ensuring a live, genuine human, including whether the individual is being socially engineered during the checks); or monitoring IP addresses used to connect to online banking sites, including detecting Remote Access Tools and 'Man-in-the-Browser' attacks. (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "liveness tests (i.e., ensuring live and genuine human being)"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Annex B control examples: extending the data reporting entities collect and analyse about customers (e.g. mobile phone numbers, IP addresses, GPS coordinates, device ID), where for fraud prevention FIs could repeat identification using a risk-based approach (e.g. when anomalous behaviour is detected). (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Extending the types of data that reporting entities collect and analyse about customers"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Annex B control example: implementing a risk-based real-time transactions monitoring system so abnormal activity can be swiftly detected, investigated and where relevant reported by suspicious transaction report; the sophistication of the monitoring system should be commensurate with the volume and nature of transactions the FI handles. (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Implementing a risk-based real-time transactions monitoring system to ensure that any abnormal activity can be swiftly detected"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The back page (November 2023, with Egmont Group, INTERPOL and FATF web addresses) summarises the report: it analyses methods used for cyber-enabled fraud, its links to other crimes and how criminals may exploit vulnerabilities in new technologies; highlights successful national operational responses and strategies; and identifies risk indicators and useful anti-fraud requirements and controls to help public and private sector entities detect and prevent CEF and related money laundering. (p. 72)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "how criminals may exploit vulnerabilities in new technologies"</sub>

## 5.3 Asset confiscation

- **descriptive / finding or result**: In Hong Kong, China the most common restrained and confiscated assets were funds in bank accounts, insurance and securities held with licensed corporations or banks; other assets included precious metals, stones, jewellery, wristwatches and physical cash. ## 5.3.1 Hong Kong, China

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 124: in 2011 several companies in several jurisdictions were deceived by an email scam into remitting over HKD 500,000 (about USD 69,570) to a Hong Kong bank account set up by a stooge, who immediately left Hong Kong; in 2023 Hong Kong Police prevented dissipation of the funds and obtained a local confiscation order over the tainted funds remaining in the stooge's account. (p. 121)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "were deceived to remit over HKD 500,000 (~ USD 69,570) to a bank account set up in Hong Kong"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 125: a couple laundered drug-crime proceeds of HKD 14 million (about USD 1.948 million) through five personal bank accounts between 2014 and 2016, showing frequent and significant turnover not commensurate with income; after conviction and trial in 2023, assets of HKD 150,125 (about USD 19,311) were confiscated. (p. 121)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they had laundered proceeds amounting to HKD 14 million (~ USD 1.948 million) through five personal bank accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 126: over 20 foreign domestic helpers sold their bank accounts to a money laundering syndicate, which received online scam proceeds and withdrew them in cash at ATMs; two core members and 24 helpers were arrested, two core members and six stooges were convicted and sentenced to eight to 30 months' imprisonment, and a confiscation order covered HKD 204,598.42, USD 200 and PHP 20; prosecutions of other stooges are ongoing. (p. 122)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Further investigation revealed that over 20 foreign domestic helpers had sold their bank accounts to a money laundering syndicate."</sub>

## 5.3.2 Macao, China

- **descriptive / context**: The Penal Code of Macao, China has specific chapters on loss of assets or rights related to criminal activities, provisions for assets used or to be used in crime or resulting from it, and confiscation of rewards, items, rights or profits obtained directly or indirectly through crime. (p. 122)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It also has provisions for the handling of assets used for or to be used for criminal actions"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Where proceeds of crime cannot be seized in a material manner, the criminal must pay a certain amount of money to Macao, China as compensation. (p. 122)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the criminal will have to pay a certain amount of money to Macao, China as compensation"</sub>

## 5.3.3 Malaysia

- **descriptive / limitation**: Malaysia reported general challenges in confiscation or forfeiture: difficulty establishing the money or asset trail, defendants not located or absconding, difficulty repatriating assets moved overseas due to differing legal frameworks and multiple jurisdictions, and loss of asset value through depreciation. (p. 122)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Difficult to establish the money/asset trail or link. Defendant could not be located, contacted or absconded."</sub>

## 5.3.4 Chinese Taipei

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Chinese Taipei's Criminal Code confiscation provisions took effect on 1 July 2016; as of February 2024 the total confiscation amount ordered by courts exceeded TWD 169.5 billion (approximately USD 5.3 billion). (p. 122)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "As of February 2024, the total confiscation amount ordered by the court has reached more than TWD 169.5 billion"</sub>

## 5.4 Use and sharing of confiscated proceeds

- **descriptive / case example**: Chinese Taipei's Constitutional Court debated Article 19, Paragraph 3 of the Narcotics Hazard Prevention Act from 17 November 2023, concluding on 26 January 2024, and held that extended confiscation of gains serves the same restorative purpose as general confiscation rather than punishment, and does not violate nulla poena sine lege, culpability, presumption of innocence, or the intelligible principle. (p. 122)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Constitutional Court declared that the extended confiscation of gains serves the same purpose as general confiscation"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Constitutional Court judgment held that extended confiscation of gains is based on public interest, reasonably related to preventing drug crimes, and in accordance with the principle of proportionality. (p. 123)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is in accordance with the principle of proportionality"</sub>

## 5.4.1 China

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 127: a pyramid scheme run by a syndicate of more than ten members, which cheated participants by collecting yuan to buy foreign products and stocks online, spread across 18 provinces with nearly 50,000 participants; leaders were arrested in December 2015 and convicted of organising pyramid selling. (p. 123)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This pyramid scheme spread across 18 provinces and involved nearly 50,000 participants."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In Case Study 127, Hunan public security agencies, under the Fox Hunt Operation Office at the Ministry of Public Security, cooperated with foreign police on asset recovery; proceeds were laundered through an underground banking network; in 2023 an in-principle asset sharing agreement was reached and the foreign counterpart returned assets worth 166 million yuan (about USD 22.8 million). (p. 123)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The foreign counterpart shared and returned assets with a total value of 166 million yuan (~USD 22.8 million)."</sub>

## 5.4.2 Japan

- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 128: three persons defrauded a pension fund with falsified investment performance data on a fund managed by Company A, where Person A was CEO, obtaining about JPY 24.8 billion (about USD 158 million); the court ordered confiscation and collection of an equivalent sum. (p. 123)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This resulted in the trio fraudulently obtaining approximately JPY 24.8 billion (~ USD 158 million)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In Case Study 128 Japan sent a mutual legal assistance request to Jurisdiction B; in May 2023 Jurisdiction B transferred about JPY 717 million (about USD 4.59 million) to Japan, which is distributing the funds to victims. (p. 124)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In May 2023, Jurisdiction B transferred the funds valued at approximately JPY 717 million (~ USD 4.59 million) to Japan."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 129: two persons defrauded foreign exchange investors through an investment school of about JPY 1.9 billion (about USD 12 million); the court ordered confiscation and collection of an equivalent sum; Japan's MLA request to Jurisdiction A recovered about JPY 120 million from an account held by Person B, distributed to victims, while JPY 1.78 billion was remitted to bank accounts in other jurisdictions. (p. 124)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "They fraudulently obtained approximately JPY 1.9 billion (~ USD 12 million) from the victims."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Case Study 130: the Malaysian FIU received a SAR from a foreign FIU about virtual assets of two Malaysian individuals held with a VASP, linked by the Royal Malaysian Police to a Bitcoin ponzi scheme that offered daily returns and referral commissions; an MLA request led to seizure of about USD 34.5 million in virtual assets, with investigations ongoing. (p. 124)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Malaysian authorities took immediate action by submitting an MLA request which led to the seizure of VAs amounting to approximately USD 34.5 million"</sub>

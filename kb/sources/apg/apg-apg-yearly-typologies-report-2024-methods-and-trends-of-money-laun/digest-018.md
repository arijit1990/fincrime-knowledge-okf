## Misuse of Citizenship and Residency by Investment Programmes

- **descriptive / context**: Each year tens of thousands of people become new citizens or permanent residents of countries where they were not born by investing; CBI/RBI programmes are government-administered and can benefit host jurisdictions and wealthy individuals. (p. 128)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Each year, tens of thousands of people around the world become new citizens or permanent residents"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: CBI/RBI programmes attract many clients who gained assets legitimately, but can be misused by criminals to launder and conceal proceeds of crime or commit new offences including financial crimes. (p. 128)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they can also be misused by criminals who seek to launder and conceal proceeds of crime or commit new offences"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The joint FATF and OECD report examines ML and financial crime risks of investment migration programmes, including foreign bribery, fraud, corruption, and public integrity, tax and migration considerations. (p. 128)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This joint report of the FATF and the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) holistically examines ML"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: The project was co-led by experts from the United States and United Kingdom with OECD experts and FATF Secretariat support, drawing on experts from 19 FATF Global Network members and three FATF Observers. (p. 128)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Experts from the United States and United Kingdom co-led this project team alongside experts from the OECD"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: The project relied on questionnaire responses from 36 FATF Global Network jurisdictions, an OECD Working Party of Senior Public Integrity Officials questionnaire, private sector input and academic and NPO literature. (p. 129)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The project relied on information, case studies, and jurisdiction examples provided in questionnaire responses returned by 36 jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Criminals exploited CBI/RBI vulnerabilities to perpetrate massive frauds and launder proceeds of crime and corruption reaching billions of USD, and to hide assets in less compliant jurisdictions. (p. 129)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "reaching into the billions of dollars (USD)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: CBI programmes are particularly vulnerable because they give illicit actors global mobility, the ability to open bank accounts and establish shell companies abroad, and disguise identity or tax liabilities using new identification documents. (p. 129)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "CBI programmes are particularly vulnerable because they allow illicit actors more global mobility"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Both CBI and RBI programmes let criminally wealthy people place assets and family members overseas to hinder asset recovery, explain suspicious high-value transactions and move illicit funds across borders. (p. 129)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the ability to place assets and family members overseas to prevent or hinder asset recovery efforts"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Professional enablers and intermediaries such as property agents, wealth managers, immigration agents, marketing agents and concierge firms can misuse programmes by failing due diligence and reporting or creating fraudulent evidence. (p. 129)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Criminal, negligent, or complicit property agents, wealth managers immigration agents, marketing agents and concierge firms"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Opportunities for misuse arise when governments struggle to govern programmes, including unclear roles of public and private actors, unmanaged conflicts of interest, lack of resources, and weak internal control and audit. (p. 129)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "where conflicts of interest are not adequately managed, and where resources are lacking to ensure proper oversight."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Vulnerable programmes may lead to suspension of visa-free travel to third jurisdictions and undermine business and international relations. (p. 129)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Programmes appearing vulnerable to criminal misuse may lead to suspension of visa-free travel"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: The report proposes mitigation measures including sound ML and corruption risk analysis, clear objectives, integrity measures in programme design and implementation, domestic co-ordination among law enforcement, immigration and FIUs, and multilateral cooperation. (p. 129)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Conducting sound analysis of ML and corruption risks, setting clear objectives and building integrity measures"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Policymakers, programme operators, financial institutions and law enforcement should be alert to elevated ML and financial crime risks from applicants and from professional enablers and intermediaries. (p. 129)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "policymakers, programme operators, financial institutions and law enforcement should be particularly alert to the elevated risks"</sub>

## Recovering the International Proceeds of Crime through Inter-Agency Networks

- **descriptive / context**: The report on Asset Recovery Inter-Agency Networks (ARINs) gives policymakers and law enforcement a broad review of ARINs' global impact, roles, management, challenges and cooperation with other international groups. (p. 129)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This report on Asset Recovery Inter-Agency Networks (ARINs) is specifically designed for policymakers and law enforcement agencies"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: ARINs facilitate informal assistance across asset identification, tracing, seizing, freezing, confiscation and repatriation, but policymakers often lack clarity on them and their use is not widespread or systematic, with only a handful of investigations facilitated. (p. 130)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The use of ARINs is also not wide-spread or systematic, with only a handful of investigations facilitated by these networks globally"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: ARINs build trust among law enforcement practitioners and act as intermediaries with other regional networks, but co-ordination with other international organisations often falls short. (p. 130)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "ARINs play a vital role in building trust among law enforcement practitioners."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: ARIN coverage has expanded but regional gaps persist, notably in the Middle East, North Africa and Central Africa; there are 178 member jurisdictions, including 159 of the 205 FATF Global Network jurisdictions. (p. 130)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Currently, there are 178 Member jurisdictions within ARIN networks, including 159 members of the FATF's Global Network of 205 jurisdictions."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: ARINs operate independently with their own governance, mandate and principles; participation is not compulsory, but they build stronger investigator ties and have provided details on bank accounts, businesses, real estate and registered possessions abroad. (p. 130)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "While participation in ARINs is not compulsory, they are proven to build stronger ties between investigators and asset recovery offices."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: ARINs face resource challenges: some leading ARINs oversee hundreds, if not thousands, of yearly tracing requests, secretariat staffing can be limiting, long-term financial sustainability is a common challenge, and language and cultural barriers hinder effectiveness. (p. 130)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Other obstacles to the effectiveness of ARINs include language and cultural barriers."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The relationship between ARINs and FSRBs varies by region: close co-ordination in some cases and none in others. (p. 130)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The relationship between ARINs and the FSRBs varies considerably from one region to another."</sub>

- **descriptive / context**: In 2019 the FATF extended its AML/CFT standards to virtual assets and VASPs; a February 2023 Roadmap to strengthen implementation of Recommendation 15 included a status table, to be updated in 2025. (p. 130)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "To strengthen implementation of Recommendation 15 (R.15), the FATF adopted a Roadmap in February 2023."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: This is the fifth targeted review of implementation of the FATF Standards on VAs and VASPs, including the Travel Rule, led by the Virtual Assets Contact Group co-chaired by Japan and the United States. (p. 131)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This report provides a fifth targeted review of implementation of the FATF’s Standards on VAs and VASPs including the Travel Rule"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Compared with the 2023 survey, some jurisdictions have progressed in putting AML/CFT regulation in place, but continued lack of global implementation leaves VAs and VASPs vulnerable and implementation remains behind other financial sectors. (p. 131)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a continued lack of implementation of the relevant FATF Standards globally means that VAs and VASPs remain vulnerable to misuse"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: VAs are used to support proliferation of weapons of mass destruction and by scammers, terrorist groups and other illicit actors; the DPRK steals or extorts virtual assets and increasingly uses sophisticated laundering methods. (p. 131)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The DPRK continues to steal or extort virtual assets from victims and"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Terrorist groups, notably ISIL in Asia and groups in Syria, use virtual assets, often stablecoins, and experiment with anonymity-enhancing cryptocurrencies. (p. 131)[^original]

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Private sector reports note increasing use of stablecoins for ML/TF/PF, continued hacks of DeFi arrangements, and some progress in smart-contract risk mitigation. (p. 131)[^original]

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Several jurisdictions reported progress such as applying AML/CFT/CPF and Travel Rule requirements to stablecoin providers, enforcement against DeFi arrangements, and risk assessments of DeFi and unhosted wallets including peer-to-peer transactions. (p. 131)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "conducting risk assessments on DeFi and unhosted wallets including peer-to-peer transactions."</sub>

## Horizontal Review of Gatekeepers' Technical Compliance Related to Corruption

- **descriptive / context**: Corruption and ML are inextricably linked, and non-financial professionals acting as gatekeepers can facilitate high-level corruption wittingly or unwittingly. (p. 131)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Through their role as gatekeepers to the financial system, non-financial professionals can facilitate, unwittingly or wittingly, high-level corruption."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FATF over 20 years ago required jurisdictions to apply AML/CFT measures to gatekeepers: lawyers, accountants, TCSP and real estate agents, to equip them to detect indications of possible crimes. (p. 131)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FATF took action over 20 years ago by requiring jurisdictions around the globe to apply AML/CFT measures to gatekeepers"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Gatekeepers not regulated in line with the FATF Standards remain exposed to significant criminal risks and lack measures to see ML red flags. (p. 131)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "When these professionals are not regulated in accordance with the FATF Standards, they remain exposed to significant criminal risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The Horizontal Review was scoped to assess FATF members' compliance with Recommendation 22 (criteria 1 to 3), Recommendation 23 (criteria 1, 2 and 4) and Recommendation 28 (criteria 2 to 5) for real estate agents, lawyers, notaries, other independent legal professionals, accountants and TCSP. (p. 131)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Recommendation 22 (criteria 1 to 3), Recommendation 23 (criteria 1, 2 and 4) and Recommendation 28 (criteria 2 to 5)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Over half of FATF members scored above 80%, but seven members scoring below 50% represent more than half of the world's GDP. (p. 131)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "over half of FATF members have scores over 80%."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The review found little difference in coverage scores among the four gatekeeper sectors (lawyers, accountants, TCSP and real estate agents), contrary to the common perception that lawyers face fewer AML/CFT rules. (p. 131)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Horizontal Review found little difference in coverage scores of the four gatekeeper sectors"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: FATF members still lagging behind should urgently ensure gatekeepers are adequately covered in line with the FATF Recommendations; the project does not pre-empt any Mutual Evaluation Report. (p. 131)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is urgent that those FATF members still lagging behind ensure that gatekeepers are adequately covered"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Some cornerstone obligations on customer due diligence, internal controls and supervisor powers for risk-based supervision lag behind compliance levels for other obligations of the FATF Recommendations. (p. 131)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "conducting customer due diligence, implementing internal controls, and providing a supervisor with adequate powers"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Horizontal Review describes customer due diligence, internal controls and adequate supervisory powers for risk-based supervision as essential requirements to address gatekeepers' vulnerability to ML and corruption threats. (p. 131)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "are essential requirements to address the vulnerability of gatekeepers to ML and corruption threats"</sub>

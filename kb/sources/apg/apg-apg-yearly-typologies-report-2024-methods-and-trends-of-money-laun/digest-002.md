## Beneficial ownership

- **descriptive / context**: A quoted observation from an APG and ATO paper on tax crimes and ML holds that concealing beneficial ownership ensures the true owner of an asset or revenue stream cannot be associated with it. (p. 13)[^original]

- **descriptive / finding or result**: In tax crime cases, concealing beneficial ownership led to non-payment of taxable income and frustrated confiscation/forfeiture; the observation is arguably true for all predicates and stand-alone ML. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "concealment of beneficial ownership led to non-payment of taxable income and frustration of confiscation/forfeiture processes"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Complex legal structures using companies and trusts across multiple jurisdictions are common in tax crimes and related ML, and are used to obscure beneficial ownership of vessels and companies in illegal fishing. (p. 13)[^original]

  <sub>anchor: "only half of surveyed members require the beneficial ownership of legal persons to be recorded in a central registry"</sub>

## Complex ownership structures

- **descriptive / context**: A main challenge in identifying beneficial owners is complex ownership structures with several layers, offshore entities or sophisticated control mechanisms; jurisdictions generally do not limit layers, nationality of entities or residence of owners. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Jurisdictions tend not to put limits on the number of layers, the nationality of the entities involved in the ownership chain"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Jurisdictions tend not to explicitly prohibit circular ownership structures, which hinder identification of beneficial owners. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions tend not to explicitly prohibit circular ownership structures"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: It may be impossible to distinguish concealing complex structures from those resulting from legitimate business activity, such as a multinational acquiring companies. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it may be impossible to distinguish between complex structures that attempt to conceal the beneficial owner"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Complex ownership shifts cost from the beneficial owner, who pays the same incorporation fee regardless, to competent authorities and reporting entities who spend considerable time and resources identifying owners at each layer. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a complex ownership structure shifts the cost from the beneficial owner"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Each jurisdiction or reporting entity determines what it considers complex, depending on factors such as sector, industry, employees or income; a complex structure may be normal for a multinational but suspicious for a PEP holding real estate. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is up to each jurisdiction or reporting entity to determine what they consider ‘complex’."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Experts in EU AML Global Facility discussions identified indicators of complex structures including the length of vertical and horizontal ownership chains and use of nominees. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Length of the ownership chain, both vertical and horizontal."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Further indicators include foreign entities (especially from higher-risk or blacklisted jurisdictions), presence of trusts combined with legal persons, and ownership by investment funds or foreign listed companies. (p. 14)[^original]

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: A lack of written documents naming the beneficial owner, such as oral nominee arrangements or powers of attorney so the beneficial owner holds no shares, is an indicator. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Lack of written documents naming the beneficial owner (e.g. oral communications between nominees and beneficial owners"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Use of generic terms in public documents, referring to a person together with family members, trusts, partnerships or LLCs for their benefit, is an indicator. (p. 14)[^original]

## Trade-based money laundering

- **descriptive / definition**: Trade-based money laundering is broadly defined as disguising proceeds of crime and moving value using trade transactions to legitimise illicit origins; it is frequently used with other ML activities. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Trade-based money-laundering (TBML) is broadly defined as disguising the proceeds of crime and moving value using trade transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Large volumes of global trade flows provide opportunities to obscure illicit transactions, particularly combined with foreign exchange or diverse trade-financing arrangements. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Large volumes of global trade flows provide opportunities to obscure individual illicit transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The World Customs Organisation identified two common methodologies: use of straw directors and related legal persons to conduct TBML; and tax fraud entities laundering funds with bearer negotiable instruments before exporting goods overseas and mis-declaring quantity. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Use of straw directors (nominees) and related legal persons to conduct TBML."</sub>

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Criminals set up two similarly named legal persons in two jurisdictions, one as main entity and one as pass-through account; ownership transferred to straw directors while original directors run operations; goods bought with proceeds are exported and undervalued.

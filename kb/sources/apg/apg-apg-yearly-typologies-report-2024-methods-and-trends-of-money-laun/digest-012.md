## 3.2.7 Macao, China

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Macao, China lists common ML methods detected from STRs in 2023 including chip conversion with no or minimal gambling activity, irregular large cash withdrawals, currency exchanges and cash conversion, and significant cash deposits with non-verifiable source of funds. (p. 84)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Chips conversion without / with minimal gambling activities. • Irregular large cash withdrawals."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Further Macao, China methods include chip conversion, marker redemption or gambling on behalf of third parties; use of ATMs, phone banking and cash deposit machines; transfers by cheque or account transfer; suspicious wire transfers; online banking use; and possible matches with screening watch-lists or blacklists. (p. 84)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Chip conversion / marker redemption / gambling on behalf of third parties."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In 2023 the Macao, China FIU (GIF) received 4,614 STRs: 3,431 from the gaming sector, 887 from the financial sector (banking, insurance and financial intermediaries) and 296 from other sectors. (p. 84)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "GIF received 4,614 STRs, with 3,431 STRs from the gaming sector"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: GIF disseminated 116 STRs to the Public Prosecutions Office for investigation by law enforcement, mainly related to crime syndicates and fraud. (p. 84)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "GIF disseminated 116 STRs to the Public Prosecutions Office for further investigation"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Most crimes including drugs, gambling-related crimes and theft increased year over year but remained below 2019 levels, while cybercrime and telecommunications fraud kept rising and were above pre-COVID-19 levels in 2023. (p. 84)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they were still lower than the 2019 level. In 2023, cybercrimes and telecommunications fraud continued to rise"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Judiciary Police (PJ) work under three concepts: strengthening the crackdown on gambling-related crimes, deepening anti-fraud work, and enhancing deployment to respond to increased cross-border crime risk. (p. 84)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These concepts focus on strengthening the crackdown on gambling-related crimes, deepening the anti-fraud work"</sub>

## 3.2.8 Malaysia

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Based on National Scam Response Centre cases, fake job scams lure victims with lucrative offers such as subscribing to YouTube channels, providing feedback or buying products. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Fake job scams: the fraudsters commonly attract the victim with lucrative offers"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Investment scams offer victims non-existing investment schemes promising high, quick returns with little or no risk. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Investment scams: victims were offered to join non-existing investment schemes"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Telecommunication scams involve impersonating authorities to lend legitimacy, exploiting fear of legal authorities and using the victim's personal information to gain trust. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "impersonating authorities to lend legitimacy to their schemes"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: E-commerce scams related to festive products increased during festival seasons, including counterfeit goods, fake promotions and phishing targeting buyers seeking festive goods. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a noticeable increase in e-commerce scams related to festivities products"</sub>

## 3.2.9 Maldives

- **descriptive / finding or result**: In 2023 the Maldives FIU strategically analysed threshold transactions (cash and electronic fund transactions above MVR 200,000, about USD 12,970) for January 2022 to March 2023 and found an increasing trend of TBML-related transactions through banks. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FIU identified an increasing trend of trade-based money laundering (TBML) related transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The FIU shared observations with banks, law enforcement and regulators and instructed banks to strengthen KYC and CDD to prevent TBML; this led to a decline in TBML-related STRs in the last quarter of 2023. (p. 85)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "resulted in a decline in TBML-related STRs during the last quarter of 2023"</sub>

## 3.2.11 Singapore

- **descriptive / context**: Singapore's key ML risks are misuse of legal persons, trade-based money laundering and syndicated ML including cyber-enabled fraud, and it remains vigilant to ML risk from wealth management. (p. 86)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The key risks for Singapore continue to relate to misuse of legal persons"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Per the MAS circular on wealth management ML/TF risks, financial institutions should stay vigilant to higher risk customers and transactions, including added risk from trust-type structures for beneficial owners. (p. 86)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIs should stay vigilant towards higher risk customers and transactions."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Wealth management red flags include prospective customers withdrawing applications due to inability or unwillingness to give CDD information, and anomalous transaction spikes or unexpected third-party fund flows, especially to or from higher risk jurisdictions. (p. 86)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "take note of prospective customers that withdraw their applications"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Singapore is vulnerable to TF through raising and moving funds for terrorists overseas; radicalised individuals pose a salient TF threat; new TF typologies include ransomware, arts and antiquities, and online crowd-funding. (p. 86)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Radicalised individuals continue to pose a salient TF threat to Singapore."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Singapore observed crowdfunding as an emerging TF typology, where terrorists may pose as charitable fundraisers to attract unwitting donors. (p. 86)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "terrorist actors may pretend to raise funds for charitable or humanitarian causes"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Singapore championed FATF crowdfunding for TF projects during its 2022-2024 FATF Presidency, culminating in a FATF report published in October 2023. (p. 86)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Crowdfunding for Terrorism Financing report published by FATF in October 2023"</sub>

## 3.2.12 Chinese Taipei

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Third-party payment providers are exploited for ML: criminals use money mules or shell companies to open accounts, lure victims into paying into virtual accounts, then move funds through multiple accounts or couriers to break the audit trail. (p. 87)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "creating breakpoints in the flow of funds to increase the difficulty of tracing the funds"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Indicator: third-party payment providers contracting with other providers as payers or payees in breach of the AML/CFT regulations for third-party payment enterprises. (p. 87)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "entering into contracts with other third-party payment providers as payers or payees"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Indicator: sellers quickly withdraw or transfer funds after receipt, often through groups suspected to be money mules. (p. 87)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the withdrawals are often conducted by a group of individuals who are suspected to be money mules"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Indicator: abnormal providers often have excessively small capital and share business addresses with similar businesses, suggesting shell companies. (p. 87)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "often have excessively small capital and share the same business address"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Indicator: clients claiming online stores provide invalid, fake or non-shopping websites, or are reluctant to give store URLs. (p. 87)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Lack of genuine online transactions: Clients claim to operate online stores"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Online gaming platforms with tradable in-game currency let criminals buy and sell virtual goods or currency to launder money. (p. 87)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "allowing criminals to exploit the in-game economic systems"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Drug-related transactions mainly use cash; cross-border flows use underground banking, dummy accounts and bulk cash smuggling by couriers, and syndicates separate movements of people, drugs and money. (p. 87)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "criminals use alternative remittance systems such as underground banking and dummy accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: STRs related to third-party payment service providers have been gradually increasing since 2019, although the series dipped slightly in 2021. (p. 87)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Since 2019, the number of STRs related to third-party payment service providers has been gradually increasing."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Yearly third-party payment STR counts were 462 in 2019, 504 in 2020, 496 in 2021, 702 in 2022 and 1,186 in 2023. (p. 87)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "(2019: 462 STRs, 2020: 504 STRs, 2021: 496 STRs, 2022: 702 STRs, 2023: 1,186 STRs)"</sub>

## 3.3 / Chinese Taipei drug crime

## 3.3.2 Cook Islands

## 3.3.3 Hong Kong, China

- **descriptive / context**: Hong Kong, China established a VASP licensing system and regulatory requirements in mid-2023; two licensed platforms offer BTC and ETH trading to retail investors under SFC regulation. (p. 88)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "two licensed VA trading platforms, through licence upgrades, are able to offer Bitcoin (BTC) and Ethereum (ETH)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The SFC keeps a 'Suspicious virtual asset trading platforms' list of unlicensed entities targeting local investors, serving as an early warning to investors. (p. 88)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It serves as an early warning service to investors and increases the public's general awareness."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: HKPF and DOJ streamlined evidence gathering and prosecution against fraud syndicates using stooge accounts and set an enhanced sentencing protocol, raising sentences of stooge account holders by 20% on average. (p. 88)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "HKPF has succeeded in enhancing the sentences received by stooge account holders, on average by 20%."</sub>

## 3.3.4 Indonesia

- **descriptive / context**: Indonesia's legal review on professional reporting parties' STR obligations to PPATK identified areas requiring improvement. (p. 88)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Indonesia has identified areas where its countermeasures require improvement"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Indonesian legal review identified three areas: obstacles in reporting suspicious financial transactions by the reporting party, factors influencing optimisation of reporting obligations, and required policy directions. (p. 88)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The legal review identified: Obstacles in Reporting Suspicious Financial Transactions by the Reporting Party;"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Obstacles for professional reporting parties registering on GoAML include PPATK lacking power to revoke permits, several advocate organisations appointing advocates, no regulation for appointing financial planners, and financial planners in financial services IT form.   <sub>anchor: "PPATK cannot impose sanctions in the form of revoking the permit"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: PPATK's internal limitation is that its sanctions for advocate and notary reporting parties are limited to warnings, which lack direct deterrent effect, so stronger sanctions such as permit revocation are needed. (p. 89)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The sanctions that can be given by PPATK are limited to warning sanctions"</sub>

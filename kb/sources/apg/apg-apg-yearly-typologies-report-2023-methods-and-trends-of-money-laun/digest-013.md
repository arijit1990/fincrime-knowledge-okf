## 6.2.5 Inter-Governmental Action Group against Money Laundering in West Africa

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The GIABA 2022 assessment on beneficial ownership and asset recovery examines legal, regulatory and compliance frameworks for beneficial ownership information and related ML/TF risks in selected Member States, and reviews asset recovery and management. (p. 115)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This report examines the legal, regulatory and compliance framework for beneficial ownership information"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: GIABA concludes West African countries are vulnerable to ML and TF because they cannot identify beneficial owners of companies, trusts and other legal persons, which leads to poor investigations and rare prosecutions and convictions; the asset recovery framework is little understood and rarely used. (p. 115)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The report concludes that West African countries are vulnerable to ML and TF through the inability to identify beneficial owners"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: GIABA beneficial ownership report recommends undertaking risk assessments, developing beneficial ownership disclosure legislation and a national register, creating specialised asset management agencies with dedicated databases and centralised management of recovered assets, and engagement with training and technical assistance. (p. 116)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Developing legislation for beneficial ownership disclosure and a national register for recording same"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: GIABA 2022 typologies report on corruption identifies corruption as a major obstacle to West African economies and a major source of enrichment and money laundering, based on Member State data and stakeholder engagement. (p. 116)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This report identifies corruption as a continuing major obstacle to the emergence of West African economies"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The GIABA corruption typologies report identifies eight ML typologies with case studies: corruption as obstacle to AML/CFT; ML by PEPs; laundering proceeds of corruption by public officials; abuse of position, illicit enrichment and misappropriation; user fees for public services; corruption proceeds through legal persons and arrangements; door openers or gatekeepers; and charitable organisations. (p. 116)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Relevant data was collected from Member States and extensive engagement with public and private sector stakeholders was undertaken to identify eight ML typologies"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: The GIABA corruption report makes 15 strategic and 14 operational recommendations, highlighting legislative reform and strengthened capacity and information sharing among AML/CFT entities, FIUs and regulators. (p. 116)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "15 strategic recommendations and 14 operational recommendations are made"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The GIABA 2021 report on investigation, prosecution and adjudication of ML/TF in West Africa, produced with FATF technical assistance, found West African countries make significant efforts against safe havens but the fight is challenged by porous borders, cash economies and low digitalisation, and enforcement is not deterring. (p. 116)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "West African countries are making significant efforts to prevent their territories from becoming safe havens for criminals"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: At policy level the GIABA 2021 report found that AML/CFT frameworks do not account for individual jurisdiction risk profiles, political intrusion into law enforcement affects integrity and independence (especially with PEPs), criminal justice understanding is inadequate, inter-agency collaboration is ineffective, FIU autonomy is insufficient or not guaranteed, international cooperation and MLA are ineffective, and confiscation is not a priority. (p. 117)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AML/CFT frameworks do not take into account individual risk profiles of jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: At operational level the GIABA 2021 report found very low judicial statistics for ML/TF trials, lack of enforcement documents impeding asset recovery, practitioners unfamiliar with international cooperation, lack of technology for civil status records, judges requiring proof of predicate offending, low financial intelligence in the judiciary, and rarely run parallel financial investigations. (p. 117)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Judicial statistics for trial of ML/TF are very low and not indicative of the statistics for relevant predicate offending"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: The GIABA 2021 report makes eight recommendations, including providing resources to investigation, prosecution and adjudication entities, strengthening their capacity, improving public and judicial understanding of AML/CFT laws and evidence handling, promoting inter-agency cooperation, strengthening political commitment, prioritising understanding of ML/TF methods, strengthening regional cooperation and enhancing specialised judicial handling. (p. 118)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Provide resources and capacity to entities involved in the investigation, prosecution, and adjudication of AML/CFT cases"</sub>

## 6.2.6 Middle East and North Africa Financial Action Taskforce

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The MENAFATF Biennial Typologies Report 2022 (fifth edition) analyses ML/TF patterns from 44 case studies submitted by member countries. (p. 118)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This report analyses the most prominent patterns of ML and TF in the region based upon 44 case studies"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: MENAFATF categories with multiple cases: corruption/laundering proceeds of corruption (8); tax offences (4); drug trafficking (4); trafficking in human beings and smuggling of migrants (3); virtual currencies/assets (2); new payment methods such as T Pay (3); trade based ML (2); fake shell companies (2); parallel banking/alternative transfer/hawala (2); currency smuggling (2). (p. 118)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Corruption/Laundering the proceeds of corruption (8)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: MENAFATF single-case typologies include gambling, trade in precious stones (jewellery), pyramid scheme, social media TF fundraising with small payments to a post office account, offshore non-resident banks, IBCs and trusts, capital markets investment via intermediaries (minor children of suspect), and gold smuggling using camels. (p. 118)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Use of social media channels in TF (raising donations via social media"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: The MENAFATF report includes data on entities exploited and number of cases under investigation, referred for prosecution or before courts, and extracts a list of 30 important suspicious activity indicators from the case analysis. (p. 118)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A list of 30 important indicators of suspicious activity are extracted from the case analysis"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The MENAFATF November 2022 NPO typologies project aimed to understand the risk of NPO abuse for TF and identify best practices to address abuse without interfering with legitimate NPO activities. (p. 118)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to identify best practices to address such abuse without interfering with NPOs’ legitimate activities"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: The MENAFATF NPO project used two questionnaires sent to public sector (FIUs, LEAs, NPO regulators), private sector and NPOs, plus case studies and open source research. (p. 118)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "MENAFATF secretariat issued two questionnaires to collect information from the public sector"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The MENAFATF NPO report identifies two-fold ML/TF risks: exploitation of legitimate NPOs and creation of fictitious NPOs. (p. 118)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The ML/TF risks to the NPO sector are two-fold."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: NPO case studies include a TF organisation member placed on an NPO board of trustees, attempts to open bank accounts for fake NPOs, and a children's welfare charity whose funds were diverted to the association's president while children were ill-treated, economically exploited and indoctrinated with extremist ideas. (p. 119)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Case studies detailed within the report included the placement of a member of a TF organisation on a NPO board of trustees"</sub>

## 6.2.7 Egmont Group

- **descriptive / context**: The Egmont Group July 2022 report explores FinTech and associated threats and vulnerabilities in new payment services and products. (p. 119)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Egmont Group published a report in July 2022 exploring the world of financial technology"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: FinTech is loosely defined as computer programs and technology supporting banking and financial services; examples include internet and mobile banking, digital currencies, money transfer platforms, robo-technology non face-to-face investments, crowdfunding platforms and VASPs. (p. 119)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FinTech is a loosely defined term but includes computer programs and other technology"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Egmont report finds FIUs poorly understand FinTech mechanics and ML/TF threats from exploitation are not fully appreciated. (p. 119)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the mechanics behind FinTech products and services is little understood by FIUs"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report notes Recommendation 15 requires jurisdictions to address risks from new and emerging technologies, but not all FinTech entities are reporting entities for regulation. (p. 119)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Whilst Recommendation 15 requires jurisdictions to address risks arising from new and emerging technologies"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Egmont FinTech project aimed to understand how FinTech entities cooperate with FIUs, explore their regulatory environment, and define potential best practices for engaging the FinTech sector; it received input from 41 members. (p. 119)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The project group received input from 41 members across the globe"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Sixteen case studies show variable ways FinTech can be exploited, challenges for FIUs in understanding data and tracing information where FinTechs are incorporated abroad; the most reported typologies involved fraud and virtual assets and bank accounts held by shell companies. (p. 119)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Sixteen case studies highlight the variable ways in which the FinTech sector can be exploited"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Egmont report concludes regulation of VASPs and blockchain is not yet in place in many jurisdictions, classification of services varies, and this complicates tracing international financial flows. (p. 119)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "regulation of VASPs and blockchain technology is not yet in place in many jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: The Egmont report recommends that financial analysts maintain a baseline understanding of FinTech mechanics and how to interpret the data in financial intelligence reports, and recommends spontaneous streamlined information dissemination between FIUs. (p. 120)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The report recommends that financial analysts maintain a baseline understanding of the mechanics behind FinTech products"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: To ensure appropriate mitigation of risk in the FinTech sector, FIUs need to agree on a standard reporting format in partnership with FinTech entities and each other. (p. 120)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIUs need to agree on a standard reporting format in partnership with FinTech entities and each other"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Egmont IEWG report on OSINT use by FIUs applies thematic and descriptive statistical analyses to data from sixty-one FIUs, covering OSINT use, compilation, types, reliability methods and dedicated teams and tools. (p. 120)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The report applies thematic and descriptive statistical analyses to qualitative and quantitative data collected from sixty-one FIUs"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The OSINT report concludes OSINT is valuable for developing typologies, identifying macro-level ML/TF trends and linking individuals and legal persons, links not otherwise available from suspicious transaction reports. (p. 120)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The report concludes that OSINT is a valuable resource for development of typologies"</sub>

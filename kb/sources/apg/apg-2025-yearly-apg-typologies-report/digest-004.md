## 2.11 New Zealand

- **descriptive / case example**: Person A is a business associate of sanctioned oligarch Person B, listed on 20 April 2022; Person B owns Group A, and Person A's client is a real estate subsidiary of Group A, which is held in a company 50% or more owned by Person B, deeming Person A an associate. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Person A is a business associate of a prominent foreign oligarch (Person B)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The investigation found Person B likely is the true owner of Company C, which holds 25.9% of Company B, so that Person B would effectively own 74.9% if evidence proves it. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Person B effectively owns 74.9% of Company B"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The case shows trusts can be misused by sanctioned entities to obscure beneficial ownership, and that the main TCSP risk lies in how thoroughly it checks the people involved in a proposed New Zealand company under KYC standards. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This case reinforces how trusts can be exploited/misused by sanctioned entities"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: KYC standards are set out in the AML/CFT Act 2009; the report cites Mossack Fonseca and the Panama Papers as an example of an actively deceptive TCSP. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The standards for KYC are set out in the _Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism (AML/CFT) Act 2009_"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: TCSPs can also be negligent, for example by failing to assess client risks or apply AML/CFT Act standards, which poses a comparable threat to the TCSP and to New Zealand. (p. 36)[^original]

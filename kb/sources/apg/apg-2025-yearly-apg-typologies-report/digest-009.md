## 3.2.9 Vietnam

- **descriptive / context**: Vietnam reports that online fraud is the most prominent cybercrime, accounting for 57% of all cybercrimes, and that such crimes are growing in scope and scale, exploiting technologies such as AI and causing losses of thousands of billions of USD annually. (p. 69)[^original]

- **descriptive / context**: Vietnam states that there are currently no sufficient legal regulations governing the trading and use of virtual currencies and cryptocurrencies, which have become increasingly complex and difficult to control. (p. 69)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there are currently no sufficient legal regulations in place to govern these activities"</sub>

## 3.3.1 Hong Kong, China

- **descriptive / finding or result**: VA-related crime cases declined by 902 in 2024, which is attributed to police efforts and the introduction of licensing requirements for Virtual Asset Trading Platforms (VATPs); legislation regulating other VA-related activities is underway. (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "with VA-related crime cases dropping by 902"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: HKMA, HKPF and the Hong Kong Association of Banks introduced new measures against financial crime including fraud and associated mule account networks; ten banks share information on the FINEST platform operated by HKPF about corporates suspected of fraudulent or illegal activities. (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Currently, ten banks are already sharing information on the Financial Intelligence Evaluation Sharing Tool (FINEST) platform"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Legislative amendment to allow sharing of personal account information when banks become aware of suspicious activity possibly indicating prohibited conduct, including ML and TF, is being introduced in the Legislative Council, with the result expected at the end of 2025. (p. 70)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "legislative amendment to enable sharing of information of personal accounts when banks become aware of suspicious activity"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Suspicious Account Alert mechanism, linked to Scameter, sends a high-risk alert to the customer before remittance if the recipient account was previously reported in a crime case; it now extends beyond the Faster Payment System to internet banking, branches and ATMs and covers the majority of the public's day-to-day transfers. (p. 71)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "If the recipient’s account number has previously been reported in a crime case, the system will send a high-risk alert"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Anti-Deception Alliance, a co-located police-bank arrangement, monitors transactions (e.g. payments to identified stooge accounts) and alerts potential victims; 3,051 ongoing deception cases had been successfully intervened in by the end of 2024. (p. 71)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A total of 3,051 ongoing deception cases had been intervened successfully by the end of 2024."</sub>

## 3.3.4 Macao, China

- **descriptive / context**: Insurance Intermediary Business Ordinance Law no. 15/2024, approved in July 2024, enters into force on 1 August 2025 and replaces Decree-Law no. 38/89/M; it adds ML/TF conviction as a fit-and-proper consideration to strengthen licensing and ongoing supervision of insurance intermediaries. (p. 72)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "incorporating the ML/TF conviction as a consideration factor"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Law no. 7/2024 revokes Law no. 5/2004 and restricts credit granting to land-based casino patrons to casino concessionaires; junket operators may only grant credit under a representation contract acting on behalf of concessionaires; new penalties and criminal liability of legal persons are introduced. (p. 73)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "junket operators are not authorised to grant credit directly to the patrons"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Law no. 20/2024 revokes several earlier laws and provisions on illicit gambling, increases penalties for related crimes and infractions, and introduces new crimes including illicit online gambling and non-authorised foreign exchange for gambling, with criminal liability of legal persons. (p. 73)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Law no. 20/2024 increases the penalties on several crimes and administrative infractions associated with illicit gambling"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Law no. 20/2024 gives legal status to informants on the defined crimes and provides precautionary measures for suspects. (p. 73)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it introduces the legal status of the informants on the types of crime defined in Law no. 20/2024"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Financial Services Bureau amended the AML/CFT Guidelines in October 2024, effective 1 November 2024, to define beneficial owners of legal persons and legal arrangements and to require verification of beneficial owner identity. (p. 73)[^original]

## 3.3.5 Malaysia

- **descriptive / context**: Malaysia endorsed the NCC Roadmap 2024-26 with measurable targets, focusing on deterrence of financial crime, compliance culture among reporting institutions, understanding emerging risks, and awareness and capacity building; it incorporates recommendations from the NRA 2023 and five thematic risk assessments. (p. 73)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The new Roadmap also incorporates key recommendations from the NRA 2023 and five thematic risk assessments"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The National Fraud Portal went live in April 2024 to support the National Scam Response Centre, which receives victim fraud reports and uses the portal to trace fraud fund flows, share information among financial institutions and monitor their interventions. (p. 74)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The National Fraud Portal (NFP) went live in April 2024"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Through the National Fraud Portal, mule accounts identified by the NSRC increased by 65%, time to obtain complete fund flow information fell by 75%, cases escalated to the Royal Malaysian Police rose by 41%, and the average monthly earmarked sum of fraud cases rose by 47%. (p. 74)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "shown by a 65% increase in the number of mule accounts identified by the NSRC"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: NSRC 2024 outcomes: 9,588 investigation papers opened involving cheating and freeze orders on targeted collection accounts totalling RM8 million (about USD 1.8 million); 71,037 suspected mule accounts identified and disrupted; 95,449 calls on online scams received, of which 40,010 came from scam victims. (p. 74)[^original]

## 3.3.6 Singapore

- **descriptive / context**: Singapore's STRO analysis in March 2024 of suspected money mules linked to scams and unlicensed moneylending uncovered a larger syndicate: two common foreign phone numbers were registered by 89 unrelated bank account holders, and further accounts linked through three residential addresses, held by about 100 foreign workers who did not live there, mostly opened in 2022 and 2023. (p. 74)[^original]

## Case Study # 93: Case involving recruiter of money mule bank accounts

- **descriptive / case example**: STRO suspected a recruiter registered bank accounts for third parties and then took control of them; the addresses likely managed hard-copy bank correspondence. Transaction patterns resembled money mule and unlicensed moneylending activity; the individual was convicted in March 2024 under the Moneylenders Act 2008, sentenced to two months' imprisonment and a SGD 30,000 fine, with CDSA offences taken into consideration. (p. 74)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the said individual was convicted and sentenced in March 2024 to two months’ imprisonment and a fine of SGD 30,000"</sub>

## Case Study # 94: Case involving fraudulent proceeds from overseas seized in Singapore

- **descriptive / case example**: In 2024 STRO received information that about EUR 29 million of suspected fraud proceeds was transferred from two Jurisdiction B companies to a Singapore payment account; the owners were alleged masterminds of an online investment platform that defrauded victims of about EUR 100 million, and the platform closed in September 2024. (p. 75)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "suspected fraudulent proceeds of about EUR 29 million (~ USD 34 million) had been transferred"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: STRO disseminated the matter to the CAD expeditiously and engaged FIU counterparts in Jurisdictions A and B; the CAD seized about EUR 11 million in the payment account in November 2024, with the remaining funds moved out of Singapore before STRO received information; investigations are ongoing. (p. 75)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "subsequently seized funds of approximately EUR 11 million (~ USD 12.9 million)"</sub>

## Case Study # 95: Spontaneous sharing with positive outcomes

- **descriptive / case example**: In November 2023 STRO received a Request for Assistance from a North American FIU about a person suspected of fraud who obtained public loans of about USD 2.3 million using fraudulent documents; STRO proactively alerted that FIU to possible proceeds in Singapore, with consent disseminated them to law enforcement, leading to seizure of an investment account holding about SGD 1 million. (p. 75)[^original]

## Legislative Developments

- **descriptive / context**: The Money Laundering Control Act was comprehensively amended in 2024: Article 6 extends criminal liability to virtual asset service providers and third-party payment providers that fail to complete required AML registrations, and Article 11 regulates fiduciary relationships; Article 6 and 11 took effect on 30 November 2024, other articles on 2 August 2024. (p. 77)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Both Article 6 and Article 11 came into force on 30 November 2024"</sub>

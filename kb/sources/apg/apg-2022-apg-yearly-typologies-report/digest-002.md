## 1.3 Law enforcement responses to addressing IUU fishing

- **descriptive / context**: Sri Lanka reported an observed increase in IUU activity since the onset of the Covid-19 pandemic; in July 2020 its Navy reported 14 IUU fishing cases, all involving illegal fishing with prohibited gear. (p. 11)[^original]

- **descriptive / finding or result**: A 2017 APG and UNODC research report found insufficient financial investigations into wildlife crime and few attempts by jurisdictions to follow the money trail. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there are insufficient financial investigations conducted into wildlife crime and few attempts by jurisdictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report (citing Haenlein/RUSI) states that AML legislation in many jurisdictions does not include IUU as a predicate offence for ML, and says this likely contributes to a lack of financial investigations into illicit financial flows generated from IUU. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AML legislation in many jurisdictions does not include IUU as a predicate offence for ML.24"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report says the lack of parallel financial investigations into IUU proceeds is likely attributable to the limited information available on the nature and dynamics of illicit financial flows generated from IUU. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "limited amount of information available on the nature and dynamics of illicit financial flows generated from IUU"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Solomon Islands 2017: Maritime Police apprehended three 'blue boats' from Jurisdiction A that illegally entered the EEZ to harvest sea cucumber and giant clams using prohibited gear such as diving compressor hoses; the crew tried to escape and police used tear gas, rubber bullets and ramming. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the police using tear gas, rubber bullets and ramming the vessel"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The court said financial prospects motivated the defendants, noted overfishing in their own waters, and stressed a deterrent penalty because the Solomon Islands seem viewed as a soft target. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the court also emphasised the need for a deterrent penalty"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Solomon Islands authorities cited challenges in pursuing proceeds of crime and ML: too few experienced investigators; limited technical knowledge on ML for environmental crimes compared with immigration, fisheries and penal code offences; low judicial familiarity with AML legislation, meaning few parallel ML investigations; and high costs of detaining persons of interest during investigations. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Lack of experienced investigators to carry out investigations on Proceeds of Crime"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Solomon Islands case: defendants claimed to be victims of human trafficking for forced labour; the court found insufficient evidence. They pleaded guilty to four counts under the Fisheries Management Act of 2015 and Fisheries (Amendment) Regulations of 2009. On 14 July 2017 they were sentenced to two years imprisonment and fines of SBD 11,050,000 (approx. USD 1.4 million), payable within thirty days, with a further two-year default imprisonment running consecutively if unpaid. The three vessels were forfeited to the Solomon Islands Government and destroyed. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "sentenced to two years imprisonment and fines amounting to SBD 11,050,000"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: RUSI's 2019 report stressed urgent need for dedicated research into IUU illicit financial flows and laundering means; the APG says jurisdictions need to prioritise research into how IUU proceeds are integrated into the legitimate economy, and that cross-agency collaboration is critical alongside financial investigative techniques. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions need to prioritise research into how the illicit proceeds from IUU are integrated"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Thailand: in February 2022 the Royal Thai Police set up the 'IUU Hunter' taskforce to investigate fishing business operations in 22 coastal provinces, seeking illegal businesses and labour exploitation or trafficking syndicates, working with the Department of Fisheries, Marine Department and Department of Labour Protection and Welfare. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Royal Thai Police established a special taskforce, ‘IUU Hunter’"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Research suggests IUU proceeds may be invested in new fishing gear, processing facilities or vessels, and laundered through fish sales at port or cash payment of crew. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may be invested in new fishing gear, fish processing facilities or vessels"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Some IUU criminal organisations are thought to launder profits through complex ownership structures with multiple front companies in different jurisdictions; shell or front companies, often in secrecy jurisdictions or tax havens, hide vessels' beneficial owners and make following the money harder.   <sub>anchor: "complex corporate ownership structures involving multiple front companies in different jurisdictions"</sub>

AMLO with the Royal Thai Police and other agencies froze and seized proceeds of crime from seven entities, worth more than one million baht (approx. - **descriptive / context**: Fishing corporations often face supply chain legality obligations by law or voluntary codes; steps such as tracing proceeds and mapping actors parallel those of LEAs. Fishing companies are subject to government supply chain transparency regulations and voluntary private initiatives; Thailand and the United States have introduced government initiatives. ## 1.4 Supply Chain Obligations on the Private Sector

- **descriptive / context**: Thailand has a sea-to-plate traceability system (Thai Flagged Catch Certification Scheme and Import Control Scheme) covering each production stage, with cross-checking and surprise inspections; certified export consignments can be traced to the vessel and batch. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Thailand has established a traceability system covering the whole supply chain from sea to plate"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The US Seafood Import Monitoring Program (SIMP) is a risk-based traceability program requiring importers to report key data from harvest to US entry on more than 1,100 species and document chain of custody; importers must retain the information for two years and may face random audits and inspections. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "requires importers to provide and report key data from the point of harvest"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Private transparency initiatives include the Marine Stewardship Council ecolabel (needs chain-of-custody certificate), SeaBOS and GDST. They offer collaboration opportunities between LEAs and fishing companies, and financial institutions and DNFBPs could use their information to identify IUU risks and improve reporting to FIUs. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "scope for financial gatekeepers (financial institutions and DNFBPs) to use the information available"</sub>

## 1.5 Conclusion

- **descriptive / finding or result**: IUU has conventionally been treated as a fisheries management problem; Asia Pacific jurisdictions largely commit to RFMOs but report challenges mounting an effective, proportionate law enforcement response at current scale. It is arguable IUU is not yet prioritised as a criminal justice matter; characterising it as an ML predicate may trigger effective AML/CFT responses. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A shift in characterising IUU as a predicate crime type for ML may trigger"</sub>

## 3.1 Typologies Projects

- **descriptive / method**: In October 2021 the APG Operations Committee approved a typologies project on illicit financial flows from illegal fishing, using information from members, observers and select private bodies and case studies on investigations and prosecutions, to clarify how proceeds are integrated into the legitimate economy and the channels and actors used. Outputs: a report, a roundtable at the 2022 Annual Meeting, and a focus stream at the next Annual Typologies Workshop. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "understand and investigate the nature and dynamics of the illicit financial flows generated from illegal fishing"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: In October 2021 the Operations Committee approved a tax crimes typologies project to collate member typologies, ML and taskforce experiences, and seizure and confiscation statistics, and explore supervisors' role and risk-based supervision of complex legal structures, shell companies, secrecy jurisdictions, gatekeepers and facilitators. The report will cover national tax crime frameworks, risk context, corporate vehicles and roles of competent authorities. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "collate tax crime typologies observed by members, experiences of money laundering and taskforce investigations"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The illegal fishing illicit financial flows project team includes Germany (Ministry of Finance), Saudi Arabia (Islamic Development Bank Group), UNODC, Fiji, United States, AFP advisor to PTCCC, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, New Zealand Customs, Pacific Islands Forum Fisheries Agency and Oceania Customs Organisation Secretariat. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Members and Observers of the project team include Germany, Ministry of Finance"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Tax crimes deliverables will support Australian initiatives such as MAP-TNC and OECD capacity building including the OECD Tax Crime Academy in Japan and Tax Inspectors Without Borders criminal investigation pilot. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "OECD Tax Crime Academy (located in Japan)"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: A project with the Global Center on Cooperative Security, approved October 2021, scopes APG members' implementation of FATF Recommendation 8 and Immediate Outcome 10 on preventing NPO abuse for terrorism financing, via mutual evaluation review, survey, consultations and roundtable, to identify where guidance is needed while avoiding unintended consequences on civic space, human rights and NPO operations and financial access.

## The Uses of Nominee Arrangements: The Good, the Bad, and the Ugly

- **descriptive / definition**: Nominee directors used only for concealment are not expected to offer technical expertise or have a substantive relationship with or knowledge of the beneficial owner; they rent their identity and serve as legal officeholders. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Nominee directors employed only for concealment are not expected to offer technical expertise or have any substantive relationship"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Panama Papers (2016) revealed that Leticia Montoya, a Mossack Fonseca employee, was nominee director for tens of thousands of corporations. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they revealed that Leticia Montoya—a Panamanian employee of Mossack Fonseca—served as the nominee director for"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Montoya was paid a yearly salary of $6,000 while Mossack Fonseca charged clients $150 annually for a nominee director; she had no ties to beneficial owners or managerial experience. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "She was paid a yearly salary of $6,000, while Mossack Fonseca charged clients $150 annually for a nominee director."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A brief OpenCorporates search suggested Montoya was still listed as an active manager of more than 16,000 Panama companies six years after the Panama Papers release. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "continues to be listed as an active member of management for more than 16,000 companies in Panama six years after"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Sham directors also sign documents such as bank account opening forms or annual general meeting minutes. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In addition to these three documents, sham directors sign papers such as the forms required to open a bank account"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Nominees using their names on many unrelated companies create false trails linking companies that share only the nominee's name, hindering investigations. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Nominees selling their services and using their names on numerous companies create false trails connecting companies"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Nominee directors may be used so a company is legally resident in a jurisdiction for tax purposes; under the English test, tax liability follows the location of 'mind and management', unlike US law which focuses on the place of incorporation. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "rather than just the jurisdiction of incorporation (unlike US law, which is more interested in the jurisdiction of incorporation)."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The authors quote Brinkmann, Obermaier and Obermayer (journalists) on a standard shell-company set-up in which sham directors sign three initial documents; the first is a waiver of claims against the true owners or their companies. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The first is a waiver declaring they won’t pursue claims against the true owners or their companies."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The authors say they have themselves formed shell companies using this form; the second document in it is a power of attorney through which the sham director hands control of the company to the true owner. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The authors have themselves formed shell companies using this form:"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Gibraltar allowed local nominee directors and shareholders to create a local tax presence even though a company's income and business were elsewhere. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Gibraltar was another such jurisdiction where local nominee directors and shareholders could be used"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: An author formed an English shell company with a CSP-provided nominee director, with a pre-signed undated resignation letter and power of attorney, plus reciprocal indemnities; the nominee's role was to hide the owner's control. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "complete with a pre-signed but undated letter of resignation and a power of attorney agreement."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The author also formed a Nevada LLC with a nominee director in Panama City, solely to obscure the beneficial owner's identity. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The author also formed a Nevada LLC with a nominee director in Panama City, Panama"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: A Canadian nominee was paid $100 per directorship for 200 companies, some involved in criminal activity worth over $100 million, and described her role as only signing papers received from lawyers. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a Canadian nominee was paid $100 for each directorship of 200 companies"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Historically, residents of UK crown dependencies such as Sark may have acted as nominee directors for hundreds of companies; after an unfavorable 1999 court decision hindered the practice, some of Sark’s nominee directors relocated offshore. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "After an unfavorable court decision in 1999 hindered the practice, some of Sark’s nominee directors relocated"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The same Canadian nominee signed blank, undated powers of attorney allowing unknown parties to act in her name without her knowledge or consent. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The same nominee then signed blank, undated power of attorney forms"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The authors note that such lawyer-organised arrangements were arguably covered by legal professional privilege even though the nominee was not a lawyer. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Given that these arrangements were organized by lawyers, they were arguably covered by legal professional privilege"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: GT Group founder Geoffrey Taylor marketed nominee services as a front man who could avoid suspicion, even offering a 'Lord Stubbington' name. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "He can act as Director and Shareholder for clients without arousing suspicion that he is a nominee only."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Other nominee directors were infants, dead persons or identity-theft victims; a deceased Russian man was shown signing a UK shell company document in 2008 three years after his death, and the CSP declined responsibility. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "apparently signed documents for the creation of a UK shell company on April 23, 2008"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Nigerian governor James Ibori listed his four-month-old child as director of one of his companies, Saagaris Properties Ltd. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "listed his four-month-old child as the director of one of his Nigerian companies"</sub>

## The Risks of Nominee Directorships for Nominees and Owners

- **descriptive / context**: The question raised is why nominee directors who misuse the role are not sanctioned, answered by a Global Witness 2012 report on UK shell company abuse. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "If nominee directors fail to follow the law on discharging their responsibilities as directors, why are they not sanctioned?"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In practice UK nominee directors neglect duties such as filing accounts and dormancy certification despite tens of millions of pounds passing through accounts, with little risk of punishment; the impunity is greater for non-UK residents. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "nominee directors of UK companies can neglect their duties in terms of failing to submit accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / requirement**: A director's responsibilities cannot be legally transferred, regardless of any private agreement with the beneficial owner. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Regardless of whether the nominees have set up a private legal agreement with the beneficial owners, a director’s responsibilities cannot be legally transferred."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Owing to long-running lack of UK enforcement, signature-for-sale nominee directors of UK companies remain common, especially where nominees are outside the UK and beyond UK enforcement reach. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "owing to a long-running lack of enforcement by UK authorities, signature for sale nominee directors of UK companies remain common"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The risks posed by nominees to their principals may be greater than sanctions risks to nominees; a nominee director can exercise legal powers, and consumer protection is limited. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the risks posed by these nominees for their principals may actually be greater."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Nominees and proxies used by Ferdinand Marcos to hold Swiss assets reportedly took control of many of them after his death, against family wishes. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "apparently took control of many of these assets after his death, in defiance of the wishes of the remaining family members"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: There is a basic trade-off between secrecy and control: more secrecy means less control. Pre-signed undated resignation letters reduce but do not eliminate this risk. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "more secrecy involves less control, and vice versa."</sub>

## Nominee Shareholders

- **descriptive / definition**: A nominee shareholder is a named shareholder acting for a principal who exercises real control and receives the economic benefit. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A nominee shareholder serves as a named shareholder for a company at the behest of another individual"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Legitimate use of nominee shareholders is mainly registering shares in a broker's name to make trade clearance and settlement efficient, mostly for publicly listed companies; politicians may also place private assets in trust to limit conflicts of interest. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The primary legitimate use of nominee shareholders centers on registering shares in the name of a stockbroker"</sub>

## The Risks of Nominee Directorships for Nominees and Owners

- **descriptive / finding or result**: On the rare occasions nominee directors are questioned, they tend to make the legally false argument that, being only nominees, they have no responsibility to know about the company or control its actions. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they have no responsibility to know anything about the company, let alone control its actions."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: A lawyer who had served as a nominee justified the role by describing an owner's mental breakdown, during which the nominee ignored harmful instructions; the authors note the lawyer did not consider the reverse scenario, where the owner would face a costly legal battle to reverse the nominee's decisions. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "At the very least, the owner would have faced a long, difficult, and expensive legal battle to reverse these decisions."</sub>

## Nominee Shareholders

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Nominee shareholders can screen the real owner: in Australia the nominee's name appears on the publicly available shareholder register while the beneficial owner's name does not. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "in Australia the nominee’s name appears on the publicly available shareholder register"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Nominee shareholders have commonly had no responsibility to declare on whose behalf they hold the shares. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Nominee shareholders commonly have had no responsibility to declare on whose behalf they are holding the shares."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The effect of public beneficial ownership registers on nominee shareholder use in private shells is unclear; a Cypriot nominee on the UK PSC register questions their practical effectiveness. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "brings into question the practical effectiveness of this measure."</sub>

## Corporate Directors and Corporate Shareholders

- **descriptive / context**: The report limits its coverage of corporate directors and shareholders to shell companies and beneficial ownership transparency, and notes they are sometimes treated as forms of nominee arrangements. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this report restricts coverage of corporate directors and corporate shareholders to shell companies"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Sanctions against corporate shareholders such as shell companies or trusts are much harder than against a real-person nominee shareholder. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "sanctions are much more difficult when the shareholder in question is merely another shell company or a trust."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Practical reasons for corporate directors include availability: a corporate director lets one of several authorised individuals sign decisions; CSPs often keep an in-house shell as director for many companies. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Corporate service providers often have an in-house shell company that may serve as corporate director"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: A Hong Kong provider suggested registering a BVI company as shareholder of a HK company to hide ownership, since HK director and shareholder details are public. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "You may consider to register another offshore company, like a BVI company, and use it to act as the Shareholder"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Circular ownership (A owns B, B owns C, C owns A) can leave no real beneficial owner, and may be illegal in some jurisdictions. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In these circumstances (which may be illegal in some jurisdictions), there is no real beneficial owner"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Layering with corporate trustees, trust-owned companies and civil law foundations creates complex multi-jurisdictional webs that pose serious investigative challenges. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This kind of layering creates long, complex webs of a mix of interlocking corporate entities"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Mukhtar Ablyazov, targeted by Kazakhstan over alleged fraud linked to BTA Bank, was traced through several thousand linked entities and legal actions in multiple jurisdictions. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Following the assets in this case necessitated investigating corporate structures composed of several thousand linked corporate entities"</sub>

## Power of Attorney

- **descriptive / definition**: A power of attorney gives a third party legal authority to act for another within a defined scope; when held by a lawyer it may be covered by legal professional privilege. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A power of attorney allows a third party the legal prerogatives to act on behalf of another within a defined scope of activities."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Powers of attorney have well-known legitimate uses, such as absence, incapacity, or property and financial matters. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A power of attorney is granted in a number of instances in which one person is not able to make legal decisions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In mystery shopping, many countries, especially Eastern Europe, required foreign owners to grant the local CSP power of attorney, presented as a legal requirement rather than an identity-concealment measure. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Especially in Eastern Europe, this approach was presented as a requirement of local laws and regulations"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Powers of attorney can be tailored broad or narrow, are rarely referenced in registers or company documents, and are often used with nominee and corporate officeholder arrangements to keep control with the owner. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "As a private legal arrangement, it will rarely if ever be referenced in share registries or company documents."</sub>

## The GT Group in New Zealand and Tightening of the Law on Directors

- **descriptive / case example**: GT Group, founded by Geoffrey Taylor in 1995, repeatedly appeared in scandals including illegal arms deals, drug smuggling, grand corruption and tax evasion. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Geoffrey Taylor founded GT Group in 1995 (OCCRP 2011)."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In December 2009 a plane was detained in Bangkok en route from North Korea to Iran, listed as carrying oil drilling equipment but found to contain explosives, rocket-propelled grenades and surface-to-air missile materials. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Thai authorities discovered explosives, rocket-propelled grenades, and materials for the construction of surface-to-air missiles"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The plane, once owned by arms dealer Viktor Bout, was leased by SP Trading, whose sole director was Lu Zhang, a recent Chinese immigrant serving as a GT Group nominee for $15 per signature. (p. 14)[^original]

## The GT Group in New Zealand and Tightening of the Law on Directors

- **descriptive / finding or result**: No one from GT Group or the Taylor family was charged for hiding arms trafficking identities, shell companies for the Sinaloa cartel or the Magnitsky case; nominee services were legal in New Zealand then. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "No one in the GT Group or any member of the Taylor family faced charges for their roles"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In November 2010 Lu Zhang was convicted of 74 counts of making false statements on company registration forms, with no penalty imposed. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Lu Zhang herself was convicted of 74 counts of making false statements on company registration forms"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: New Zealand reformed company law in 2014 after the scandal and after the EU removed it from its whitelist of equivalent AML countries in 2011; every new and existing company must have a resident real-individual director registered with name and date of birth. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The amendments mandated that every new and existing New Zealand company have a director who was a local resident real individual"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In 2018 the authors found foreign nominee and corporate directors unavailable (except Australian residents) and local CSPs unwilling to act as nominees due to liability under the amended laws. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "foreign nominee and corporate directors were unavailable (with the limited exception of Australian residents)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Corporate service providers were brought within the AML reporting regime, and a 17-member Integrity and Enforcement Team was set up to check CSP compliance and investigate suspicious activity. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the government set up a 17-member Integrity and Enforcement Team"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Use of NZ shell companies in international crime seems to have fallen significantly after the reforms, which affected under 1 percent of the 550,000 companies on the register. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "even though the amendments affected less than 1 percent of the 550,000 companies on the register"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Lu Zhang, SP Trading's sole director, was an unwitting participant in the international arms trade; she had recently taken part-time work with the Taylors of GT Group as a nominee director. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "she was an unwitting participant in the international arms trade"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The GT Group charged $15 per signature to place Lu Zhang's name on company documents, obscuring who was really in control of this and other shell companies. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "For the rate of $15 per signature, the GT Group placed Lu Zhang’s name on company documents"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Vicam (Auckland) Limited, SP Trading's corporate shareholder, had Nesita Manceau as its named director by 2009; in July 2010 Manceau was made its sole shareholder, giving her the dual role of nominee shareholder and nominee director. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "giving Manceau the dual role of nominee shareholder and nominee director"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A brief review suggests Nesita Manceau has been a professional nominee director for the GT Group, with OpenCorporates listing her as a director of more than 400 New Zealand companies. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "her name was listed on OpenCorporates as a director of more than 400 New Zealand companies"</sub>

## The Beirut Blast, Cypriot Nominees, and the UK Beneficial Ownership Register

- **descriptive / case example**: The 4 August 2020 Beirut explosion source was ammonium nitrate stored at the port since October 2013, confiscated from the MV Rhosus; a group of shell companies with nominee-obscured owners was central to the affair. (p. 16)[^original]

- **descriptive / case example**: The bill of lading named Rustavi Azot as shipper and the International Bank of Mozambique as consignee for Fábrica de Explosivos de Moçambique, but the explosives company said it ordered from UK go-between Savaro Limited. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it had actually ordered the ammonium nitrate not from the manufacturer Rustavi Azot, but from a go-between company"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Savaro Limited is listed on the UK PSC register as owned by Marina Psyllou through Status Grand Limited; Interstatus Limited, another Psyllou company, is its secretary and is owned by Cypriot Interstatus Business Services. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Psyllou owns Savaro through being the sole shareholder for the company Status Grand Limited"</sub>

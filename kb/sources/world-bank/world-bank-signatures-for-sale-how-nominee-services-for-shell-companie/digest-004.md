## Comparisons With Mutual Evaluation Reviews

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Evaluations suggest nominee arrangements are a messy cluster of related services rather than a clear-cut discrete category. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The evaluations tend to confirm the idea that nominee arrangements are a messy cluster of related services, rather than a clear-cut discrete category."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The UK Companies Act 2006 defines a shadow director as a person in accordance with whose directions or instructions the company's directors are accustomed to act; being a shadow director depends on behaviour rather than a formal position or a private agreement such as a power of attorney, and individuals can become one unwittingly. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "“a person in accordance with whose directions or instructions the directors of the company are accustomed to act.”"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The UK evaluation states that although UK law has no formal provision for nominee directors, in practice they exist, a point supported by research. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "although there is no formal provision for nominee directors under UK law, in practice they exist"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The US evaluation observes that no state expressly permits corporations to use nominee directors and there is no express bar against them; the mystery shopping shows nominee directors are readily available and marketed to hide the beneficial owner, and many intermediaries do not identify clients. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "No State expressly permits corporations to use nominee directors; neither is there an express bar against them.”"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A nominee director remains a director with the same duties and liabilities, including in informal shadow director cases; the Hong Kong SAR China evaluation states nominee directors are treated as directors in law. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "“[N]ominee directors are treated as directors in law’"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The US evaluation quoted in the report finds no licensing requirements for nominee directors or nominee shareholders, no requirement for them to disclose the identity of the nominator, and no other mechanisms to ensure compliance. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "nominee directors/nominee shareholders or requirements for them to disclose the identity of nominator"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Mystery shopping found that nominee directors are readily available in practice and are explicitly marketed as a way to hide the beneficial owner. Many intermediaries offering such services do not identify their clients. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they are explicitly marketed as a way to hide the beneficial owner"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The bigger problem, illustrated by the Beirut blast example, is Cypriot providers acting as nominees for foreign companies, not only for local companies as the MONEYVAL evaluation focuses on. Such problems fall between different countries' regulatory regimes. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the bigger problem, epitomized by the Beirut blast example discussed earlier, is when Cypriot providers act as nominees for"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The New Zealand review states nominee directors have the same duties as ordinary directors, including good faith and acting in the company's best interests, and a duty of care; a person who appoints and directs a nominee director would likely also be treated as a director, though not explicit. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Although not explicit, a person who appoints and directs a nominee director would also likely be treated as a director under New Zealand law"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The evaluations note legitimate uses of nominee arrangements, such as stockbrokers and nominee shareholding; the Australia evaluation says nominee companies help investors maintain public anonymity and give flexibility in investment options. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "nominee companies play an important role for [stock market] investors in helping them to maintain a level of public anonymity"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The MONEYVAL evaluation of Cyprus identifies international banking and then the CSP sector as major money laundering risks, and the nominee services offered by Cypriot providers are borne out by the mystery shopping; the focus, however, is on Cypriot providers acting for local companies rather than foreign ones. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "seems to be accurate in diagnosing first the international banking but then the CSP sector as the major money laundering risk."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Hong Kong SAR China evaluation says nominee shareholders must be disregarded and the beneficial owner entered on the Significant Controllers Register, but a Hong Kong provider suggested the measure could be defeated relatively easily by holding ownership through a second British Virgin Islands company. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The evaluation notes approvingly that nominee shareholders must be disregarded and the beneficial owner entered on the Significant Controllers Register"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The 2019 UK evaluation concludes that risks posed by nominee shareholders are largely mitigated; the report argues this may give false security because the bigger problem is UK shell companies with nominees supplied by foreign CSPs beyond UK authorities' reach. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "“risks posed by nominee shareholders are largely mitigated”"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The 2018 APG follow-up report on Vanuatu finds Vanuatu notably more compliant with international beneficial ownership rules than the United States or Australia, yet shows little thought given to multijurisdictional arrangements; a Vanuatu-Australian combined arrangement is unlikely to be caught under Vanuatu's local nominee rules. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Vanuatu is notably more compliant with international beneficial ownership rules than the United States or Australia."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Evaluations by international organisations focus on domestic legal entities and their enforcement, without considering foreign-origin vulnerabilities, even though money laundering and legitimate finance are increasingly cross-border. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "evaluations by international organizations focus exclusively on the characteristics of domestic legal entities and their enforcement"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Evaluations tend to assume CSPs establish and manage local companies even for foreign beneficial owners, so a provider in jurisdiction A selling a company from jurisdiction B to a customer in jurisdiction C is generally neglected. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "even the comparatively simple structure of a provider in jurisdiction A selling a company from jurisdiction B to a customer in jurisdiction C is generally neglected."</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: The report concludes that evaluations of beneficial ownership standards need to go further in considering and analyzing the multijurisdictional nature of such arrangements. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Evaluations of beneficial ownership standards need to go further in considering and analyzing the multijurisdictional nature of such arrangements."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In March 2022 the FATF Plenary adopted changes to Recommendation 24 on beneficial ownership, requiring greater attention to risks from entities with a foreign dimension. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "at the FATF Plenary in March 2022, FATF adopted changes to its rules on beneficial ownership (Recommendation 24)"</sub>
- **expected / definition**: The test for a 'sufficient link' should be risk-based; examples include foreign entities with an ongoing business relationship with a local corporate service provider, or significant real estate or other investments in the country. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The test for determining which foreign entities fall into the “sufficient link” category should be based on risks, and examples include"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The revised Recommendation 24 requires countries to choose among three options for nominees: (a) disclose nominee status and nominator identity, making the status disclosure public; (b) license professional nominees and report nominee status and nominator identity to the beneficial ownership authority; or (c) prohibit the use of nominees altogether. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "They require countries to (a) disclose nominee status and identity of the nominator, and make that disclosure of status public, or (b) license professional nominees"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Previously countries had wide discretion by adopting unspecified other mechanisms, which resulted in the nominee issue receiving little attention in evaluations. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Previously, countries had a wide margin of discretion by adopting unspecified _other mechanisms_"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The revised rules explicitly state that beneficial owner identification, where a nominee director or shareholder controls a legal entity, requires establishing the identity of the natural person on whose behalf the nominee is ultimately acting. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "“requires establishing the identity of the natural person on whose behalf the nominee is ultimately, directly or indirectly, acting”"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Table 5 sets out new FATF glossary definitions: a nominator is an individual or legal person who instructs a nominee, directly or indirectly, to act as director or shareholder (also called shadow director or silent partner); a nominee is an individual or legal person instructed by a nominator to act for them regarding a legal person; a nominee director (also called resident director) routinely exercises director functions under the nominator's instructions and is never the beneficial owner; a nominee shareholder exercises voting rights and/or receives dividends per the nominator's instructions and is never the beneficial owner based on those shares. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A Nominee Director_(also known as a “resident director) is an individual or legal entitythat routinely exercises the functions of the director"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Table 5 glossary definitions were added to the FATF Recommendations glossary for the first time, sourced from FATF 2022. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "introduction of definitions of nominee director, nominee shareholder, and nominator to the glossary of the FATF Recommendations for the first time"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Under the revised FATF Recommendation 24, countries will be required to assess risks from all classes of entities with a sufficient link to their jurisdiction, whatever law they were incorporated under, and to take measures to mitigate those risks. Previously the assessment duty covered only entities incorporated under the country's own laws. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to assess the risks of all classes of entities with a sufficient link to their jurisdiction"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Under the revised rules, competent authorities will be required to have a mechanism for obtaining beneficial ownership information on foreign-created legal persons that have a sufficient link to their country. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "have a mechanism for obtaining beneficial ownership information on foreign-created legal persons"</sub>

## Conclusions and Recommendations

- **descriptive / finding or result**: A change in rules will have limited effect if there is not due attention to enforcing those rules. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a change in rules will have limited effect if there is not due attention to the enforcement of those rules"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report concludes that effective regulation of nominee arrangements is critical to beneficial ownership transparency, and that neglect of nominee abuses is a major vulnerability in the campaign against untraceable shell companies; without nominee transparency enforcement, beneficial ownership registers will not achieve their aims. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "without proper attention to enforcing the transparency of nominee arrangements, beneficial ownership registers will not achieve their aims."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Fourteen percent of live responses to the thousands of email solicitations for shell companies offered nominee arrangements unprompted, which the report takes to show such arrangements are very common, generic and cheap. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Given that 14 percent of the live responses to the thousands of email solicitations for shell companies offered nominee arrangements unprompted"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Nominee services are especially prevalent among the most problematic company formation providers, such as Mossack Fonseca and GT Group, and in cases where shell companies were used for money laundering; nominee arrangements combined with powers of attorney maximise beneficial owners' control while minimising their public profile. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "nominee arrangements can be combined with powers of attorney to maximize beneficial owners’ control while minimizing their public profile."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report argues the gap between formal rules and practical enforcement is a fundamental vulnerability, and that the AML policy community has spent far more effort composing rules than assessing whether they make a difference. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the AML policy community has spent much more time and effort composing and diffusing formal rules than it has assessing whether these rules actually make any difference."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The FATF Executive Secretary is quoted describing a 100% failure rate for preventive measures expected of banks and saying that all too often the process becomes a tick-box exercise. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There’s a 100% failure rate.... [A]ll too often it just becomes a tick-box process.”20"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Beneficial ownership rules and nominee rules do not enforce themselves; without enforcement, requiring beneficial owners to declare their identity will not ensure they do so. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This evidence is a stark reminder that beneficial ownership rules and those relating to nominees do not enforce themselves."</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: The report argues stronger controls on CSPs and better regulation of nominee arrangements are both necessary underpinnings of successful beneficial ownership registers, rather than alternatives; directors including nominees should be made to fulfil legal duties and be penalised if they do not. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Rather than being alternatives, stronger controls on CSPs and better regulation of nominee arrangements are both necessary underpinnings of successful beneficial ownership registers."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Examples of improvement include New Zealand's substantial investment in enforcement, and the observation that even the most open nominee providers more often than not verify customer identity, unlike twenty years earlier. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "New Zealand’s substantial investment in enforcement is a recent positive development."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The tacit assumption that jurisdictions compliant with rules make multijurisdictional structures compliant is wrong for nominee services and shell companies. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "With regard to nominee services and shell companies (and probably much else besides), this presumption is wrong."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Even if countries A, B and C are fully compliant, a shell company incorporated in A, by a provider using nominees in B, for a beneficial owner in C may remain opaque and untraceable; the corporate bank account is often in yet another jurisdiction. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Even if countries A, B, and C are fully compliant with beneficial ownership rules, a shell company incorporated in jurisdiction A"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: The report hopes the revised Recommendation 24 will push a change toward considering the risks of foreign legal entities, and argues the disconnect between single-jurisdiction rules and multijurisdictional problems needs equal attention. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "by obliging countries to take into account the risks posed by foreign legal entities."</sub>

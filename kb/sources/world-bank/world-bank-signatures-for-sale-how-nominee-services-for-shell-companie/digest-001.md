## Executive Summary

- **descriptive / context**: The report analyzes nominee arrangements in which nominees act as agents of principals in control of shell companies, focusing on how they obscure those in control and on policies to counter such abuse. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "analyzes a family of related corporate arrangements in which nominees act as agents of T principals in control of shell companies"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Evidence is drawn from a global mystery shopping exercise based on thousands of solicitations for shell companies, marketing information from shell company providers, and journalistic and policy research. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The report draws evidence from a global _mystery shopping_ exercise based on thousands of solicitations for shell companies"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Nominee arrangements are both a threat and a missed opportunity for policy makers and a major but underappreciated point of vulnerability in corporate beneficial ownership transparency. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Such arrangements are critical to corporate beneficial ownership transparency as a major but underappreciated point of vulnerability"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: The report argues for greater attention to nominee transparency, better practical enforcement of rules, and a multijurisdictional rather than country-by-country perspective in national evaluations. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "replacement of a single country-by-country approach in national evaluations with a more multijurisdictional perspective"</sub>

## Key Findings

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Nominee services offered by corporate service providers range from innocuous routine uses, to legitimate uses vulnerable to abuse, to uses whose primary purpose is hiding the beneficial owner. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "extending on a spectrum from those that are innocuous and are routinely used for legal business purposes"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Nominee arrangements are one of the most common devices for hiding the identity of those controlling shell companies, especially prevalent among the most problematic parts of the company formation industry. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "nominee arrangements are one of the most common devices for hiding the identity of those controlling shell companies"</sub>
- **expected / finding or result**: The report states that greater attention to enforcement is necessary, since beneficial ownership and nominee rules do not enforce themselves, particularly for beneficial ownership registries. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Greater attention to enforcement is necessary: the evidence presented in this report"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In 14 percent of active responses to email solicitations to set up shell companies, company service providers unprompted suggested a nominee type of arrangement. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In 14 percent of active responses to email solicitations asking to set up shell companies"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Networks of shell companies with nominees threaten corporate transparency mainly because they are multi-jurisdictional, while rules remain single-jurisdiction. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Yet there is a fundamental disconnect between the multi-jurisdictional threat and the single-jurisdiction rules to address this."</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: Effective regulation of corporate service providers and of nominee arrangements is described as critical to increasing beneficial ownership transparency. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "enforcing effective regulation of corporate service providers and regulation of nominee arrangements is critical"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report hopes recent changes to FATF Recommendation 24 will require more robust transparency rules for nominees, encourage enforcement, and oblige authorities to consider risks posed by foreign legal entities. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "requiring more robust transparency rules for nominees, by encouraging more robust enforcement"</sub>

## Introduction

- **descriptive / context**: Shell companies are among the most common means of facilitating financial crime because they can act simultaneously as an alter ego and a veil, and nominees often hide the puppet master behind a corporate puppet. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "such companies can be used simultaneously as an alter ego and a veil"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: A nominee is a person holding a role in a company as a substitute or proxy for another with a more substantive claim to control or ownership, a stand-in or front for the beneficial owner; the role is derivative and requires a principal. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a nominee is a person who holds a role in a company as a substitute or proxy for another"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Nominee directors and nominee shareholders can act like a mask concealing the principal, but they also have many legal and legitimate functions; most corporate nominee arrangements appear to serve legitimate purposes. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Nominee company directors and nominee shareholders can function like a mask"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report focuses on abuse potential of nominee and related arrangements, especially powers of attorney, that can obscure the beneficial owner, even though most nominee arrangements are legitimate. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this report focuses on the potential for abuse of nominee and related arrangements, especially the use of powers of attorney"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Research questions include legitimate and illegitimate uses of nominee services, how they are marketed with shell companies, their prominence by jurisdiction and customer type, comparison with FATF and FATF-style Mutual Evaluation Reports, and implications for beneficial ownership rules. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "What does evidence from a global mystery shopping exercise tell us about the prominence of nominee services"</sub>

## The Nature of Nominee Arrangements and the Scope of the Report

- **descriptive / definition**: Nominee arrangements are a family of related legal and informal devices forming a continuum, from pure signature-for-sale fronts with no real knowledge or control to nominees playing an important and genuinely independent role. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they are not composed of a clear-cut category that can be neatly counterposed against normal or real directors and shareholders"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Formal nominee roles may exist without explicit legislation, so finding which jurisdictions allow nominee arrangements in practice is harder than reading the laws on the books. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "finding which jurisdictions allow nominee arrangements in practice is more difficult than just reading laws on the books"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In a strict legal sense a nominee director may not be distinct from a normal director, since a director is a director regardless of private agreements with a principal; nominee roles are analogous to shell companies in this respect. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In a strictly legal sense, there may be no such thing as a nominee director distinct from a normal director"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Nominee directors receive the most coverage because they seem the most widely available nominee service and the main point of vulnerability in hiding beneficial owners. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "nominee directors receive the most coverage in this report, because they seem to be the most widely available nominee service"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: A key vulnerability is that nominee arrangements tend to be multijurisdictional while regulation is imposed and assessed country by country. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "nominee arrangements tend to be multijurisdictional, whereas regulation is imposed and assessed on a country-by-country basis"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Tighter regulation of nominee directorships is an important but underappreciated point of intervention; more pressing is greater enforcement, and transparency about who plays principal and agent roles. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is a greater need for transparency in making explicit the identity of those performing the principal and agent roles"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Nominee shareholders are proxy owners with a simpler role than directors, offering a range of legitimate and less legitimate functions; power of attorney arrangements are commonly used with nominee services. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Nominee shareholders are also important, though as proxy owners their role tends to be simpler than that of directors"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The report distinguishes nominee directors and shareholders (natural individuals acting for a natural beneficial owner) from corporate directors and shareholders (one company serving as director or owner of another); the differences are material. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "For this report, nominees are only natural individuals acting on behalf of another natural individual, the beneficial owner."</sub>

## Sources of Evidence and Mystery Shopping

- **descriptive / limitation**: Most scholarly research on nominees concerns publicly listed companies, while this report concerns privately owned shell companies, so public-company work is only indirectly relevant. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Most of the existing scholarly research on nominees deals with their role in publicly listed companies"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The Global Shell Games and Banking Bad mystery shopping field experiments are based on more than 20,000 email solicitations from real shell companies and fictitious consultants seeking shell companies or corporate bank accounts. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "based on more than 20,000 email solicitations from real shell companies and fictitious consultants seeking"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Responses from more than 7,000 corporate service providers from 2019 to 2021 provide the most systematic picture of how shell companies are sold and used. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The responses from more than 7,000 corporate service providers from 2019 to 2021"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Solicitations followed a common template with deliberate variations in risk profile, such as insisting on secrecy or changing nationality to signal corruption or terrorist financing risk, to test provider sensitivity to customer risk. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "deliberate variations were used to vary the risk profile"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The overall goal is to assess effectiveness of international beneficial ownership rules, testing whether providers follow the risk-based approach in accepting or rejecting solicitations. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The overall goal of the study is to assess the effectiveness of international beneficial ownership rules"</sub>

## Structure and Road Map

- **descriptive / context**: The report's sections cover general uses of nominees, two misuse cases (New Zealand's GT Group and UK shell companies fronted by Cypriot nominees linked to the Beirut explosion), marketing of services, mystery shopping data, comparison with eight jurisdictions' Mutual Evaluation Reports, and conclusions. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The second section considers two cases of the misuse of nominee arrangements"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Of corporate service providers willing to do business in the mystery shopping exercise, 14 percent offered nominee services of one kind or another, often to hide the beneficial owner. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "of those corporate service providers (CSPs) in the mystery shopping exercise willing to do business 14 percent offered nominee services"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: FATF country assessments should not only consider more than domestic entities and regulations but also features of foreign entities with a strong link to the assessed jurisdiction. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FATF country assessments should not only take into account more than domestic entities and regulations"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: If nominee arrangements are unregulated, or regulations exist without enforcement, the real controllers of shell companies will often remain hidden. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "regulations remain on the books without being enforced, the identity of the real people in control of shell companies will often remain hidden"</sub>

## The Uses of Nominee Arrangements: The Good, the Bad, and the Ugly

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Leaks, scandals and research show shell companies play a central role in large-scale cross-border financial crime, and nominee arrangements are among the most common devices for hiding controllers. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "nominee arrangements are one of the most common devices for hiding the identity of those controlling shell companies"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The authors' experience with thousands of CSPs and banks indicates these services often obscure real owners, either incidentally or by design. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these services often serve to obscure companies’ real owners, either incidentally or by design"</sub>

## Legitimate Uses of Nominee Directorships

- **descriptive / context**: Many and probably most uses of nominee arrangements, like shell companies generally, including offshore ones, are perfectly legal and innocuous. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "many, and probably most, uses of nominee arrangements are perfectly legal and innocuous"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Nominee directorships were created to appoint agents with specialised skills for supervisory and advisory duties in complex corporate environments involving publicly listed companies, to improve governance and performance. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Nominee directorships were created to allow the appointment of agents with specialized skills"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Nominee directorships proliferated in the latter twentieth century as a managerial tool to monitor conglomerate corporate groups and serve principals' particular interests. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Nominee directorships appear to have proliferated in the latter half of the twentieth century"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: A nominee director works on behalf of a specific nominator, often an existing board member or shareholder, with an expectation of loyalty to the principal rather than to the company as a whole. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an “expectation of loyalty to [the principal] other than to the company as a whole”"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Three features of intended nominee directorships: they are used in complex corporate groups of large interlinked companies; nominees are close to their principal; and nominees are qualified to advance the principal's interests. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "First, nominee directors were intended to be used in complex corporate groups"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: These intended criteria differ from abuse in shell companies, where nominees are simply signatures for sale designed to hide the beneficial owner. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "where nominees are simply signatures for sale designed to hide the beneficial owner"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Holding companies of physical assets or intellectual property need directors, but since they passively hold assets there is little for directors to do, so the same individuals may legitimately serve as nominee directors of many holdings. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "one can imagine the same individuals being nominee directors of many different holding companies"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In estate planning, a company may be used with a trust or foundation; the nominee may be passive until the beneficial owner dies, then assists distribution of the estate per the will. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the nominee’s role may be entirely passive until the principal (beneficial owner) has died"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Local laws requiring some or all company officers to be jurisdiction residents force nominee arrangements on foreign owners; the authors had to provide local resident directors when setting up shell companies in Australia and New Zealand. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they had to provide local resident directors (natural persons) to satisfy local laws on incorporation"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Equating nominee arrangements with suspicious activity is described as highly misleading, though obligatory nominee arrangements remain prone to abuse. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "equating of nominee arrangements with suspicious activity is highly misleading"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In some jurisdictions, such as the British Virgin Islands (the single-largest offshore incorporation jurisdiction), beneficial owners may be legally considered directors with their knowledge, and nominee-like categories are unclear. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "in some jurisdictions, beneficial owners may be legally considered directors with their knowledge"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The UK and New Zealand treat shadow directors, who exercise director functions, as directors in law. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "those who exercise the functions of a director should be legally treated as directors"</sub>

## Nominee Directors as Signatures for Sale

- **descriptive / case example**: A Hong Kong provider described nominee service as renting another person's name to protect the beneficial owner's identity, with nominee directors and shareholders appointed, nominees not signing contracts, a trust deed and power of attorney issued, and nominees not usable for account opening. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Nominee service is basically renting another person’s name to protect the identity of the real beneficial owner"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Providers offer a cluster of related services (nominee director, nominee shareholder, power of attorney) that work together. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is worth reiterating the point evidenced here about the tendency of CSPs to offer a cluster of related services"</sub>

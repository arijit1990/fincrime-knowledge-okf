## Attorney-Client Privilege

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Almost all investigators interviewed reported attorney-client privilege as an obstacle to obtaining information from TCSPs. (p. 112)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "almost all of the investigators interviewed for this study mentioned that one of the obstacles"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Attorney-client privilege imposes a lawyer's duty of confidentiality toward the client so that clients disclose information fully; it is rooted in the right to counsel and a fair trial. (p. 112)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "imposes a duty of confi dentiality on the part of the lawyer with respect to his client"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The scope of privilege varies by country; in some countries it extends to expert comptables or notaries, but there is general agreement that privilege should not apply when a lawyer performs purely fiduciary services. (p. 112)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is general agreement among authorities in most countries that the privilege should not apply"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Overcoming privilege often requires judicial proceedings; in Canada a privilege hearing is required for a judge to review each document before police receive it, and in the United States a prima facie case is needed if a lawyer misuses privilege. (p. 112)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "in Canada, a privilege hearing is required for the judge to review each piece of paper"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Investigators must weigh the benefits of a lawyer's information against the risk of tipping off the client. (p. 112)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they have to carefully weigh the benefi ts of information that the lawyer may have"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In Brazil, investigators who find the TCSP that formed a corporate vehicle may find it has sold the company to a law firm, which can then invoke privilege to avoid naming the buyer. (p. 112)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "that TCSP will oft en have sold the company to a law fi rm, which then invokes privilege"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In Germany, after a lawyer for a special purpose vehicle claimed privilege, the investigator opened insolvency proceedings and retrieved the documents from the liquidator. (p. 113)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the investigator instituted insolvency proceedings against the entity and was able to retrieve"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Lawyers engaged in certain transactional activities are well placed to obtain ownership and control information on corporate vehicles. (p. 113)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "are in a good position to obtain the relevant information on the ownership and control structure of a corporate vehicle"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Implementation of the lawyer reporting obligation varies across countries and compliance is on the whole low (see appendix A). (p. 113)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Countries have implemented this obligation to various degrees, but on the whole"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The Third EU Money Laundering Directive requires independent legal professionals to report suspicious transactions when executing transactions for clients, including creating, operating or managing trusts, companies or similar structures. (p. 113)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "which requires independent legal professionals to report suspicious transactions when execut- ing transactions for their client"</sub>

## Inclusion of Lawyers in the AML Framework

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Investigators considered TCSPs more likely than not complicit but the burden of proof was often too high, and investigators preferred to target principal perpetrators. (p. 114)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Investigators considered TCSPs more likely than not to be complicit"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A TCSP is arguably often either knowingly involved in or willfully blind to the criminal conduct it facilitates, since its services usually require human intervention unlike automated bank transactions. (p. 114)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is arguable that a TCSP is oft en either knowingly involved in or at least willfully blind"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Inclusion in the AML framework is argued to be valuable because innocently involved TCSPs may file useful reports, and complicit ones may be penalised for non-reporting. (p. 114)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "First, a minority of the TCSPs will be innocently involved and would fi le a report"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: Inclusion mainly implies conducting proper client due diligence before entering a business relationship, so that willful blindness is no longer an option. (p. 114)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "First and foremost, it is about conducting proper due diligence of a client"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In Bell v Att. Gen. (Jersey, 2006), the Court of Appeal ruled that a single provable breach of due diligence procedures by a financial services provider may constitute an offence; failure need not be systemic. (p. 114)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a single provable instance in which a TCSP fails to adhere to due diligence standards"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In Luxembourg (May 8, 2006), a lawyer was fined for failing to perform due diligence to identify beneficial owners of corporate clients, though the clients were not found to have laundered money. (p. 114)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a lawyer received monetary penal- ties for failing to perform due diligence obligations"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: From the review of cases in this study, it was difficult to ascertain whether TCSPs were knowingly involved in money laundering; some were investigated and prosecuted, while in many cases they were not. (p. 114)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "From the review of cases in this study, it was diffi cult to ascertain whether TCSPs were knowingly involved"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: One argument against including lawyers and TCSPs in the AML framework is that when they facilitate crime they rarely do so unwittingly, so reporting obligations would be unlikely to produce reports. (p. 114)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they rarely do so unwittingly. In those circumstances, what is the point of imposing an obli"</sub>

## Part 2. The Beneficial Owner

- **descriptive / context**: Beneficial ownership is relatively straightforward in theory but difficult in practice; it identifies the person who ultimately controls a corporate vehicle, is a highly context-dependent de facto judgment and cannot be reduced to a legal definition. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "benefi cial ownership cannot be reduced to a legal defi nition"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: With few exceptions, service providers lack the resources or access to information needed to really investigate a corporate vehicle; they can ask questions, search databases and compare financial conduct with profile, but little more, and any due diligence system can be beaten. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In the end, any due diligence system can be beaten"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: An investigator observed that the Abacha case, where the link between asset and beneficial owner was relatively easy to establish, dated from the 1990s, whereas current corruption cases tend to be significantly more complicated. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "corruption cases we see now tend to be signifi cantly more complicated"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: A corrupt official can control a corporate vehicle without revealing himself by holding signatory authority over its financial accounts, with the corrupt party justified to the bank by being listed as a low-level financial employee. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Th is authority can be justifi ed to the bank by deceptively listing the corrupt party as a low-level fi nancial employee"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Another strategy is to place ownership and control of a corporate vehicle with a front man who, out of loyalty, fear or financial incentive, will carry out the corrupt party's bidding. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "vest the ownership and control of the corporate vehicle in the hands of a front man"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Under the formal approach, a corrupt party can achieve control of a corporate vehicle from within and outside its structure without being identified as the beneficial owner. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is perfectly possible for a corrupt party to achieve control of a corporate vehicle"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Box 2.2 describes the Sweet Pink Inc. and Unlimited Horizon Inc. case: from 2004 to 2008 Teodoro Nguema Obiang Mangue used US lawyers, bankers, real estate agents and escrow agents to move over US$110 million in suspect funds into the United States. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "move over US$110 million in suspect funds into the United States"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Lawyer George Nagler, from 2005 to 2007, helped Obiang purchase and manage property in Malibu, California, and incorporated shell companies for him. (p. 46)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "helped him purchase and manage property in Malibu, California, and incorporated shell companies for him"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: A Sweet Pink Inc. checking account opened at Union Bank of California on 29 September 2005 had Jeffers as one of five signatories, with Obiang not on the signature card; two wire transfers of nearly US$30,000 each arrived from an Obiang Equatorial Guinea company in October 2005. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Obiang was not on the signature card"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Union Bank first noticed Obiang-related activity in 2004 after rating Equatorial Guinea high-risk; its search found a US$6.2 million wire in 2001 and seven smaller wires from 2003 to 2004, and it closed the Sweet Pink account on 27 October 2005. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "On October 27, 2005, less than one month after the Sweet Pink account had been opened, the bank closed it"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Over 2006 to 2007, Equatorial Guinea wire transfers totalling more than US$1.7 million were deposited into the law office account of attorney Michael Berger, who the Senate report said helped open the shell company Unlimited Horizon Inc. and mask Obiang's financial activities. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "instrumental in opening the shell company [Unlimited Horizon Inc.] and law ofﬁ ce accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The US$1.7 million in transfers triggered internal AML alerts, but the bank, then negotiating a deferred prosecution agreement over AML programme deficiencies, only reviewed them in June 2007, judged them suspicious and closed all three accounts. (p. 47)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the bank was in the midst of negotiating a deferred prosecution agreement"</sub>

Nagler was later told that Eve Jeffers, Obiang's then-girlfriend, would become the corporation's president. (p. 47)[^original]

- **descriptive / context**: Without the natural-person principle the beneficial ownership concept is virtually useless, since every legal entity and arrangement is ultimately controlled by a natural person; a policy not requiring penetration to that level is deficient. (p. 49)[^original]

- **expected / expectation**: Service providers should be aware of the dangers of relying on evadable standards confirmed only by client information and public records, should verify on an ongoing basis whether client information fits the services and transactions, and dig deeper in suspicious cases. ## Part 3. Where Does the Beneficial Owner Hide?

- **descriptive / finding or result**: In the vast majority of grand corruption cases studied, corporate vehicles including companies, trusts, foundations and fictitious entities were misused to conceal the identities of those involved, with companies the most frequently used. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "corporate vehicles—including companies, trusts, foundations, and fi ctitious entities— are misused to conceal the identities"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Most countries have no coherent strategy to tackle the misuse of corporate vehicles, which is used for corruption, terrorism financing, money laundering and fraud. (p. 51)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "most countries have no coherent strategy to tackle this problem"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The study distinguishes four categories of corporate vehicle: companies, trusts, foundations, and fictitious entities and unincorporated economic organisations, the last being an outlier category covering sole proprietorships, partnerships and functionally equivalent forms. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Fictitious entities is an outlier category"</sub>

## Shell Companies

- **descriptive / context**: The FATF Recommendations do not use the term shell company, but mention shell banks in Recommendation 18; the study's usage focuses on tangibility rather than illicit intent, unlike the OECD 2001 definition which implies an illicit purpose. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "comes closer to our intended usage by focusing on tangibility rather than illicit intent"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: A U.S. Senate report on a Riggs Bank money laundering case stated that in many instances a private banker sets up a shell corporation for a client and opens accounts in that corporation's name to disguise the client's ownership of the account or assets. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In many instances, a private banker will set up [a] shell corporation for a client"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 22 explains that 'significant assets' means operationally necessary assets meant primarily to benefit the company rather than its owners, such as office space, furniture or industry-specific equipment. (p. 52)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "refers to operationally necessary assets meant primarily to benefi t the company rather than its owners"</sub>

## Shell Companies

- **descriptive / context**: Shell companies are commonly formed for legitimate functions such as facilitating mergers via a neutral shell, housing joint ventures in neutral jurisdictions, sequestering liabilities, creating equity or debt tranches, personal holding, and inheritance or creditor protection. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "shell companies are also used to sequester liabilities"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: International business corporations (IBCs) are a shell company structure typically used by non-residents in offshore financial centres; they cannot conduct business in the incorporating jurisdiction and are generally exempt from local income taxes. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "IBCs are typically used for shell companies set up by nonresi- dents"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Unlike normal companies, shell companies have no economic activity, which makes information about them hard to find. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Unlike normal companies, shell companies have no economic activity"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A non-operational company may hold some of the meetings a normal company would, but it probably does not have to, although companies are often obligated to hold a shareholder meeting once a year. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "companies are oft en obligated to hold a meeting of shareholders once a year"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In the grand corruption cases reviewed, shell companies used illicitly were generally combined with additional mechanisms to obscure ownership. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "shell companies, when used illicitly, are generally used in combination with additional mechanisms to obscure"</sub>

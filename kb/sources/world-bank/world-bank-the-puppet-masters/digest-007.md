## Part 3. Where Does the Beneficial Owner Hide?

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Mechanisms used to hide beneficial ownership include exercising control surreptitiously through contracts rather than standard ownership and control positions, adding layers of corporate vehicles, hiding behind bearer shares, and locating the beneficial owners or their identifying information in another jurisdiction. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "exercising control surreptitiously through contracts (rather than “standard” ownership and control positions), adding layers of corporate vehicles"</sub>

## BOX 3.2 Misusing a Shell Company

- **descriptive / case example**: Anthony Seminerio, a former New York State assemblyman, was sentenced in February 2010 to six years in prison for defrauding the public of his honest services and was ordered to pay US$1 million in forfeiture. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Seminerio was sentenced to six years in prison for defrauding the people of New York"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Seminerio used a purported consulting firm, Marc Consultants, to solicit and receive hundreds of thousands of dollars in payments in exchange for official action benefiting those paying entities. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Seminerio used Marc Consultants to solicit and receive payments of hundreds of thousands of dollars"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Bank records showed Marc Consultants was a shell company: its account was used to receive corrupt payments and fund personal expenses rather than a genuine consulting business, with Seminerio doing little or no consulting work. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "bank records demonstrate that Marc Consultants was a shell company"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Seminerio did little or no consulting work, and U.S. government filings described Marc Consultants as a shell company whose bank account was used to receive corrupt payments and fund personal expenses. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In fact, Seminerio did little or no consulting work."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Bank records for the Marc Consultants account showed the address was Seminerio's home, only Seminerio and his wife had signature authority, and no disbursements went to employees or payroll companies. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the sole individuals with signature authority for the Marc Consultants bank account are Seminerio and his wife"</sub>

## Shelf Companies

- **descriptive / definition**: A shelf company is typically, though not uniformly, a company incorporated with a standard memorandum or articles of association, with inactive shareholders, directors and secretary, left dormant for later sale. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is incorporated with a standard memorandum or articles of association; (b) has inactive shareholders"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: On sale of a shelf company, inactive shareholders transfer shares to the purchaser and directors and secretary resign; the purchaser may receive the company's credit and tax history, and directors may continue as nominees so outsiders see only a change of ownership, which may not be registered. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Upon transfer, the purchaser may receive the company’s credit and tax history"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Until the purchaser starts operations with a shelf company, it may also be considered a shell company. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it also may be considered a shell company"</sub>

## BOX 3.3 Using Shelf Companies to Conceal Ownership of Bank Accounts

- **descriptive / case example**: Raul Salinas transferred US$100 million in questionable assets to the United States through a private banking relationship with Citibank between 1992 and 1994, with Citibank using shelf companies to disguise the source and destination of funds. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "transferred to the United States US$100 million in questionable assets using a private bank- ing relationship formed with Citibank"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Citibank set up the offshore company Trocca Ltd. and appointed three Panamanian shelf companies (Madeline Investments S.A., Donat Investments S.A., Hitchcock Investments S.A.), all incorporated in 1979, as its board; Cayman shelf company Tyler Ltd., incorporated in 1984, was a principal shareholder. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "All three of these companies had been incorporated in 1979, nearly 15 years before Trocca’s incorporation"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Citibank helped Raul Salinas avoid being connected to the scheme by circumventing the incorporation process, so no documentation identified him as beneficial owner of the accounts. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "With the help of Citibank, Salinas avoided his name being connected to the scheme by circumventing the incorporation process"</sub>

## Shelf Companies

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The grand corruption database identified a shelf company in a small number of cases (six) where a considerable amount of time elapsed between incorporation and use in a scheme. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a shelf company was involved in a small number of cases (six)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The exact number of shelf companies in the grand corruption database could not be determined, and the time lapse before use may be only a few months where shelf companies are bought soon after incorporation. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the time lapse may be only a few months"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: It is difficult to tell a shelf company from a company incorporated by a service provider and sold on, especially when the company's establishment history cannot be traced. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "especially when it proves impossible to trace the company’s establishment history"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Aged shelf companies are priced according to age: for a company under five years old, about US$1,000 per year of existence might be expected, rising to US$35,000 for a company over 10 years old, with extra benefits such as credit lines, records and bank accounts increasing cost. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "For example, for a company less than fi ve years old, one might expect to pay US$1,000 per year"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Service providers may hold stocks of shelf companies bought in bulk; transferring a shelf company is often quicker than incorporating a new one, though in some countries simplified formalities have made the timing difference minimal. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is oft en easier, quicker, and less expensive to transfer ownership of a shelf company"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Law enforcement is concerned that criminals can throw investigators off the trail by buying shelf companies and never registering the ownership change, so investigations often reach a dead-end formation agent with no purchaser records. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "criminals can easily throw investigators off the trail by purchasing shelf companies"</sub>

## Operational Entities

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Misuse of legal entities is often seen as limited to non-operational companies, but the grand corruption cases show that approximately one in seven schemes misused operational companies, called front companies. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a signifi cant proportion of the schemes (approximately one in seven) misuse operational companies"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Operational entities have inflows and outflows of assets, allowing illicit streams to be mingled with legitimate funds and laundered, so large sums can move without raising suspicion. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Operational entities have infl ows and outfl ows of assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: One supervisory authority interviewed for the project said misuse of operational entities for money laundering is a significant and growing problem. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the misuse of operational entities for money laundering purposes is a signifi cant and growing problem"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Front companies may be used to give and receive bribes; the most financially significant bribery, kickbacks and self-dealing are undertaken by persons working for big corporations rather than unaffiliated individuals. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the most fi nan- cially signifi cant instances of bribery, kickbacks, and self-dealing"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: In monetary bribery, one scheme has the briber create or contract with a consulting company to pass funds to the recipient, obscuring the payment chain; in another, the recipient creates a corporate vehicle to hide assets and his connection to them. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the giver of the bribe either creates or contracts with a consulting company"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Where an official holds a concealed stake in a venture or the bribing company, corporate vehicles become the opaque link between the corrupted party and the wealth acquired. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these corporate vehicles become the opaque link between the corrupted party and the wealth acquired"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Active bribers may hide behind not being the beneficial owner while still being able to authorise transactions; in at least one-third of database cases, bribery investigations led to operational companies settling with or without convictions. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In at least one-third of the cases in our data- base, bribery or kickback investigations led to operational companies"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Around one in seven of the grand corruption schemes in the study misused operational companies, sometimes called front companies, rather than non-operational companies. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a signifi cant proportion of the schemes (approximately one in seven) misuse operational companies"</sub>

## Companies with Bearer Shares or Share Warrants

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Bearer shares allow anonymous transfers of control because whoever physically holds the certificate is deemed its owner, and transfer requires only delivery of the instrument. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "whoever is in physical possession of the bearer shares is deemed to be their owner"</sub>

## Operational Entities

- **descriptive / case example**: In the IBM case, people at IBM's Argentina subsidiary, without knowledge of US employees or shareholders, passed millions of dollars via a subcontractor to directors of Banco de la Nacion; IBM settled with the US SEC. IBM itself was excluded from the database because it was not used to conceal payments. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In one typical case, IBM accepted a settlement with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In such cases the company provides an essential veil, and its overarching legitimate activities give cover for small transactions used to bribe officials, which are often too small to attract internal control, management or shareholder attention; control over specific transactions therefore matters more than beneficial ownership of the whole entity. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is not the benefi cial ownership of the legal entity as a whole that is important"</sub>

## BOX 3.5 Laundering Money through a Front Company

- **descriptive / case example**: Pavel Lazarenko, former Ukrainian Prime Minister, and coconspirator Peter Kiritchenko bought a 67 percent interest in EuroFed, an offshore bank in Antigua, in August 1997, after learning it was for sale while Lazarenko faced corruption allegations. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Lazarenko and Kiritchenko purchased a 67 percent interest in the bank"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The indictment alleged Lazarenko transferred approximately US$70 million into accounts he and Kiritchenko controlled at EuroFed between May and September 1997. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Lazarenko transferred approximately US$70 million into accounts he and Kiritch- enko controlled"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In 2005 the US Department of Justice filed a civil asset forfeiture case to seize Lazarenko's assets, including approximately US$85.5 million alleged to have been on deposit at EuroFed. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "approximately US$85.5 million alleged to have been formerly on deposit"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In autumn 1999, at Ukrainian request, the Antiguan government investigated EuroFed for alleged money laundering and froze its assets. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Antiguan government began an investigation of EuroFed for alleged money laundering activities and froze its assets"</sub>

## Companies with Bearer Shares or Share Warrants

- **descriptive / definition**: Bearer shares are shares in certificate form, where whoever physically possesses the certificate is deemed the owner; transfer requires only delivery, sometimes with endorsement. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "whoever is in physical possession of the bearer shares is deemed to be their owner"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Unlike registered shares, whose ownership is determined by entry in a register, bearer shares typically give the possessor voting or dividend rights; unregistered share warrants are almost identical in function, acting as a voucher entitling the holder to acquire shares. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Unlike “registered” shares (for which ownership is determined by entry in a register"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Bearer-share companies are said to be used extensively for illegal activities such as tax evasion and money laundering, and AML forums have raised concerns about this. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "companies that issue bearer shares are used extensively for illegal activities"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Most jurisdictions are reforming or eliminating bearer-share statutes, typically through dematerialization, which computerises shares and registers them in company ledgers, or immobilization, which places the bearer share with a custodial agent. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Dematerialization requires bearer shares to be computerized and registered in company ledgers"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: As an example, a Belgian law of December 14, 2005 provides for phasing out bearer shares in all domestic companies. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a Belgian law of December 14, 2005, provides for the phasing out of bearer shares"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: A bearer share promoter's marketing claims that an owner can legally and truthfully deny ownership under oath while the shares are not in the owner's possession, an approach the report cites as contributing to the reputation of such entities. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "you can legally and truthfully say under oath, ‘I am not the owner of that corpora- tion.’"</sub>

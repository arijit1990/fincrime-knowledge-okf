## Use of Front Men and Others

- **descriptive / case example**: Estrada publicised the Erap Muslim Youth Foundation and solicited donations for it but had no legal ties to it and was not an incorporator, signatory or board member, though he was considered its chairman emeritus. (p. 213)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Estrada was considered the foundation’s chairman emeritus, but he did not serve on its board of directors"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The defence argued it was impossible for Estrada to use the foundation for money laundering, because he was not a signatory and the bank chairman acting as treasurer would not allow his name to be used in money laundering. (p. 213)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the defense for Estrada argued that it was “impossible” for him to use the foundation"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The defence claimed Estrada reacted to news of a US$4.26 million gift from Governor Singson by ordering it returned, saying his immediate reaction was that it was jueteng money. (p. 213)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "his immediate reaction was that it was ‘jueteng’ money.”"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Sandiganbayan held that the funds deposited in the foundation's account could be traced to illegal jueteng collections and ordered them forfeited, relying on testimonial and documentary evidence. (p. 213)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Sandiganbayan held that the funds deposited in the founda- tion’s account could be traced to the illegal jueteng collections"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Many individuals played major and minor roles in Estrada's schemes, as detailed in the Sandiganbayan's plunder trial decision. (p. 213)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "many individuals played major and minor roles in Estrada’s schemes."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Sandiganbayan's quoted reasoning says the paper trail showed the foundation deposit came from jueteng collections, via cashier's or manager's cheques bought by Estrada's auditor Yolanda Ricaforte using deposits in accounts she opened at different bank branches. (p. 213)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "paper trail of the [funds] deposited for the Erap Muslim Youth Foundation, Inc. incon- trovertibly established"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Jinggoy Estrada, the former President's son and a named co-defendant in the plunder case, was acquitted by the Sandiganbayan, which held there was no evidence he collected or received the jueteng proceeds. (p. 213)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "was acquitted of the charge by the Sandiganbayan which held that there was no evidence"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The foundation's predecessor, Erap Para sa Mahirap, was according to trustee testimony duly established in 1988 and had 14,000 scholarship recipients as of 2000, before it folded. (p. 213)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "was duly established in 1988 and had 14,000 recipients of schol- arships as of the year 2000"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Luis Singson, then governor of Ilocos Sur, orchestrated the jueteng collection scheme for Estrada, had a long close relationship with him, and was godfather to Estrada's son; later he fell out with Estrada, publicly revealed the scheme and became a chief witness. (p. 214)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Estrada and Singson later had a falling out, and Singson publicly revealed the jueteng scheme"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Sandiganbayan found Singson lacked the purest motives in exposing the jueteng collections but nevertheless held him credible. (p. 214)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "did not have the “purest motives in exposing the ‘jueteng’ collections,” but nevertheless found him credible."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Charlie Atong Ang was a key coconspirator who met jueteng operators and fixed the amount collected from each province; he was extradited from the United States in 2006 and pleaded guilty in March 2007 to a lesser offence of corruption of public officials, ultimately receiving probation. (p. 214)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In 2006, Ang was extradited from the United States, and in March 2007, he pleaded guilty"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Yolanda Ricaforte was designated by Estrada in April 1999 as auditor for the jueteng scheme, worked from a Singson-owned building, was paid a monthly salary of US$1,705 (PHP 80,000), and kept ledgers of twice-monthly collections and expenses. (p. 214)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "designated in April 1999 by Estrada as his auditor in the jueteng scheme."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Ricaforte opened numerous bank accounts and handled transfers to and from them, and kept detailed logs of collections in two sets of ledgers covering November 1998 to August 2000. (p. 214)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "She opened numerous bank accounts and han- dled the transfers to and from the many accounts."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In the Belle Corporation shares sale scheme, Jaime Dichaves, a business associate of Estrada and director of Belle, a gaming company, spoke to Estrada about the shares, who then mentioned it to SSS and GSIS heads. (p. 214)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Jaime Dichaves, a business associate of Estrada, was a director of Belle, a gaming com- pany."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Singson testified that Dichaves was a front for Estrada in Fontaine Bleau, Inc., a casino built with jueteng protection money, with shares distributed 5% to the casino president, 25% to Singson and 70% to Estrada, placed in the names of Dichaves and Susie Pineda; the Sandiganbayan made no finding on ownership, and the company was dissolved in August 2000.   <sub>anchor: "which was a casino owned by Pres. Estrada and built with the use of jueteng protection money."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Carlos Arellano (SSS chairman, childhood friend of Estrada) and Federico Pascual (GSIS president), both appointed by Estrada, said they were uncomfortable with pressure to have their agencies buy Belle shares, which had a speculative flavour. (p. 215)[^original]

  <sub>anchor: "the two agencies spent nearly US$39.4 million in Belle shares."</sub>

## Investigation

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Estrada was convicted of plunder, but the Sandiganbayan traced only about US$18.6 million of illegal activity, far short of the US$87 million charged; it found insufficient evidence of sources for Velarde account deposits except the Belle commission and jueteng collections. (p. 215)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the approximate US$18.6 million the San- diganbayan was able to trace to Estrada’s illegal activities fell far short of the US$87 million"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Estrada's lack of legal ties to the foundation, including absence from its incorporation documents, was an investigative obstacle, though the court still held him beneficial owner of the traced funds; he also had no legal ties to the Boracay Mansion, which was owned by St. Peter Holdings Corp. (p. 215)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "his name did not appear on the incorporation documents."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Sandiganbayan held that funds used to buy the Boracay Mansion, where Estrada's mistress lived, could be traced to the Jose Velarde account of which Estrada was the beneficial owner. (p. 215)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the funds used to purchase it could be traced to the Jose Velarde account"</sub>

## Case Study 7: Saudi Arabian Fighter Deals and BAE Systems

- **descriptive / finding or result**: BAE admitted it failed to adequately review or verify benefits to the Saudi official, including more than US$5 million in invoices from May 2001 to early 2002, against its prior anticorruption compliance statements to the US government. (p. 217)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "BAE admitted it failed to undertake adequate review or verifi cation of these benefi ts"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: BAE agreed to transfer over £10 million and more than US$9 million to a Swiss bank account controlled by an intermediary, aware of a high probability that part would go to the same Saudi official. (p. 217)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "BAE also agreed to transfer more than British Pounds (£) 10 million"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: On 1 March 2010 BAE pleaded guilty to conspiring to defraud the US, making false statements about its FCPA compliance programme, and violating the Arms Export Control Act and ITAR, and was ordered to pay a US$400 million criminal fine. (p. 217)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "BAE was ordered to pay a US$400 million criminal fi ne"</sub>

## BAE’s Use of Shell Companies to Conceal Intermediary Relationships

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: BAE used marketing advisors and intermediaries and paid them through offshore shell companies, despite their failure to perform required FCPA due diligence, and encouraged advisors to set up their own offshore shells to disguise payment origins and recipients. (p. 217)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "BAE made payments to these advisors through off shore shell companies"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: One shell used to conceal marketing advisor relationships was established in the British Virgin Islands. (p. 218)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "One such entity, used by BAE to conceal the marketing advisor relationships, was estab- lished in the British Virgin Islands"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Under the BVI Business Companies Act 2004, incorporation requires only a registered office and a registered agent; physical location, legal ownership, management and beneficial ownership need not be filed in the central registry. (p. 218)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "incorporation of a legal entity in BVI requires minimal information at the time of registration"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Registers of members and directors must be kept with the registered agent but are available only to directors and members; BVI incorporation offered anonymity and hampered authorities' ability to penetrate arrangements. (p. 218)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they are available only for inspec- tion by directors and members of the company."</sub>

## The Role of the KSA Official

- **descriptive / context**: The case exemplifies the often passive role of the Politically Exposed Person in grand corruption; formal arrangements included Letters of Offer and Acceptance that set expenditure types, services and prices. (p. 218)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this case is exemplary of the oft en “passive” role of the Politically Exposed Person (PEP)."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Saudi official was not the facilitator; BAE's marketing advisors fulfilled that role. He received shell company money without his name on incorporation papers, did not devise the scheme, and was opportunistic, receiving bribes for influence behind the scenes. (p. 218)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "He did not devise the scheme, but was merely—to no lesser fault—opportunistic."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: BAE provided some of these support services to the Saudi official through travel agents retained by a trusted BAE employee, who was also a confidant of the official. (p. 218)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "retained by a BAE employee, who was also a trusted"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The support benefits, provided through payment mechanisms in the United States and elsewhere, included travel, accommodation, security services, real estate, automobiles and personal items. (p. 218)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "included the purchase of travel, accommodations, security services, real estate, automobiles"</sub>

## Investigation and Asset Recovery

- **descriptive / limitation**: The plea agreement cited the BVI offshore entity as an investigatory obstacle; penetrating BVI entities is difficult because of limited registration information, a problem that also affects many other jurisdictions. (p. 219)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Penetrating an arrangement involving an incorporated BVI entity can be diffi cult"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: BAE avoided written communication with intermediaries, failed to record reasons for advisor suitability or relevant work documents, and advisor contracts were often held by secretive offshore legal trusts, concealing payments. (p. 219)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "BAE avoided communicating with the intermediaries in writing"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FBI Washington Field Office FCPA squad and ICE Counter Proliferation Unit investigated, assisted by DoD Criminal Investigative Services, GSA Inspector General and DOJ Office of International Affairs; the UK Serious Fraud Office gave significant assistance. (p. 219)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the BAE case was investi- gated by the Federal Bureau of Investigation’s Washington Field Offi ce’s FCPA squad"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In a sentencing memorandum footnote, BAE replaced nearly all top leadership, overhauled corporate responsibility, imposed a moratorium on new marketing advisor agreements, terminated most advisor agreements after a 2007 review, and created an External Review Panel of US and UK lawyers to vet advisors. (p. 219)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "BAE has now replaced nearly all of its top leadership"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: BAE pleaded guilty on 1 March 2010 in the US District Court for the District of Columbia and was fined $400 million, one of the largest criminal fines levied against a company for business-related violations in the US. (p. 220)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "one of the largest criminal fi nes ever levied in the United States against a company"</sub>

## 2.2 Origin of the Term Beneficial Owner

- **descriptive / context**: Although the term is now applied in many situations without trusts, its essence remains the person who ultimately controls an asset and can benefit from it; in criminal investigations the answer is often to find out who benefits. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the essence of the concept—as referring to the person who ultimately controls an asset and can benefi t from it—remains the same"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The beneficial owner in criminal situations exercises control indirectly and covertly, not directly and overtly, and may be absent from the scene while still being the one who benefits and controls. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "he is the one who exercises control in the end—not directly and overtly, but indirectly and covertly"</sub>

## 2.3 Defining Beneficial Ownership: The Theory

- **descriptive / definition**: The FATF defines beneficial owner as the natural person(s) who ultimately owns or controls a customer and/or the person on whose behalf a transaction is conducted, including those who exercise ultimate effective control over a legal person or arrangement. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Benefi cial owner refers to the natural person(s) who ultimately owns or con- trols a customer"</sub>

## 2.3.1 Natural Person versus Legal Person

- **descriptive / definition**: A beneficial owner is always a natural person; a legal person cannot by definition be a beneficial owner, and ultimate control is always exercised by a natural person even where a legal person holds ownership. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a legal person cannot, by defi nition, be a benefi cial owner"</sub>

## 2.3.2 Beneficial versus Legal Ownership

- **descriptive / definition**: The defining characteristic of a beneficial owner is a degree of control over an asset that allows benefit from it; whether the person holds legal title is irrelevant, and the essence of beneficial ownership is control rather than ownership in the ordinary sense. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Whether he is the legal owner (that is, holds legal title to it) is irrelevant"</sub>

## Box 2.1 The Origin of the Trust

- **descriptive / context**: Trusts were in use in the 12th century during the Crusades, when a crusader left his land with a person in charge who could exercise the owner's powers while the crusader kept his rights; courts of equity treated the crusader as owner, while common-law courts treated the person left in charge as owner. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they were in use in the 12th century during the time of the Crusades"</sub>

## 2.3 Defining Beneficial Ownership: The Theory

- **descriptive / context**: The FATF definition was developed for a bank or other service provider that must establish the identity of a prospective customer's beneficial owner before carrying out transactions, and it does not intend to suggest the customer is a natural person. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Th e defi nition does not intend to suggest that the “customer” is a natural person"</sub>

## 2.3.2 Beneficial versus Legal Ownership

- **expected / expectation**: Beneficial ownership should not be confused with legal ownership, because control and legal title often lie in different hands in the situations the report addresses. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is important, therefore, not to confuse benefi cial ownership with legal ownership"</sub>

## 2.3.3 Control—What Is It and Who Has It?

- **descriptive / context**: The concept of control is difficult given the many ways it can be exercised; the legal form and actual structure of a corporate vehicle is a useful starting point but does not give the whole answer. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Th e legal form and actual structure of the corporate vehicle provide a useful starting point"</sub>

## Control in Companies

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The analysis of 150 grand corruption cases shows the company is the main type of corporate vehicle used to conceal beneficial ownership. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Our analysis of 150 grand corruption cases shows that the main type of corporate vehi- cle used to conceal benefi cial ownership is the company"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In a company limited by shares, three groups may arguably have ultimate control: shareholders (who can exercise voting rights), the board of directors (who generally exercise a more immediate control under their powers), and possibly executive officers (who exercise day-to-day control). (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In a company limited by shares, three groups of people might arguably qualify as having ultimate control"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Shareholders are usually said to have the most control, since they are not controlled by others (if natural persons acting for themselves), can typically remove directors, and ultimately enjoy the financial benefits of the company. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they typically can remove the directors and ultimately enjoy the fi nancial benefi ts"</sub>

## Control in Trusts

- **descriptive / context**: In a trust, the trustee may arguably be the beneficial owner because he manages the trust asset day to day and could dispose of it, but he is legally bound to act in the beneficiary's interest and so acts on behalf of someone else under fiduciary obligations. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "He is, how- ever, legally bound to act in the interest of the benefi ciary"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Companies have a relatively straightforward structure with identifiable owners (the shareholders), whereas a significant number of alternative corporate vehicles such as trusts cannot be owned and have no position equivalent to a shareholder. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they cannot be owned, and simply no position is equivalent to the shareholder"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The trustee is treated as both the customer and the beneficial owner under the FATF methodology, since the trustee qualifies as part of the trust and as its ultimate controller. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "So the trustee is perceived as being both the customer and the benefi cial owner"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The settlor, who created the trust and contributed the asset, and the beneficiary, who stands to benefit, may each be considered, but neither can exercise control over the trust. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "He, however, is no longer able to exercise control over the trust"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Compliance officers interviewed for the study generally confirmed that all standard trust parties (settlor, trustee and beneficiary) are relevant and should be considered. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "all standard parties to the trust (settlor, trustee, and benefi - ciary) are relevant and should be considered"</sub>

## Control in Foundations

- **descriptive / context**: A foundation cannot be owned, so it may be analysed like a trust; although the director or board is the obvious first point to look at, for a private foundation with a private beneficiary that analysis is too simplistic because the private beneficiary is also of interest. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the private benefi ciary is also of interest"</sub>

## The Relationship between Ownership and Control

- **descriptive / definition**: Because the FATF definition refers to ultimate ownership of a customer, and natural persons cannot be owned, the owned customer can only be a corporate vehicle; ownership is treated as a proxy for control and is relevant only to the extent control can be inferred from it. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "ownership is a proxy for control and, in this context, is only relevant to the extent that control can be inferred from it"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: According to the FATF methodology, for companies identifying the beneficial owner normally means identifying people with a controlling interest and those who make up the mind and management of a company. (p. 39)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "those who make up “the mind and man- agement of a company.”"</sub>

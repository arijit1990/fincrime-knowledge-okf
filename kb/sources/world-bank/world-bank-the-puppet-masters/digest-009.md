## 3.2.4 Fictitious Entities and Unincorporated Economic Organizations

- **descriptive / context**: The FATF 40 Recommendations glossary says the transparency guidance in Recommendation 33 extends beyond formally incorporated entities to partnerships, associations and similar bodies that can establish a permanent customer relationship with a financial institution or otherwise own property. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "guidance given in Recommenda- tion 33 on the need for transparency of legal persons is meant to extend"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Fictitious entities provided opacity in a small number of grand corruption cases, typically used as the name of a business-class bank account, as a contract name such as a government vendor, to move funds abroad, or to make cash withdrawals before fraud was discovered. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Th ese types of fi ctitious entities provided opacity in a small number of the grand corruption investigations"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Authorities are less aware of such entities' existence, while criminals face no more liability than they already faced from the illicit nature of their activities. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "authorities are less aware of the existence of the entity,42 while the criminals face no more liability"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Some misused entities were originally legitimate operating businesses that owners later misused, such as Berry Exports in Case Study 2 in appendix D; others were built to withstand some scrutiny, such as the Hollis Griffin partnership registered with local authorities; others were blatant falsehoods. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Some of these misused entities were originally legitimate, operational businesses"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Some cases involved nonexistent companies purportedly incorporated in some jurisdiction that did not appear in any company registry, even as shell companies. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "several cases involved nonexistent companies that were purportedly incorporated in some jurisdiction"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnotes 42-45 explain: unincorporated entities avoid bureaucratic red tape; they offer no legal protection against unlimited liability, a concern less relevant to purely criminal entities; registration means providing business names and parties to local authorities, distinct from incorporation; and fictitious companies often received government contracts used as fraud vehicles, never performed, or subcontracted. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Although they did not really exist, such companies oft en received government contracts"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The subsection's category of corporate vehicle includes only those with the most tenuous separation of personality from their controllers, existing entirely as an alternative name under which persons conduct business. (p. 67)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the category of corporate vehicle referred to in this subsection includes only those with the most tenuous separation"</sub>

## Box 3.10 Receiving Fraudulent Government Contracts by a Partnership

- **descriptive / case example**: Hollis Griffin, a U.S. Virgin Islands Environmental Protection Director, with other officials authorised and awarded more than US$1.4 million in contracts in exchange for bribes and kickbacks; this is described as one of the only clear instances of a general partnership created for anonymity to launder corruption proceeds. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "One of the only clear instances in which a general partnership was found to have been created"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Griffin and others formed a fictitious partnership called Elite Technical Services (Elite I), registered in May 2000 with the Lieutenant Governor's office under a trade name, with a forged signature of another official listed as partner. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In May 2000, several of Grifﬁ n's conspirators registered Elite I"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In February 2001, after the first illicit contract was completed, the Elite I partnership was converted into a U.S. Virgin Islands corporation, Elite Technical Services, Inc. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Elite I partnership was converted into a U.S. Virgin Islands corporation"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Griffin, Modeste and Brewley pled guilty, were sentenced to jail time and were jointly and severally liable for US$1.1 million as a general partnership. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "found jointly and severally liable for US$1.1m."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In some cases, bankers' dishonest collusion or negligence gave fictitious entities access to financial services; in others, banks accepted plausible but false or forged documents and satisfactory explanations for transactions. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In some cases, access to the fi nancial services for these types of entities was the result of dishonest collusion"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Most unincorporated businesses opened accounts under bank protocols for sole proprietorships, partnerships and trading-as or doing-business-as names used by persons engaged in trade. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Most unincorporated businesses were able to open fi nancial accounts under the protocols"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Elite I was awarded a no-bid contract several months before its registration and was paid US$125,755.34 without fulfilling the contract's terms, with about US$80,000 in cash delivered to Hollis and other officials. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Several months before registration, the partnership was awarded a no-bid contract by DPNR relating to a building-permit request."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Payment was received via two checks paid into a First Bank account, and the funds were then removed in structured cash withdrawals. (p. 68)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Payment was received via two checks paid into a First Bank account, following which the funds were removed from the account in structured cash withdrawals."</sub>

## Box 3.11 Laundering Money through a Sole Proprietorship

- **descriptive / case example**: Nigeria pursued civil asset recovery in the UK to recover £762,000 that entered the UK financial system from £2.6 million of Plateau State public funds obtained by Governor Joshua Dariye through abuse of office. (p. 69)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "in the hopes of recouping £762,000 that had found its way into the U.K."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Dariye applied in December 1999 to open an account in the alias name Ebenezer Retnan Ventures, which he did not register with the Nigerian Corporate Affairs Commission; he asked the bank to waive account-opening requirements beyond the application form, and used the account to receive large sums from Plateau State. (p. 69)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Mr. Dariye did not register Ebenezer Retnan Ventures with the Nigerian Corporate Affairs Commission"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Nigeria's Particulars of Claim alleged Dariye wrongfully transferred N438.6 million (about £2.6 million) from public funds to the Ebenezer Retnan account; the High Court ordered judgment for Nigeria against Dariye and his wife for US$5.7m plus interest, totalling US$8m, on June 7, 2007. (p. 69)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "wrongfully transferred N438.6 million (about £2.6 million) from public funds"</sub>

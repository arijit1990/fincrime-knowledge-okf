## Grand Corruption: 10 Case Studies - Case Study 2: Charles Warwick Reid

- **descriptive / case example**: Charles Warwick Reid, a New Zealand lawyer, joined Hong Kong's Attorney General's Chambers in 1975, rose to principal crown counsel and head of the Commercial Crime Unit, and by 1989 controlled assets of roughly HK$12.4 million. (p. 193)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Charles Warwick Reid, a lawyer from New Zealand, arrived in Hong Kong SAR, China,148 to join the Attorney General's Chambers in 1975"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Reid pled guilty to a single count of unexplainable possession of pecuniary resources disproportionate to his official emoluments, testified that the funds were bribes for obstructing prosecutions, and served four-and-a-half years of an eight-year sentence before deportation to New Zealand in November 1994. (p. 193)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "He served four-and-a-half years of his eight-year sentence, and then was deported to New Zealand, arriving November 30, 1994."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Reid's ostensible cooperation with the recovery process and the challenges in recovering the ill-gotten gains are presented as valuable insight in the case study. (p. 193)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Reid's ostensible cooperation with the recovery process provide valuable insight as well"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Reid became embroiled in another bribery scandal shortly after returning to New Zealand, despite having been stripped of his status and reputation. (p. 193)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Reid became embroiled in another bribery scandal shortly upon his return to New Zealand"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Reid used trust arrangements to obscure ownership of illegal assets: he held money in trust accounts of local solicitors and his family home was registered to a solicitor acting as trustee. (p. 194)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Reid held money in trust in the trust accounts of his local solicitors,157"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Within a little over a week of release, Reid again held corrupt assets moving from a foreign jurisdiction into New Zealand and back out to another foreign jurisdiction. (p. 194)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In a period of just over a week, Reid was again in pos- session of corrupt assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The laundering risks of Reid's tactics (moving assets through financial institutions via trusts and similar arrangements, using agents, lawyers and straw persons) still exist, and New Zealand faces a dangerous absence of safeguards designed to detect them. (p. 194)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the risks of money laundering from tactics employed by Reid"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Reid was alleged to have received an additional bribe to help derail another trial; a trust was then created by his accountant, with the bribe giver as settlor, the accountant as trustee and Reid and his family as beneficiaries, and the trust money was managed from a foreign bank account. ## Grand Corruption: 10 Case Studies - Development of the Constructive Trust Doctrine

- **descriptive / case example**: Hong Kong's Attorney General fought a precedent-setting battle through New Zealand's courts up to the Privy Council to recover about HK$12.4 million of bribe money converted into property through CVs and legal owners. (p. 195)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the attorney general of Hong Kong was forced to fi ght a precedent-setting battle"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Privy Council held that bribe assets should be transferred to the person who suffered the breach of duty, applying equity's principle that what ought to have been done is treated as done, and recognised that property acquired in breach of trust is held on constructive trust for the true owner. (p. 195)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Th e Privy Council judgment was based on the principle of equity"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Constructive Trust Doctrine is described as having controversies, yet it is now regarded as a useful tool for preventing dispersal of corrupt funds and recovering proceeds of corrupt activities such as bribery. (p. 195)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Although not without its controversies, the Constructive Trust Doctrine is now a useful tool"</sub>

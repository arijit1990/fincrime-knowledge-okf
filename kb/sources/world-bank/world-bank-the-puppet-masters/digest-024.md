## BOX C.1 The Liechtenstein Anstalt

- **descriptive / context**: The Anstalt is a flexible corporate form particular to Liechtenstein, closely related to the trust enterprise but distinct from a foundation or trust, with a history in the Principality dating to 1928. (p. 165)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it has a relatively long history in the Principality dating back to 1928."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: An Anstalt has legal personality like a company or foundation; its capital may be divided into shares, but this is rare because it incurs withholding tax. (p. 165)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Its capital may be divided into shares; however, because this incurs a withholding tax liability, it is rare."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: An Anstalt can be formed in three to five days when the founder transfers asset rights to a board of directors by an act of assignment, with at least CHF 30,000 or US$30,000 paid up and creation by entry in the Public Register. (p. 165)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Capital of at least either CHF 30,000 or US$30,000 must be paid up"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The board may have a single member and administers the Anstalt subject to bylaws, which need not be registered and may be revoked or modified by the founder, who is also the beneficiary unless bylaws specify otherwise. (p. 165)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The bylaws do not have to be registered, and they may be revoked or modiﬁ ed by the founder"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Anstalten could historically be used for commercial or noncommercial purposes, but commercial use has recently been restricted; since 1980 commercial Anstalten must lodge annual audited accounts. (p. 165)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Since 1980, Anstalten pursuing commercial activity have had to lodge annual audited accounts."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Anstalten used like a holding company must have a local representative in Liechtenstein, usually the board member, who may also be a local company. (p. 165)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Those used like a holding company must also have a local representative in Liechtenstein"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: TCSPs charge about CHF 5,000 to form an Anstalt plus CHF 3,000 annually; the Anstalt is taxed annually at 0.1 percent of capital or CHF 1,000, whichever is greater. (p. 165)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Anstalt is taxed annually at 0.1 percent of capital, or CHF1,000, whichever is the greater."</sub>

## BOX C.2 The Panamanian Foundation

- **descriptive / definition**: The Panamanian Foundation was established by 1995 legislation, modelled jointly on the Liechtenstein foundation, the Panamanian corporation and the common law trust, and is used for estate planning, holding assets, asset protection or charity. (p. 166)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "was established by legislation in 1995, being jointly modeled on the Liechtenstein foundation"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: In a Panamanian foundation the founder transfers assets, which become legally owned by the foundation; purposes are set in a public charter or private bylaws, a foundation council administers assets, and beneficiaries may include the founder. (p. 166)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A foundation is established when the founder transfers assets to the foundation, which becomes the legal owner"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Panamanian foundation resembles a common law trust in its founder-foundation-council-beneficiary structure but, unlike trusts, is a legal person like a company. (p. 166)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Unlike trusts, however, foundations are, like companies, legal persons."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The Panamanian foundation combines high practical founder control with tight confidentiality; founders, council members and beneficiaries may be corporate entities from any jurisdiction, and nominee founders, usually a Panamanian law firm acting as registered agent, keep the original founder unregistered. (p. 167)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is common to use nominee founders (usually a law ﬁ rm in Panama, which must act as the registered agent)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A founder may also be a council member, beneficiary or protector, and a protector may veto certain council decisions; the foundation pays no tax in Panama and is not covered by foreign inheritance laws. (p. 167)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The foundation pays no tax in Panama and is speciﬁ cally not covered by foreign inheritance laws."</sub>

## CLOSING MATTERS

## Legal Arrangements

- **descriptive / definition**: FATF defines 'legal arrangements' as express trusts or similar arrangements, including fiducie, treuhand and fideicomiso. (p. 183)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "“Legal arrangements” refers to express trusts or other similar legal arrangements"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: FATF defines 'legal persons' as bodies corporate, foundations, Anstalten, partnerships, associations or similar bodies that can establish a permanent customer relationship with a financial institution or otherwise own property. (p. 183)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "“Legal persons” refers to bodies corporate, foundations, Anstalten, partnerships, or associations"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The key difference is that a legal person can engage in business and hold rights and obligations itself, whereas a legal arrangement is a relationship in which one person holds legal title and another holds beneficial title. (p. 183)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the essential characteristic being that one person holds the legal title while another holds a benefi cial title"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A fideicommiso is a Roman-law-derived arrangement used in testate law to leave an estate to one person who must pass it on to another. (p. 183)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A fi deicommiso, for instance, is an arrangement of Roman law extrac- tion used, in testate law"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A usufruct achieves something similar, often allowing a surviving spouse full benefit of an estate while title rests with the children. (p. 183)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A usufruct achieves something similar, oft en used to allow a surviving spouse the full benefi t of an estate"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The trust is the most typical and most discussed legal arrangement used with corporate vehicles for illicit purposes. (p. 183)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Th e most typical and certainly most discussed legal arrangement when it comes to the use of corporate vehicles for illicit purposes is the trust."</sub>

## The Trusts

- **descriptive / definition**: The trust relationship was originally created by the English Court of Equity; in a typical trust, a settlor transfers legal title to property to a trustee and equitable title to beneficiaries under the trust instrument. (p. 183)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In a typical trust, a grantor or settlor transfers the legal title to property (the right to control"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: If trustee and beneficiary are the same, legal and equitable title merge and the trust ceases to exist. (p. 184)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "If the trustee and benefi ciary are the same, legal and equitable title are said to merge"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: As a general rule, separating legal and beneficial interests prevents beneficiary creditors from seizing trust assets, subject to exceptions. (p. 184)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the separation of legal and benefi cial interests prevents creditors of the benefi ciary from seizing trust assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Trustees owe beneficiaries a duty of loyalty, preferring beneficiaries' interests over their own in relation to the trust. (p. 185)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Trustees owe benefi ciaries a duty of loyalty, meaning that with respect to the trust relationship"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: A constructive trust may arise involuntarily where someone has a claim to property but legal title cannot be transferred. (p. 185)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A trust relationship may be created involuntarily in instances in which some- one has a claim"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In most jurisdictions the grantor may select the governing law of the trust; some civil law jurisdictions have adopted trust statutes and most common law jurisdictions have modified trust law by statute. (p. 185)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In most jurisdictions, the grantor may select the law that governs the operation of the trust."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: A trust protector is a 'super' trustee-like overseer who does not manage day-to-day operations but may replace the trustee or change the governing law. (p. 185)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the protector acts as a kind of overseer of the trust, and is oft en given the right to replace the trustee"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Trusts generally lack separate legal personality, so they cannot own property, engage in business or be party to contracts, unlike companies or civil law foundations. (p. 186)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a trust can- not own property, engage in business, or be a party to contracts."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The vast majority of trusts serve legitimate purposes such as family estate planning, charitable donations and corporate functions such as isolating pension plan funding. (p. 186)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Th e vast majority of trusts are used for legitimate purposes, such as family estate plan- ning"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Trusts have traditionally been granted confidentiality; with one or two exceptions, no jurisdiction currently requires trusts to register in a publicly accessible register. (p. 186)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "With one or two exceptions, no jurisdiction in the world currently requires trusts to register"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In Panama, a trustee, government agent or anyone transacting with a trust who discloses trust information, other than as required by law, faces up to six months' jail and a fine of up to US$50,000. (p. 186)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "if a trustee, government agent, or any person transacting with the trust discloses information about the trust, except as required by law"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Trusts can protect individuals' assets from creditors, for example physicians placing personal assets in trusts so malpractice judgments exceeding insurance cannot attach them. (p. 187)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "many physicians have chosen to place their personal assets (for example, homes, investments) into a trust"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In English-law jurisdictions, trusts holding company stock to retain control after the grantor dies have created difficulty because the duty to manage for beneficiaries may require selling stock. (p. 187)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In jurisdictions that follow Eng- lish law, this has created some diffi culty."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In the United States courts follow the trust instrument over general fiduciary duty rules, so retaining stock or voting it against the grantor's wishes is not a problem. (p. 187)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In the United States, this is not a problem, because the courts follow the terms of the trust instrument"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Several Commonwealth offshore centres have adopted by statute a similar rule, creating the so-called Virgin Islands Special Trust Act (VISTA) trust. (p. 187)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A number of Commonwealth off shore centers (including many that are British dependencies"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Trusts' confidentiality has led to a popular perception of them as useful instruments for illicit activities; they can launder corruption proceeds by camouflaging assets and creating barriers to recovery. (p. 187)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it has led to a popular perception of trusts and similar legal arrangements as particularly useful instruments for illicit activities."</sub>

## BOX C.3 The British Virgin Islands VISTA Trusts

- **descriptive / definition**: VISTA trusts, established under the BVI Special Trust Act of 2003, combine the advantages of a conventional trust with greater settlor control over business activities. (p. 187)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "while allowing the settlors more control over the business activities carried on within the structure"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A VISTA structure must have at least two elements: the trust itself and an underlying BVI company wholly owned by the trust, with a licensed BVI service provider as trustee responsible for collecting and retaining customer due diligence records. (p. 187)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A VISTA trust structure must consist of at least two basic elements"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Settlors typically act as directors of the underlying company and retain business control because trustees are excused from the normal duty to monitor the company and maximise share value, and may be prevented from changing directors. (p. 187)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the trustees are excused from the normal ﬁ duciary duty to monitor the performance of the company"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Settlors may also retain control through a protector who can veto trustee decisions or replace trustees; VISTA trusts suit family business succession planning and may be charitable with no named beneficiary. (p. 187)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "VISTA trusts might be particularly useful for the head of a family business"</sub>

## The Trusts

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Trusts can camouflage assets by making them appear as unqualified property of a trustee, who cannot reveal being a trustee for another party, and where registration is often not required so existence is hard to determine. (p. 188)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it oft en is diffi cult for a jurisdiction (and investigators in particular) to determine whether a trust exists at all"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Split legal and beneficial ownership makes it difficult for private or public parties to enforce claims against trust assets, unless the trust was set up specifically to defeat legitimate claimants. (p. 188)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Having split legal and benefi cial ownership, it is diffi cult for other private or public parties to enforce claims"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Asset protection trusts do not always block action: courts may order trustees to release assets to creditors where a transfer breached a statute or was fraudulent, provided the court has jurisdiction over the trustee or protector. (p. 189)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "courts may order the trustee to release assets to creditors if they fi nd that the transfer"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Flee clauses required a trustee to move trust assets to another jurisdiction when litigation arose, but the Mareva injunction and similar US techniques, and 'Mareva by letter', have made flee clauses less effective. (p. 189)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Th e development of the Mareva injunction (and similar techniques in the United States) made such fl ee clauses less eff ective."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Many trusts have replaced flee clauses with Protector Resettlement Clauses, which let the protector move assets more quickly and may make Mareva actions harder. (p. 189)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In many cases, fl ee clauses have been replaced with Protector Resettlement Clauses"</sub>

## Appendix D. Grand Corruption: 10 Case Studies

- **descriptive / case example**: In 1990 the Government of Antigua and Barbuda, under Prime Minister Lester Bird, authorised Ambassador Bruce Rappaport to renegotiate its debt with IHI; the civil complaint alleges he manipulated figures so the GOAB overpaid to IHI Debt Settlement, a company he beneficially owned. (p. 189)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "issued to GOAB Ambassador Bruce Rappaport111 the authority to renegotiate the GOAB’s debt"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The IHI debt required monthly payments of US$199,740.25 for 25 years, but Rappaport allegedly arranged monthly payments of US$403,334 for 25 years. (p. 189)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Th e IHI debt required monthly payments of only US$199,740.25 to IHI for 25 years"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The GOAB began payments on December 31, 1996, eight months before Rappaport claimed to have reached agreement with IHI, and was allegedly deceived into paying over US$14 million. (p. 189)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "As a result of this scheme, the GOAB was deceived into making payments in excess of US$14 million."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The GOAB later recouped the majority of the US$14 million through a settlement with IHI Debt Settlement and Bruce Rappaport. (p. 189)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the GOAB eventually was able to recoup the major- ity of the US$14 million"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: From December 1996 to October 2003 monthly payments went to IHI Debt Settlement's account at Bank of N.T. Butterfield in Bermuda, and Rappaport then moved the overpayments to other corporate vehicles, including Debt Settlement Administrators LLC and National Petroleum Ltd. (p. 190)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the monthly payments were made into IHI Debt Settlement’s bank account at Bank of N.T. Butterfield in Bermuda"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The scheme relied on corporate vehicles to maintain anonymity and hide behind entities; two notable issues were the choice of jurisdictions and the use of shelf companies. (p. 190)[^original]

## Choice of Jurisdictions: From Hong Kong SAR, China, to Florida, United States

- **descriptive / case example**: IHI Debt Settlement was set up in Hong Kong SAR, China by Bruce or Ruth Rappaport; both were listed as directors on the 2006 annual return but only Ruth Rappaport signed it. (p. 190)[^original]

## Use of Shelf Companies

- **descriptive / case example**: IHI Debt Settlement, Dredson and Gregson were all shelf companies; IHI Debt Settlement was incorporated as Offshore Services Limited in 1970 and renamed in September 1997, while Dredson and Gregson were incorporated in 1972. (p. 191)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "IHI Debt Settlement was incorporated under the name Offshore Services Limited in 1970."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: People may use shelf companies to create an appearance of legitimacy from longevity, or to circumvent information requirements at incorporation. (p. 191)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "One such reason may be to create the appearance of legitimacy that comes with longevity."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Per a US Department of Justice official, criminals can evade investigators by buying shelf companies without officially transferring ownership, leaving investigations to lead to formation agents with no purchaser records. (p. 191)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "criminals can easily throw investigators off the trail by purchasing shelf companies"</sub>

## Layering of Corporate Vehicles

- **descriptive / context**: The Airports Authority of Trinidad and Tobago (AATT) paid funds into Calmaquip's Dresdner Bank accounts in Miami between April and November 2000 for its work on CP-13, with forty-five payments totalling more than US$29,095,477. (p. 226)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Forty-fi ve payments were made, ranging from US$20,461.95 to US$5,500,663.75, and amounting to more than US$29,095,477."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The payments from Trinidad and Tobago for CP-9 and CP-13 went to Northern and Calmaquip, which gave the appearance of legitimacy because those companies actually bid on and performed the work, although they were later found to have been vastly overpaid. (p. 226)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is now clear that the companies were vastly overpaid for their work, but at the time, this fact was obscured by the rigged bid-"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: BHC's role as project manager was essential to securing future subcontracts, so a façade of legitimacy, including a purported competing presentation in Canada, was critical to the scheme's success. (p. 226)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "providing a façade of legitimacy was critical to the scheme's success."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: On May 11, 2000, Calmaquip's president Raul Gutierrez allegedly moved US$2,000,000 from Dresdner Bank to AMA Investment Group's Bank Leu account, and AMA then transferred US$1,500,000 to Argentum's Bank Leu account the same day. (p. 227)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "wire transferred US$2,000,000 from Dresdner Bank to Bank Leu Ltd. on behalf of the company, AMA Investment Group (AMA)."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Over the following month, Steve Ferguson allegedly moved funds from Argentum's Bank Leu account to accounts of other CVs such as Bocora Holding and Maritime Securities Holdings; in August and September 2000 Gutierrez and Armando Paz made transfers to Empresas Sudamericana accounts, which later sent money to Argentum. (p. 227)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Over the course of the next month, Steve Ferguson, on behalf of Argentum, allegedly wire transferred from Argentum's Bank Leu account"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Layering is the practice of moving money from one CV's account to the next to disguise the money trail, separating illegally obtained funds from the crime and giving the funds an appearance of legitimacy. (p. 228)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Layering separates the illegally obtained funds from the crime by obscuring the trail of money through a complex web of fi nancial transactions."</sub>

## Investigation and Asset Recovery

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The scheme used CVs from several jurisdictions, including the Bahamas, Florida, Panama, Portugal and Trinidad and Tobago, which created investigative obstacles and required mutual legal assistance requests. (p. 228)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "CVs from a wide variety of jurisdictions including—but not limited to—the Bahamas; Florida, the United States; Panama; Portugal; and Trinidad and Tobago were employed in the scheme."</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Argentum, Bocora and Empresas were all incorporated in Panama, where the company registry does not collect legal or beneficial ownership information and sociedad anónimas need not disclose legal ownership on incorporation. (p. 228)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "for sociedad anónimas/corporations, legal ownership information does not need to be disclosed upon incorporation."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Panama's legislation sets no updating requirements for information submitted at incorporation, so no public source holds this information and it can only be obtained from the company itself. (p. 228)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In essence, no public source exists for this information; the only place to obtain this information from Panama is from the company."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The source states in its own voice that layering makes an investigation exponentially more costly, as it did in this case. (p. 228)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "For one, layering makes an investigation exponentially more costly—as was the case here."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Gutierrez pleaded guilty on March 19, 2007 and was ordered to pay US$22,556,100 in restitution. (p. 229)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Gutierrez was ordered to pay US$22,556,100 in restitution."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Diaz pleaded guilty on December 17, 2007 and was ordered to pay a fine of US$50,000; Hillman-Waller was ordered to pay US$2 million in restitution; Paz was ordered to pay US$489,618.06 in restitution for bank fraud. (p. 229)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "He was ordered to pay a fi ne of US$50,000."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Lacle was fined US$15,000 after pleading guilty to conspiracy to structure financial transactions; Mora was ordered to pay US$80,000 in restitution; Calmaquip pleaded guilty but the restitution amount is unclear. (p. 229)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Lacle was ordered to pay a fi ne of US$15,000."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Trinidad and Tobago government (RTT) brought a civil suit in Miami-Dade County against many of the same defendants plus Ronald Birk, Brian Kui Tung and other CVs, alleging improper dealings with contracts CP-3, CP-5 and CP-9; the suit was ongoing at the time of writing and Birk reportedly signed a plea deal to give evidence. (p. 229)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A civil suit was brought by the RTT against many of the same defendants in the Elev- enth Judicial Circuit Court for Miami-Dade County, Florida."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In total, the defendants in the US criminal case were ordered to pay more than US$25 million in restitution for their admitted guilt in the CP-13 contract. (p. 229)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In total, the defendants were ordered to pay more than US$25 million in restitution for their admitted guilt in the CP-13 contract."</sub>

## Case Study 10: Telecommunications D'Haiti

- **descriptive / context**: Between 2001 and 2005 officials at Haiti's state-owned Telecommunications D'Haiti accepted bribes and laundered funds through CVs; as sole provider of local telephone service, Haiti Teleco contracted with international telecom companies, whose US representatives paid bribes for reduced rates and credits. (p. 230)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "As the sole provider of local telephone service in Haiti, Haiti Teleco contracted with international telecommunications companies"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Bribes were funnelled through wire transfers and checks to intermediary shell companies and disguised as consulting services, commissions and vendor payments, although no such services were rendered; false invoice reference numbers were written on check memos to conceal the payments. (p. 230)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "although no such services were ever rendered."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Bribes were also reportedly paid to the director general of Haiti Teleco, and in July 2011 the US indicted former director general Patrick Joseph. (p. 230)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It had been mentioned that bribes were also paid to the director general of Haiti Teleco"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The US telecommunications companies paying bribes obtained preferential and reduced telecommunications rates and credits toward amounts owed, defrauding Haiti Teleco of revenue. (p. 230)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "in exchange for commercial advantages that included preferential and reduced telecommunications rates and credits toward amounts owed"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Robert Antoine, director of international relations at Haiti Teleco, received US$1,150,000 in bribes from three US telecom companies through intermediary shells, including JD Locator Services formed by codefendant Juan Diaz. (p. 231)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "he received US$1,150,000 in bribes from three U.S. telecommunications companies through intermediary shell companies,"</sub>

## A System of Corruption

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Funds from JD Locator were disbursed at Antoine's direction through wire transfers to his account, checks payable to him, cash withdrawals and transfers to family members. (p. 231)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "sending funds to Antoine's family members and others at his direction."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Fourcand Enterprises, a Florida company set up by Jean Fourcand, received incremental payments that were used to buy and sell real property, with proceeds transferred to Antoine through Fourcand's personal account. (p. 231)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Funds that accumulated in the Fourcand Enterprises account were collectively used to purchase real property,"</sub>

  <sub>anchor: "Diaz would cash checks typically in amounts no greater than US$10,000, thereby obviating his obligation to fi le Currency Transaction Reports"</sub>

## Investigation

- **descriptive / limitation**: Florida law does not require disclosure of legal or beneficial owners of a company to a public authority, either at incorporation or on an ongoing basis, as cited in the Telecom Consulting case. (p. 233)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "impose no obligation for the identity of the legal or benefi cial owners of the company to be disclosed to a public authority"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Bribe payments appeared legitimate in records; checks cashed by JD Locator were typically at or under US$10,000 so Currency Transaction Reports would not have been filed, and shells hid direct links between bribe-givers and takers. (p. 233)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "because the amounts of the checks cashed by JD Locator were each typically at or under US$10,000,"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The investigation succeeded at least in part through cooperation between US and Haitian authorities; evidence was obtained through requests under the Inter-American Convention Against Corruption, and Duperval was arrested in Haiti and expelled to the US. (p. 234)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Th e investigation was able to proceed successfully, at least in part because of eff ective cooperation between United States and Haitian authorities."</sub>

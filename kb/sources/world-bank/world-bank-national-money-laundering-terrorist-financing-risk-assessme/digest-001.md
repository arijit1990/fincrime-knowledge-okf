## 1.1. Context: Virtual Assets and Money Laundering (ML) and Terrorist Financing (TF) Risks

- **descriptive / context**: The excerpt states the global VA market reached a record valuation of $4 trillion in July 2025. VAs present characteristics such as pseudonymity, speed of cross-border transactions and technological complexity that challenge traditional regulatory and supervisory approaches. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "global VA market reaching a record valuation of $4 trillion in July 2025"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF has emphasized that jurisdictions need to assess and understand the financial crime risks of the VA sector. A comprehensive ML/TF risk assessment of VAs and VASPs enables a proportionate, risk-based regulatory response, deploying resources where most needed without unnecessarily hindering legitimate innovation. (p. 8)[^original]

## 1.2. The Risk Assessment Tool

- **descriptive / context**: The World Bank developed an updated VA and VASP risk assessment tool to help national authorities systematically identify and assess ML/TF risks of VAs and VASPs. Since 2022 it has supported over 20 jurisdictions of low and high capacity, and this experience informed the revision. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supported over 20 jurisdictions with both low and high capacities"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The revised tool has three tiers (basic, intermediate, advanced) so jurisdictions can use a version matching their needs, resources and VA/VASP sector maturity. It reflects greater data availability than in 2022, allowing more quantitative questions, and integrates new ML/TF techniques and typologies. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This revised tool has three tiers: basic, intermediate, and advanced."</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The tool is an Excel-based workbook with a multistep process: Step 1 Scoping and Mapping (clarify definitions, select module tier, collate data); Step 2 Threat Assessment (criminal or terrorist actors likely to use VAs/VASPs); Step 3 Vulnerability Assessment (inherent weaknesses of the VA/VASP ecosystem and supervisory/national context); Step 4 Assessment of Mitigation Measures (effectiveness of national controls); Step 5 Tailored VA and VASP Assessments (granular risks of specific VAs and services); Step 6 Analysis of Results and Action Plan (overall risk level and priority actions). (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The risk assessment tool is an Excel-based workbook that guides jurisdictions through a multistep process"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Jurisdictions that want to assess proliferation financing (PF) risks involving VAs should refer to the World Bank's separate methodology for PF risk assessments. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should refer to the World Bank’s separate methodology for PF risk assessments"</sub>

## 1.4. Key Definitions

- **recommended / recommendation**: Authorities are encouraged to maintain their own list of definitions relevant to international standards, national laws, guidance and implementing regulations. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Authorities are also encouraged to maintain their own list of definitions"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The tool uses the FATF definition of a virtual asset: a digital representation of value that can be digitally traded or transferred and used for payment or investment purposes, excluding digital representations of fiat currencies, securities and other financial assets covered elsewhere in the FATF Recommendations. The definition here includes VAs that may be considered securities or wider tokenized financial instruments when used for payment or investment purposes. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a digital representation of value that can be digitally traded, or transferred"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The tool uses the FATF VASP definition: any natural or legal person not covered elsewhere under the Recommendations that as a business conducts for or on behalf of another person: exchange between VAs and fiat; exchange between forms of VAs; transfer of VAs; safekeeping/administration of VAs or instruments enabling control over them; and participation in and provision of financial services related to an issuer's offer and/or sale of a VA. The tool lets jurisdictions scope in additional VA services and products as appropriate. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "provides flexibility for jurisdictions to scope in additional VA services and products as appropriate"</sub>

## 1.4. Key Definitions

- **descriptive / definition**: A domestic VASP is any VASP licensed or registered under the jurisdiction's law or regulations, and/or any company or individual based in the jurisdiction offering VA services on behalf of another person; it can include VASPs headquartered elsewhere that obtained a local license or registration. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Any VASP that is licensed or registered under the jurisdiction’s law or regulations"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: A foreign VASP with material local presence is a VASP that is not licensed, registered or legally established in the jurisdiction but has a material local presence, which can be determined from a market survey showing a material customer base or marketing targeting consumers in the jurisdiction. It may be regulated by a third jurisdiction or not at all. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A material local presence can be determined on the basis of a market survey"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: An ML/TF threat in the VA context refers to individuals or entities seeking to exploit VAs or related services to conceal illicit origin of funds or support terrorism. Examples: a cybercriminal extorting VAs from victims; an organized crime group converting cash proceeds into VAs; individuals converting funds into VAs to support terrorist fighters in another jurisdiction. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an ML/TF threat refers to individuals or entities seeking to exploit VAs or re- lated services"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: ML/TF vulnerabilities are factors making a jurisdiction, sector or service attractive for ML/TF that threat actors can exploit; some are inherent to a sector or product, others arise from national gaps. Examples: weak law enforcement capacity to trace VA proceeds; high-risk products provided by local VASPs. ML/TF risk is the potential for threats to exploit vulnerabilities within a jurisdiction or an organization's financial system. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Vulnerabilities refer to factors that make a jurisdiction, a sector, or a particular service attractive"</sub>

## 2.1. Working Group and Focal Point

- **expected / expectation**: Jurisdictions should nominate a designated focal point and lead agency to coordinate the VA risk assessment and should establish a multiagency working group, which is vital for coordination and accountability. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions should nominate a designated focal point and lead agency to coordinate the process"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The VA/VASP assessment can be part of a wider NRA (more holistic, drawing on latest national threat and vulnerability information) or standalone (may suit greater depth or a specific timeline). A dedicated VA working group should be formed in either case. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A dedicated working group on VAs should be formed in either scenario."</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: The focal point (e.g. an official in the national AML/CFT coordination body, the FIU or lead supervisor) coordinates the working group, oversees the process and timelines, manages quality control and documentation of findings, and is primary liaison with external stakeholders. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Serving as the primary liaison with external stakeholders"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The working group should draw on key national AML/CFT institutions (and regional/subnational where applicable) and generally should not exceed 50 representatives. Experience shows benefits of both public and private sector perspectives. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it generally should not exceed more than 50 representatives"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: Suggested public sector core agencies: VA/VASP supervisors if appointed; the FIU; law enforcement and intelligence agencies (ideally investigators experienced in VA-related ML, TF, sanctions evasion or predicate cases); Public Prosecutor's or Attorney General's Office; other financial supervisors and DNFBP supervisors; central bank; tax and revenue authority; Ministry of Justice or international cooperation agencies; anticorruption authority. Additional agencies: customs and subnational authorities. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Public sector core agencies"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: Final ratings should be decided collectively by the working group, though subtasks may be assigned; FIU and law enforcement typically focus on threats, supervisors on vulnerabilities. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The final decision on ratings should be taken collectively by the working group"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Jurisdictions should at a minimum seek private sector inputs at critical stages to validate findings: initial data collection, developing preliminary findings, and drawing final conclusions including follow-up recommendations. Whether to include private sector, civil society and academia in the formal group or through separate consultation depends on context. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions should ensure that they seek private sector inputs at critical stages"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: Relevant stakeholders include VA industry associations (exchangers, wallet providers, stablecoin issuers), blockchain analytics companies, traditional financial sector representatives, academics, civil society organizations, independent experts in AML/CFT, anticorruption or economic crime, and consumer protection or fraud victim support groups. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Blockchain analytics companies that can supply data on jurisdiction-specific trends"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Engagement with nongovernment stakeholders should ensure confidentiality of sensitive data, particularly law enforcement and intelligence information. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should ensure the confidentiality of sensitive data"</sub>

## 2.2. Training

- **expected / expectation**: All working group members should have sufficient understanding of VAs, VASP businesses, products and practices, and/or associated ML/TF risks. It may be preferable to include analyst- or investigator-level staff rather than management. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "All members should have a sufficient understanding of VAs"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Jurisdictions are encouraged to invest in targeted training covering VA ecosystem introductions, regulatory and supervisory frameworks for VASPs, best practices for supervision and enforcement, and case studies and typology reviews. They should consider whether domestic authorities, external providers or both deliver it, and reassess working group membership after training. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Jurisdictions are encouraged to invest in targeted training for working group members"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The World Bank offers training on VAs and the tool, covering the risk assessment process, navigating the tool, and best practices for entering data and interpreting outputs. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the World Bank offers training on virtual assets and the use of the risk assessment tool itself"</sub>

## 2.3. Substantive Scope of Risk Assessment

- **expected / expectation**: The working group should discuss objectives and scope, including the time period and the VA/VASP activities covered. Global AML/CFT requirements oblige jurisdictions to understand ML/TF risks of both VASPs and VA coins, services and products more broadly. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "oblige jurisdictions to understand their ML and TF risks related to both virtual asset service providers"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Jurisdictions would typically include in scope: cryptocurrencies (Bitcoin, Ether, Monero); stablecoins (pegged 1:1 with another asset, typically fiat, e.g. Tether, USD Coin, DAI); certain utility and governance tokens used for investment or payment (e.g. Basic Attention Token, Chainlink, depending on use and regulatory framework); NFTs used for payment or investment (inclusive approach may be taken given hybrid characteristics); and other VAs such as wrapped/bridged assets. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Stablecoins, which are virtual assets whose value is pegged 1:1 with another asset"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: Generally excluded from scope, though jurisdictions may still include them: central bank digital currencies, securities regulated under other legal frameworks, loyalty points and in-game assets not convertible to fiat, and tokenized deposits. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "generally excluded from the scope of ML/TF risk assessments"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The working group should adapt scope to local market context and clearly document and justify inclusion or exclusion of VA types, services or activities. In line with FATF guidance, jurisdictions should adopt a functional approach, deciding token scope by the function served in practice rather than terminology or marketing. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "jurisdictions should adopt a functional approach when deciding whether a certain token is within scope"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: The World Bank recommends that, at a minimum, jurisdictions assess ML/TF risks of all activities in the FATF VASP definition (exchange or transfer of VAs, safekeeping or administration, participation in and financial services related to an issuer's offer or sale of a VA). (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The World Bank recommends that, at a minimum, jurisdictions assess the ML/TF risks"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: The tool encourages jurisdictions to consider risks of broader VA services and actors, including mixing and tumbling services that pool VA transactions and obscure the trail to the original source, and the interaction between VAs, VASPs and traditional financial institutions, including the extent to which banks service VASP clients or clients trading VAs. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "encourages jurisdictions to consider the interaction between VAs, VASPs, and traditional financial institutions"</sub>

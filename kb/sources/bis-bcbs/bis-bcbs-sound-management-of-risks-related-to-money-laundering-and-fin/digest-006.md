## B.3 Assessment of natural persons

- **expected / expectation**: Annex 5 paragraph 16: the prudential supervisor, generally responsible for fit and proper assessment of shareholders (including ultimate beneficial owners and others with significant influence), senior management and board members at authorisation and on an ongoing basis, should consult the AML/CFT supervisor for additional information to ensure these persons have a record of integrity and good repute, in particular that a bank is not controlled or owned by persons with links to significant ML/FT risks (per Core Principle 5 essential criterion 7 and the Corporate governance principles for banks). (p. 52)[^original]

## Box 1: Example of practice: fit and proper assessment

- **descriptive / case example**: In some jurisdictions, an individual or legal person being under investigation, indictment, enforcement action or prosecution for money laundering, sanctions violations or similar offences could lead the supervisor to seek information on the underlying facts; depending on the facts and legal framework this could mean conditional approval of an application, preventing an institution or individuals from certain banking activities (establishing or acquiring a bank, serving as executive management, new activities outside the approved business model), or in some cases denial of approval without prejudice to other authorities' investigations. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could lead to a conditional approval of an application or to preventing an institution"</sub>

## C. Cooperation and exchange of information in ongoing supervision

- **expected / expectation**: Annex 5 paragraph 17: weaknesses or breaches in AML/CFT compliance could have significant legal, regulatory, criminal, reputational and financial impacts and may affect prudential analysis of soundness; the prudential supervisor should therefore take ML/FT risks into account by collecting and considering all relevant information identified by AML/CFT supervisors, paying particular attention to risks from cross-border banking group structure. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the prudential supervisor should take into account the impact of the ML/FT risks by collecting and considering all relevant information"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: Paragraph 17(1) examples of ML/FT impact on assessment of risk management, internal controls and governance: (a) personnel awareness of AML/CFT within corporate culture, particularly compliance records and transparency and behaviour toward supervisors and FIUs; (b) quality of AML/CFT information to management and the board, including major breaches, criminal or administrative sanctions and risk indicators; (c) independence and capacity of the AML/CFT risk management and internal control functions; (d) new adverse facts about shareholders (including beneficial owners), management body members and qualifying holders. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the quality of the information given to the management and board of directors of the bank"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: Paragraph 17(2): ML/FT risks could impact soundness of the business model, for which information on transactions, business relationships and establishment in higher-risk countries could be relevant. (p. 53)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "information on transactions, business relationships and establishment in higher-risk countries could be relevant"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: Paragraph 17(3)-(4): ML/FT risks could affect operational risk (information on deficiencies in IT systems used to manage ML/FT risks could raise awareness of broader IT issues) and liquidity risk (information on deposits from higher ML/FT risk countries or suspension of settlement services access due to ML/FT issues could be relevant). (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "information on deposits received from countries with higher ML/FT risks or suspension of access to settlement services"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: Paragraph 18: AML/CFT supervisor information could indicate weaknesses in prudential components (risk management, internal controls, governance); examples of relevant sources are (1) AML/CFT priority topics; (2) ML/FT risk assessments with components linked to a prudential component; (3) scheduled AML/CFT on-site examinations and prior outcomes; (4) relevant AML/CFT reports from internal and external sources; (5) AML/CFT supervisory measures (measures against board or senior management could affect governance assessment, activity limits the business model, financial penalties capital or liquidity); (6) whistleblower ML/FT alerts (see Corporate governance principles for banks paragraph 32). (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "alerts related to ML/FT issues reported by whistleblowers"</sub>

## Box 2: Example of practices - AML/CFT report

- **descriptive / case example**: Some jurisdictions have required banks to prepare an AML/CFT risk assessment report and internal control report transmitted to senior management and AML/CFT supervisors, giving information on ML/FT risks and mitigation; it could be shared with prudential supervisors when relevant, ie if a specific AML/CFT issue or increased AML/CFT risk has been identified for an institution. (p. 54)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Some jurisdictions have required banks to prepare an AML/CFT risk assessment report and internal control report"</sub>

## Box 2: Impact of AML/CFT supervisor's assessment

- **descriptive / case example**: Some jurisdictions have laws requiring prudential supervisors to incorporate an explicit assessment of a bank's AML/CFT programme in supervisory decisions such as reviewing applications; there, less than satisfactory AML programmes may affect a bank's ability to expand, merge or acquire, so information exchange on the AML/CFT assessment could be useful in licensing and ongoing supervision. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AML programmes that are less than satisfactory may affect a bank’s ability to expand, merge or acquire"</sub>

## C. Cooperation and exchange of information in ongoing supervision

- **descriptive / expectation**: Paragraph 19(1) examples of prudential information relevant to AML/CFT supervisors on governance and risk management: (a) ownership and corporate structure and the complexity and transparency of organisation; (b) deficiencies in governance and management oversight, where lack of involvement of risk management, control and compliance functions in important managerial decisions could signal AML/CFT function weaknesses; (c) prudential measures on the governance framework and rejections of qualified holdings, proposed senior managers or board members on operational and reputational risk grounds; (d) concerns on integrity and good repute of senior managers, management body members and significant shareholders (including beneficial owners). (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a lack of involvement of the risk management, control and compliance functions in important managerial decisions could signal weaknesses"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: Paragraph 19(2)-(3): prudential information on the business model (including measures such as restrictions to operations, eg establishment or provision of certain services) and on operational risks (deficiencies in IT systems or lines of defence; footnote 77 refers to the three lines of defence in the June 2011 Principles for the sound management of operational risk) could be relevant for AML/CFT supervision. (p. 55)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Prudential information on operational risks such as deficiencies in a bank’s IT system or lines of defence77"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: Paragraph 20: prudential information relevant to AML/CFT supervisors can come from (1) on-site examinations, off-site surveillance and enforcement actions related to ML/FT risks, and (2) information gathered during authorisation, licensing or passporting related to ML/FT risks. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "information gathered during the authorisation, licensing or passporting process that is related to ML/FT risks"</sub>

## Box 3: Example of practices - Practice concerning access to sources

- **descriptive / case example**: In some jurisdictions where AML/CFT and prudential supervision are by the same authority, AML/CFT examiners have access to current and historical information and documents about examinations by the prudential supervisor. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AML/CFT examiners have access to current and historical information and documents about examinations conducted by the prudential supervisor"</sub>

## Box 3: Prudential-AML/CFT coordinated examination processes

- **descriptive / case example**: In some countries where one authority conducts both supervisions, examiners with various specialties including AML/CFT can be on-site at once, examining safety and soundness and AML/CFT compliance together and evaluating policies, procedures and processes jointly. A merger review is a common example: concurrent review might highlight dissimilar business models, credit underwriting standards and customer types, and an acquirer may lack experience or infrastructure to monitor acquired customer types for suspicious activity; without both disciplines some details could be overlooked. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The acquiring bank may be unfamiliar with certain products and services acquired"</sub>

## D. Cooperation and exchange of information regarding enforcement actions

- **descriptive / context**: Annex 5 paragraph 21: this section establishes principles for sharing information on enforcement actions or sanctions between prudential and AML/CFT supervisors at domestic and international levels. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This section establishes principles for sharing information on enforcement actions or sanctions"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Communication where appropriate should include sufficient detail on the nature of the AML/CFT deficiencies for each supervisor to assess impact on the supervised institution, and should occur as early and often as possible to manage potential impact on financial stability (eg capital adequacy and liquidity concerns, revocation of licences or authorisations, termination of deposit insurance, significant curtailment of activities). (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It should occur as early and often as possible in order to manage the potential impact of the enforcement action"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Annex 5 paragraph 23: for this guideline, "enforcement action" or "sanctions" means the range of civil or administrative actions, often public, imposed on financial institutions by supervisors for significant non-compliance with AML/CFT requirements; it excludes actions by law enforcement authorities for criminal violations or by non-bank supervisory government agencies (footnote 79: authorities with AML/CFT-related enforcement powers that are not bank supervisors) and is separate from FATF "asset seizures and freezes" or "targeted financial sanctions". (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the range of civil or administrative actions, often public, imposed on financial institutions by supervisors"</sub>

## Prudential-AML/CFT coordinated examination processes

- **descriptive / case example**: A common example of beneficial synergy between prudential and AML/CFT supervisors is review of a merger between two institutions: a concurrent review might highlight dissimilar business models, distinct credit underwriting standards and different customer types. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A concurrent review might highlight dissimilar business models, distinct credit underwriting standards and different customer types."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In the merger example, the acquiring bank may be unfamiliar with certain products and services it acquired, and may lack the experience or infrastructure (technology or staff) to adequately monitor the different customer types for suspicious activity; without both disciplines discussing the merger, some important details could be overlooked. (p. 56)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The acquiring bank may be unfamiliar with certain products and services acquired"</sub>

## D. Cooperation and exchange of information regarding enforcement actions

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 23(1)-(3): in some countries enforcement actions or sanctions can include civil or administrative money penalties, corrective action and supervisory actions curtailing banking activities; some supervisors may assess penalties against bank insiders and remove and prohibit individuals from participating in a bank's affairs (including employment); sanctions can affect ability to engage in activities and expand since AML/CFT effectiveness may be expressly required to be considered in banking applications and licensing, and in some jurisdictions supervisors may consider revoking a banking charter/licence, terminating deposit insurance, or appointing an administrator or receiver. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the revocation of an institution’s banking charter/licence or termination of the institution’s deposit insurance"</sub>

## Box 4: Example of practice - enforcement action information exchange in the cross-border context

- **descriptive / case example**: In some host jurisdictions, prudential supervisors share with foreign home supervisors information about AML/CFT compliance deficiencies and possible consequences, then follow up with pending enforcement action information once imposition is imminent; they may also share, as able and appropriate, information about possible non-supervisory (eg criminal) sanctions and consequences such as impact on the bank's future expansion and acquisitions. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "prudential supervisors share information with their foreign home supervisors about deficiencies in AML/CFT compliance"</sub>

## E.1 Channels of cooperation and information exchange at the jurisdictional and international level

- **expected / expectation**: Annex 5 paragraph 24: supervisors should establish and maintain official channels to structure ongoing dialogue, information exchange and cooperation between prudential and AML/CFT supervisors at jurisdictional and international level and use them effectively at relevant supervisory stages; examples are bilateral or multilateral exchanges with or without an MoU, prudential supervisory colleges and dedicated AML/CFT colleges. (p. 57)[^original]

## E.1 Channels of cooperation and information exchange at the jurisdictional and international level

- **expected / expectation**: Annex 5 paragraph 26: at the international level, for banks operating across borders, prudential and AML/CFT supervisory authorities should put in place and maintain mechanisms to ensure timely and effective cross-border exchange of information and cooperation. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should put in place and maintain mechanisms to ensure the exchange of information and cooperation across borders"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Annex 5 paragraph 26: to that end, supervisors should consider taking into account the mechanisms described in paragraph 25(2), and supervisory authorities should consider whether prudential colleges, AML/CFT colleges (where provided for), bilateral relationships, or a combination of these, effectively meets their supervisory needs. (p. 57)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supervisory authorities should consider whether the use of either, or a combination of, colleges of prudential supervisors"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Annex 5 paragraph 25: at the jurisdictional level, supervisors should consider the listed examples when putting in place mechanisms for timely and effective exchange; (1) where both are in the same authority, internal processes should clearly establish when different teams need to work together and exchange information, proactively and on request, while maintaining each function's independence. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "internal processes should clearly establish, when the teams in charge are different, the need to work together and exchange information"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Paragraph 25(2): where supervisors are in different authorities, both should consider an Agreement or MoU covering, for information exchange: type of information that can or should be exchanged; situations where a request may be refused; modalities (means of communication, language); confidentiality and data protection (acceptable uses and onward transmission to third parties at home or abroad); and legal basis. Footnote 80 notes the ECB and EEA AML/CFT authorities have signed an agreement including most of these items. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "situations where the request for information may be refused"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Paragraph 25(2)(b)-(c): the MoU should also cover, for cooperation: areas and purpose of cooperation; a mechanism to agree how to cooperate where several authorities want to investigate the same entity and one investigation might prejudice another (including which authority takes priority or leads and when); and legal basis; plus (c) a mechanism to attempt to resolve disagreements among authorities as necessary. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a mechanism to agree on the manner to cooperate effectively in cases where several authorities"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Paragraph 26(1): prudential colleges can discuss prudential implications of AML/CFT issues in meetings or AML/CFT substructures, creating a clear link between supervisors; to support informed decision-making they should consider inviting AML/CFT supervisors (eg the lead supervisor of the AML/CFT college) as members or observers, avoiding AML/CFT issues being considered only after ML/FT risks have crystallised. (p. 58)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "To support informed decision-making, prudential colleges should consider inviting"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 26(2): AML/CFT colleges, where provided for, give a formal framework for cooperation and information exchange among AML/CFT supervisors, helping identify and mitigate ML/FT risks of banks operating in multiple jurisdictions before they crystallise; to be effective they have to build strong links with prudential supervisors and prudential colleges so relevant ML/FT and prudential risk information is exchanged and acted on timely; prudential supervisors could be members or invited observers, and colleges might improve AML/CFT supervision of international groups while recognising national specificities. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AML/CFT colleges nevertheless have to build strong links with prudential supervisors and their prudential college counterparts"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 26(3): where college mechanisms are not yet in place, prudential and AML/CFT supervisors should put in place and maintain mechanisms for timely and efficient bilateral cross-border information exchange and cooperation, and should consider referring to the paragraph 25(2) mechanisms. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should put in place and maintain mechanisms to ensure the bilateral exchange of information and cooperation on a cross-border basis"</sub>

## Box 5: Example of practice - AML/CFT supervisory colleges

- **descriptive / case example**: EU AML/CFT and prudential legislation obliges competent authorities to cooperate and exchange information but does not detail how, so cooperation can sometimes be difficult; the ESAs issued Guidelines on supervisory cooperation and information exchange in December 2019 laying down rules on establishing and operating AML/CFT colleges, which serve as a forum for collaboration, support common understanding of ML/FT risks of a bank and allow supervisors to adopt a common approach and coordinated actions. (p. 59)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the European Supervisory Authorities (ESAs) issued Guidelines on supervisory cooperation and information exchange in December 2019"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: The ESAs Guidelines provide that AML/CFT colleges be set up for all banks and financial institutions operating in at least three EU member states; all EU AML/CFT supervisors involved are permanent members; EU prudential supervisors and AML/CFT supervisors of non-EU countries where the institution operates are invited as observers; non-EU prudential supervisors and the FIU of the lead supervisor's member state may be invited as observers as appropriate; FIUs from other jurisdictions and other relevant persons may be invited ad hoc. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AML/CFT colleges be set up for all banks and financial institutions that operate in at least three EU member states"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Under the ESAs Guidelines, all observers must be subject to confidentiality rules equivalent to those in the EU and are expected to participate actively, including exchanging information within the college; observers that are prudential supervisors are further expected to take action to ensure information from AML/CFT college meetings is shared with prudential colleges and acted upon as appropriate. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "All observers have to be subject to confidentiality rules equivalent to those in force in the EU"</sub>

## E.2 Processes of cooperation and information exchange

- **expected / expectation**: Annex 5 paragraph 27: prudential and AML/CFT supervisors should exchange information periodically and when necessary as relevant for their tasks, taking into account the mechanisms in paragraph 25(1) and 25(2) as appropriate. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should exchange information periodically and when necessary as relevant for their respective tasks"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Annex 5 paragraph 28: on an information request, the requested supervisor should determine whether the information is available and can be provided under applicable laws; (1) if the request cannot be fulfilled in part or whole (eg information unavailable or fulfilment would breach laws), the requested supervisor should cooperate to the fullest extent possible, consider other assistance, inform the requester of its decision, explain why it cannot exchange and consider advising how else it might assist. (p. 60)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the requested supervisor should cooperate to the fullest extent possible and consider whether it can provide other assistance"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 28(2): where a request can be fulfilled, the supervisor should endeavour to exchange the information within the time set in the request; if that is impossible or excessively burdensome, it should offer an alternative delivery date that both parties can agree. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should offer an alternative delivery date on which the requesting and requested parties can agree"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Annex 5 paragraph 29: prudential and AML/CFT supervisors should consider whether, and in which situations, to cooperate, for example by coordinated or joint inspections, which could take place among supervisors of the same jurisdiction and among home and host supervisors under cooperation arrangements and applicable laws. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should consider whether, and if so, in which situations, to cooperate, for example by carrying out coordinated or joint inspections"</sub>
- **expected / recommendation**: Annex 5 paragraph 30: when deciding whether to cooperate, supervisors should consider (1) the nature and level of ML/FT risk associated with the bank; (2) the focus of the proposed cooperation, including compliance with specific legal or regulatory provisions such as customer due diligence or suspicious transaction reporting requirements, or specific identified ML/FT risks; and (3) availability and planned allocation of supervisory resources. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the availability and planned allocation of supervisory resources"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Annex 5 paragraph 31: when deciding to cooperate, supervisors should consider setting out in writing, at a minimum: (1) which authority coordinates actions, if appropriate; (2) an action plan including the nature and type of actions by each supervisor, timing, and modalities of information exchange including sharing information gathered during and as a result of the cooperation; and (3) options for coordinated follow-up, if any, including cooperation on an enforcement action where applicable. (p. 61)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the options for coordinated follow-up, if any, including, where applicable, cooperation in relation to an enforcement action"</sub>

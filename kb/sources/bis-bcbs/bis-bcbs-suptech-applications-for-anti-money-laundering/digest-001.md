## Executive summary

- **descriptive / definition**: The FSI Insights paper (No 18, August 2019) defines suptech collectively as the use by financial authorities of advanced data collection and analytics tools enabled by new technologies; in data analytics, development has been helped by advances in artificial intelligence and its practical applications in machine learning, natural language processing and other advanced analytics. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Use by financial authorities of advanced data collection and analytics tools enabled by new technologies is collectively called suptech."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The paper states that detecting potential AML/CFT violations is one field where data analytics tools seem more advanced, and it covers nine AML/CFT authorities that have supervision or financial intelligence functions, or both, exploring the tools they use and their practical experience. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Nine AML/CFT authorities are covered in this paper."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: AML supervisory authorities are expected to ensure financial institutions comply with requirements to combat money laundering and terrorist financing, whereas financial intelligence units (FIUs) are expected to be national centres for receiving and analysing suspicious transaction reports and other ML-relevant information and for disseminating the results; FIUs sometimes also have AML supervision functions. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AML/CFT supervision and financial intelligence functions have different mandates."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Both AML/CFT supervisors and FIUs need advanced data analytics tools to analyse large volumes of information; they typically receive substantial transactional and non-transactional data, some collaborate with other government agencies and private entities to widen available data, and some are exploring non-traditional sources such as newspaper articles and social media. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AML/CFT authorities typically receive substantial amounts of transactional and non-transactional data."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The paper finds that the difference in mandates does not seem to affect the types of advanced analytics tools pursued: the authorities covered pursue similar tools such as network analysis, natural language processing, text mining and machine learning, which increase their ability to detect networks of related transactions, identify unusual behaviours and turn large amounts of structured and unstructured data into useful information. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The difference in mandates does not seem to affect the types of advanced data analytics tools"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Authorities have used different strategies to develop these tools: those within a central bank, or prudential or conduct authority, generally benefit from an institutional strategy to use innovative technology and can build solutions in-house; for some, ready market solutions may be more efficient; others collaborate with the academic community; many combine approaches. The optimal solution depends on the authority's profile, the characteristics of the financial system it oversees and its legal framework. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The optimal solution for a specific authority will depend on several factors"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Efficiency gains seem to be the number one benefit of advanced data analytics tools for capacity-constrained AML/CFT authorities; authorities highlighted time savings, which could allow reallocation of resources from manual work to more judgment-based work. Assessing effectiveness, particularly of tools used by FIUs, is not straightforward. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Efficiency gains seem to be the number one benefit of advanced data analytics tools"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The paper identifies four challenges in using these technologies: computational capacity given large data volumes; data privacy and confidentiality requirements that constrain use of certain data and external resources; difficulty assessing effectiveness, especially for FIUs because of the time needed to prove money laundering; and loss of effectiveness over time of supervised machine learning tools if not regularly updated with new training data, because criminal organisations change behaviour to avoid detection. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "tools based on supervised machine learning could lose their effectiveness over time"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The executive summary says the benefits of these suptech tools are especially important for jurisdictions heavily affected by the unintended consequences of international AML/CFT standards, particularly de-risking. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "particularly important for jurisdictions that have been heavily impacted by the unintended consequences of AML/CFT international standards"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The executive summary observes that the jurisdictions global correspondent banks exit most often seem to be those whose AML/CFT supervisory and regulatory frameworks are weak. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Jurisdictions most frequently exited by global correspondent banks seem to be those with weak AML/CFT supervisory and regulatory frameworks."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The executive summary says that developing advanced data analytics tools, together with the necessary skills, could help strengthen the frameworks of those jurisdictions and potentially reverse the correspondent-bank exit trend. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could help strengthen these jurisdictions’ frameworks and potentially reverse this trend"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The paper says there is scope for information-sharing among AML/CFT authorities on analytics tools to promote peer learning: although tools are tweaked to reflect mandates, the underlying methodologies are quite similar, so regular exchange of information and experiences offers opportunities for peer learning. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There is scope for information-sharing among AML/CFT authorities on the data analytics tools"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Authorities just starting to develop their data infrastructure have a 'late mover' advantage: without legacy systems they can design seamless data collection, validation and management processes and more easily integrate newly developed analytical tools. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "have a “late mover” advantage and may find it easier to integrate advanced data analytics tools"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Because ML/TF risks have international reach and all tools discussed in the paper are national in scope, the paper says development of data analytics tools that are international in scope should be considered; criminal organisations exploit loopholes anywhere, so a strong argument could be made for international cooperation and collaboration. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "development of data analytics tools that are international in scope should be considered"</sub>

## Section 1 – Introduction

- **descriptive / definition**: Paragraph 1 defines suptech as the use by financial authorities of advanced data collection and analytics tools enabled by new technologies, with analytics development facilitated by AI and its applications in machine learning and natural language processing (NLP), which have provided opportunities to enhance authorities' capacity. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These tools have provided opportunities to enhance financial authorities’ capacity."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 2 notes that suptech data analytics tools appear in market surveillance, misconduct analysis, microprudential supervision and macroprudential supervision, and that detecting potential AML/CFT violations is a field where misconduct-analysis tools are used and seem more advanced. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Detecting potential AML/CFT violations is one field where data analytics tools for misconduct analysis are used"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Paragraph 3 cites one estimate (LexisNexis Risk Solutions 2018) that AML/CFT compliance cost by financial firms is USD 25.3 billion per year for US financial services firms alone; it says many AML/CFT authorities, particularly in emerging market and developing economies, have severe capacity constraints, and that progress globally in reducing ML/TF risks seems slow. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "USD 25.3 billion per year for US financial services firms alone."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 3 reports the Financial Stability Board noted that jurisdictions most frequently exited by global correspondent banks seem to be those with weak AML/CFT supervisory and regulatory frameworks, with implications for international trade and remittance flows to those jurisdictions. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Financial Stability Board (FSB), for example, noted that jurisdictions most frequently exited by global correspondent banks"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 4 says AML/CFT authorities typically receive substantial transactional data from reporting institutions, supplemented by non-transactional data (for example from tax, customs and property registration authorities) for context; some collaborate with other agencies and private entities, some explore non-traditional sources such as newspaper articles and social media, and big data analytics enables integration of these sources. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supplemented by non-transactional data (eg data from tax, customs and property registration authorities) to provide context"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 5 states that rule-based AML/CFT measures traditionally relied on by financial institutions are shown to generate false positive alerts of around 90-95%, with substantial resource implications; financial institutions are increasingly using machine learning to reduce false positives significantly, and similar improvements could be expected for AML/CFT authorities using advanced analytics (source cited: Saaradeey et al 2019). (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "false positive alerts of around 90–95% leading to substantial resource implications."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 2 states that Broeders and Prenio (2018) define suptech as the use of innovative technology by supervisory agencies to support supervision, and that this paper broadens the definition to include advanced data collection and analytics tools used by other financial authorities such as FIUs; footnote 7 says 'AML/CFT authorities' in the paper encompasses both AML/CFT supervisors and FIUs. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "defines suptech as the use of innovative technology by supervisory agencies to support supervision."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 3 states that suptech tools have the potential to contribute significantly to reducing ML and TF risks by strengthening jurisdictions' AML/CFT frameworks. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "has the potential to significantly contribute to reducing money laundering (ML) and terrorist financing (TF) risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 3 cites one estimate of AML/CFT compliance cost by financial firms at USD 25.3 billion per year for US financial services firms alone. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "One estimate of AML/CFT compliance cost by financial firms put it at USD 25.3 billion per year"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 3 states that, despite these efforts, estimates of money laundered worldwide are still staggering, and that progress globally in reducing ML/TF risks seems slow, with wide-ranging economic and social repercussions. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Despite these efforts, estimates of money laundered worldwide are still staggering."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 3 reports that the FSB noted jurisdictions most frequently exited by global correspondent banks seem to be those with weak AML/CFT supervisory and regulatory frameworks, which has implications for international trade and remittance flows to those jurisdictions. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This has implications for international trade and remittance flows to these jurisdictions."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 6 says existing information is scarce but a number of advanced analytics tools are being pursued by AML/CFT authorities, as glimpsed in papers (Kraft 2018, di Castri et al 2018), authority publications (AUSTRAC 2018) and the Broeders and Prenio (2018) overview showing many authorities actively exploring such tools. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "While existing information remains scarce, there are already a number of advanced data analytics tools"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 7 states that international dialogue on use of advanced analytics by AML/CFT authorities seems limited (Kraft 2018), which inhibits peer learning; it notes that the Egmont Group has an Information Exchange Working Group with a workstream on enhancing data analytics and effective communications, aiming at 'embracing innovation in technology' so FIUs can be agile, responsive and effective when exchanging and exploiting information. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Egmont Group has an Information Exchange Working Group with a workstream on enhancing data analytics"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Paragraph 8 describes the paper's approach: nine AML/CFT authorities were selected based on information available about their analytics work or on recommendations from other authorities; most were interviewed and publicly available information was used for a few. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These authorities were selected based on information available about their data analytics work"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 9 says countries organise AML/CFT authorities differently: AML/CFT supervision may sit within the central bank, a prudential or conduct authority, or the FIU, while the financial intelligence function may be a standalone entity, an agency attached to but independent from the central bank, or part of law enforcement. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Countries organise their AML/CFT authorities in different ways."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 10 says FATF Recommendation 27 expects AML/CFT supervisors to supervise or monitor, and ensure compliance by, financial institutions with requirements to combat money laundering and terrorist financing, and Recommendation 29 expects FIUs to serve as national centres for receipt and analysis of suspicious transaction reports and other relevant information and for dissemination of the results of that analysis (footnote 11). (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FATF Recommendations 27 and 29, respectively."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 11 outlines the paper's structure: section 2 covers data-related challenges of AML/CFT authorities, section 3 covers specific analytics tools plus how authorities develop them and the issues they face, and section 4 gives concluding thoughts. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Section 3 dives into the specific data analytics tools that AML/CFT authorities use to address these challenges."</sub>

## Section 2 – Data-related challenges faced by AML/CFT authorities

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 12 states that AML/CFT authorities analyse data to identify, assess and understand ML/TF risks in their jurisdictions, citing FATF Recommendation 1 which states countries should identify, assess and understand their ML and TF risks; these ongoing assessments inform changes to AML/CFT laws, risk-based prioritisation of work and resource allocation, and serve as input to assessments by financial institutions and designated non-financial entities. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FATF recommendation 1 states that countries should identify, assess and understand their money laundering and terrorist financing risks."</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Paragraph 13 explains that FIUs receive suspicious transaction reports (STRs) combining structured information (for example amount and entities involved) and unstructured information (for example reasons for suspicion), whereas AML/CFT supervisors in general do not have access to STRs and tend to look at transactional information above a threshold (for example wire transfers above a certain amount) or aggregated transactional data on certain counterparties or jurisdictions. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In general, AML/CFT supervisors do not have access to STRs"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Paragraph 13 adds that authorities also use non-transactional information to give context to transactions, such as information on politically exposed persons, countries on sanctions lists, property or business ownership, and business relationships. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "information relating to politically exposed persons, countries in sanctions lists, property or business ownership, and business relationships"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Paragraph 14 gives examples of authorities going beyond traditional data: CNBV and UIF use information obtained systematically from press articles to enhance monitoring; CNBV also uses social media and a customer database of financial institutions' clients including know-your-customer questionnaire data and client risk rating; FIC created an open source analysis capability and uses open sources to enhance monitoring and intelligence products. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIC has created an open source analysis capability"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Paragraph 15 describes collaboration platforms: the Fintel Alliance, created in Australia in 2017, brings together government and private-sector members (domestic and international) to share and analyse financial intelligence, aiming to build the financial sector's resilience against criminal exploitation and support law enforcement investigations into serious crime and national security matters. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In 2017, the Fintel Alliance was created in Australia to combat money laundering, terrorism financing and other serious crime."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Paragraph 15 also reports that in the Netherlands a joint venture among public entities (DNB, police, prosecutors, tax authority, customs) and data providers such as the Chamber of Commerce and the Land Registry shares data from different sources and gives each participant access to the large combined data set. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In the Netherlands, a joint venture among a number of public entities"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Paragraph 16 gives data volumes: UIF receives about 100,000 STRs per year and about 100 million records of the SARA database (anonymous aggregated transactions above EUR 15,000); FINTRAC receives almost 180,000 STRs, over 10 million large cash transactions and over 14 million electronic funds transfer reports per year; FIC receives more than 330,000 STRs and over 4.8 million cash threshold and cash threshold aggregation reports per year. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "UIF receives about 100,000 STRs per year and about 100 million records of the SARA database"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Paragraph 17 says large data volumes create collection and management challenges: CNBV's data collection is still a time-consuming and inefficient manual operation, so it is running a project with the Regtech for Regulators Accelerator (R2A) to let institutions submit reports digitally and automatically, with central data storage and automatic visualisation tools; smart contracts are also being explored, for example at AUSTRAC for reporting international funds transfer instructions. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The existing data collection process at CNBV is still a timeconsuming and inefficient manual operation."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 18 notes data quality can be a challenge because most data come from supervised entities' reports and those entities range from big global banks to very small entities with widely varying reporting capabilities. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Supervised entities range from big global banks to very small entities, with widely varying reporting capabilities."</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Paragraph 18 states authorities have set up automatic data-quality checks that return data to the supervised entity if necessary; validations can be embedded in automatic collection (as CNBV and AUSTRAC are developing), and DNB is standardising received data and will automatically compare it with data sent by peers to check consistency in data volume and other dimensions. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "DNB, on the other hand, is working on the standardisation of data received"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Paragraph 19 says matching data from different databases can be a problem, for example in Australia where there is no national ID system and each agency keeps its own database; AUSTRAC is running data matching projects with other agencies and with private entities in the Fintel Alliance, and in Europe FIU.NET (a decentralised network supporting EU FIUs) has a project matching data from different FIUs when they exchange information. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "where there is no national ID system and so each government agency maintains its own database"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 20 concludes that automating data collection and validation addresses some challenges, but advanced data analytics tools are still necessary to analyse large volumes of information from disparate sources and obtain a complete and coherent picture of AML/CFT risk. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "use of advanced data analytics tools is still necessary to enable the analysis of large volumes of information"</sub>

## Section 3 – Data analytics tools

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 21 states that AML/CFT supervisors and FIUs use similar tools (network analysis, machine learning, natural language processing and text mining) with different objectives, to detect networks of related transactions, identify unusual behaviours and convert large amounts of structured and unstructured data into useful information. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AML/CFT supervisors and FIUs use similar innovative data analytical tools to achieve their objectives."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 22 says supervisors' advanced analytics mainly target improving compliance assessment of individual institutions; supervisors must assess governance, risk management, internal controls, processes and systems, and this integrity risk assessment combines onsite and offsite activities. Their tools seek to enhance the offsite assessment of institutions' risk profiles, which also contributes to risk-based prioritisation of onsite inspections. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "seek to enhance the offsite assessment of the risk profile of individual institutions"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 23 says FIUs focus primarily on identifying suspicious activity at the micro level (individual transactions or networks, supporting intelligence and law enforcement; visualisation and automation tools belong here) and at the macro level (monitoring trends and patterns to improve analysis and raise market participants' awareness of relevant typologies). (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FIUs seek to identify suspicious activity in individual or networks of transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 23 says data analytics help FIUs in two main ways: assessing the likelihood of money laundering activity to prioritise work, and detecting trends and patterns in potential ML/TF activity, which could decrease FIUs' reaction time to a changing environment, important because criminals constantly seek new ways to launder proceeds. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they could decrease the reaction time of FIUs to a changing environment"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Figure 2 shows the objectives of the tools: for AML supervision authorities, identification of networks, compliance with AML requirements and risk scoring; for financial intelligence units, detection of networks, assessment of the likelihood of money laundering and identification of patterns and trends. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Figure 2 – Objectives of the tools employed by AML supervisors and FIUs"</sub>

## Risk scoring of supervised entities

- **descriptive / method**: Paragraph 24 says advanced analytics may be used to assess the overall risk of financial institutions, with tools typically assigning each institution a rating based on likelihood of non-compliance with AML/CFT requirements. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "tools typically assign a rating to each financial institution depending on the likelihood of noncompliance"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: FINTRAC has developed a heuristic model using risk factors on an institution's profile, compliance history, reporting behaviour and other intelligence, determined by methods such as principal component analysis and geospatial analysis (for example closeness to borders) and based on large data (STRs, wire transfer reports, large cash transactions, casino disbursements) from reporting entities and other sources; the model ranks institutions by likelihood of non-compliance. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "FINTRAC, for example, has developed a heuristic model that uses several risk factors"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: FINTRAC also develops a challenger model each year, using a combination of machine learning and heuristic methods, to compare the performance of the production model and update it if required. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A challenger model is also developed each year using a combination of machine learning and heuristic methods"</sub>

## Identification of networks to which financial institutions are exposed

- **descriptive / method**: Paragraph 25 says several supervisory authorities use network analysis to assess supervised entities' exposure to ML risk; building networks is a means to assess compliance, not a goal in itself; results normally help prioritise onsite examinations and are sometimes shared with FIUs and law enforcement. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The construction of networks is not a goal in itself but the means to assess the compliance"</sub>

## Box 1 – DNB application of network analysis using transactional data

- **descriptive / case example**: Box 1: DNB has a tool using transactional data to detect networks of related entities sending funds to the same counterparties in high-risk countries via different financial institutions (money transfer offices); the outcomes and existing links to these networks feed the assessment of institutions' risk profiles and the allocation of supervisory resources. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "detect networks of related entities sending funds to the same counterparties in high-risk countries"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Box 1 reports that the DNB tool is operational, its results have already been used in a number of police operations, and it even led to the suspension of the licence of one financial institution. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "led to the suspension of the licence of one financial institution"</sub>

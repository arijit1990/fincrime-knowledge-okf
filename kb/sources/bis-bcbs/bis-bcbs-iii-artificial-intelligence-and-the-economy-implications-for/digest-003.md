## Nowcasting with artificial intelligence

- **descriptive / context**: The chapter states that AI could play an important role in supporting financial stability analysis, because machine learning and AI methods are strongest at identifying patterns in a cross-section. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The strongest suit of machine learning and AI methodologies is identifying patterns in a cross-section"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: AI can help identify the cross-section of risk across financial and non-financial firms in a large sample of observations, but availability of timely data is key; in periods of low liquidity and market dysfunction AI could help prediction through better monitoring of anomalies across markets. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI could help prediction through better monitoring of anomalies across markets"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Systemic risks often arise from slow build-up of imbalances and materialise in infrequent but very costly stress events; the scarcity of data on such events and the uniqueness of financial crises limit stand-alone use of data-intensive AI models in macroprudential regulation. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The scarcity of data on such events and the uniqueness of financial crises limit the stand-alone use"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Pairing AI-based insights with human judgment could support macroprudential regulation; together with human expertise and economic reasoning, gen AI tools could yield large benefits to regulators and supervisors, and with rich data sets AI could help build early warning indicators of emerging pressure points associated with system-wide risks. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI could help in building early warning indicators that alert supervisors to emerging pressure points"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In sum, with sufficient data AI tools let central banks draw on a richer set of structured and unstructured data, speed up data collection and analysis, and analyse economic activity in real time at a granular level. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the use of AI enables the analysis of economic activity in real time at a granular level"</sub>

## Macroeconomic impact of AI

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Recent evidence suggests AI directly raises productivity in cognitive tasks: generative AI had a sizeable, rapid positive effect on customer support agents and college-educated professionals doing writing tasks, and software developers using LLMs through GitHub Copilot could code more than twice as many projects per week. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Software developers that used LLMs through the GitHub Copilot AI could code more than twice as many projects per week"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A BIS collaborative study with Ant Group shows productivity gains are immediate and largest among less experienced and junior staff (Box E). (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "productivity gains are immediate and largest among less experienced and junior staff"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Early studies associate AI patenting and AI use with faster employment, output and revenue growth relative to comparable firms; adopting firms see higher growth in sales, employment and market valuations, primarily driven by increased product innovation, over a horizon of one to two years. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These effects have materialised over a horizon of one to two years"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In a global sample, AI patent applications have a positive effect on the labour productivity of small and medium-sized enterprises, especially in services industries. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI patent applications generate a positive effect on the labour productivity of small and medium-sized enterprises"</sub>

## Box E: Gen AI and labour productivity: a field experiment on coding

- **descriptive / context**: Box E notes experimental evidence on gen AI and worker productivity is scarce; BIS work uses data from Ant Group, which unveiled the coding LLM CodeFuse in September 2023, accessible before wide release to a select group of programmers for an initial six-week trial. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In September 2023, Ant Group unveiled CodeFuse, a specific large language model (LLM)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Comparing programmer groups with similar productivity and experience with and without LLM access shows a 55% average increase in productivity (measured by lines of code produced) for the group with access. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "shows a 55% increase in productivity (measured by the number of lines of code produced) on average"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Roughly one third of the 55% increase is attributed directly to code lines generated by the LLM, with the rest resulting from improved efficiency in coding elsewhere (likely reflecting additional time available for other programming tasks). (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Roughly one third of this increase can be attributed directly to the code lines generated by the LLM"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Productivity increased only among junior programmers; senior programmers used the LLM less, with the volume of requests negatively correlated with years of experience, while the acceptance rate of LLM suggestions did not vary with experience. The box concludes the lower impact on seniors stems from lower engagement rather than lack of usefulness. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "stems from their lower engagement with the LLM rather than a lack of usefulness"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Graph E1 footnote: the analysis is a difference-in-differences comparison of programmers with access to CodeFuse (treatment) and without (control), spanning six weeks before and eight weeks after introduction (time 0 marks the two-week introduction period); junior programmers have up to one year of experience and seniors more than one year. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Junior programmers (panel A) are defined as those with up to one year of experience"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: In Graph E1 the acceptance rate is the proportion of LLM requests for which a programmer accepted the suggestion with less than 50% human modification; requests per user are the average number of assistance requests in the eight weeks after introduction, by years of experience. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "with less than 50% human modification"</sub>

## AI and productivity

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Graph 7.B (based on 893 respondents of the New York Fed Survey of Consumer Expectations) shows higher-income or better-educated workers expect greater productivity benefits from generative AI. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Based on 893 respondents from the Survey of Consumer Expectations conducted by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: AI's macroeconomic impact on productivity growth could be sizeable: beyond raising worker and firm efficiency, AI can spur innovation indirectly, since most innovation arises in occupations requiring high cognitive abilities; literature estimates of the impact on annual labour productivity growth are substantive though ranges vary, and faster productivity growth expands productive capacity and raises aggregate supply. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Through faster productivity growth, AI will expand the economy’s productive capacity and thus raise aggregate supply"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Higher productivity growth will affect aggregate demand through firm investment; in 2023 alone spending on AI exceeded $150 billion worldwide, and a survey of US company technology officers suggests almost 50% rank AI as their top budget item over the next years. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In 2023 alone, spending on AI exceeded $150 billion worldwide"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Improved prediction could further boost investment, as AI adoption leads to more accurate predictions at lower cost, reducing uncertainty; AI could also introduce new sources of uncertainty (for example by changing market and price dynamics) that counteract some of the positive investment impact. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI could also introduce new sources of uncertainty that counteract some of its positive impact on firm investment"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: AI could spur household consumption by reducing search frictions and improving matching, making markets more competitive; AI agents could improve consumers' search and help firms advertise and target products. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI could spur consumption by reducing search frictions and improving matching, making markets more competitive"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: AI's impact on consumption also depends on labour demand and wages, determined by the relative strength of three forces: how much AI raises productivity, how many new tasks it creates and how many workers it displaces by making tasks obsolete (Graph 8). (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "by how much AI raises productivity, how many new tasks it creates and how many workers it displaces"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: If AI is a true general-purpose technology raising total factor productivity in all industries to a similar extent, labour demand is set to increase across the board; it could also create new tasks, further increasing labour demand and wage growth, which would increase aggregate demand. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the demand for labour is set to increase across the board"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Effects may differ across tasks and occupations: AI might benefit only some workers (for example nurses interpreting x-rays), while gen AI could make tasks such as summarising documents, processing claims or answering standardised emails obsolete, displacing some workers with possible declines in employment and lower wage growth and distributional consequences. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "summarising documents, processing claims or answering standardised emails, which lend themselves to automation by LLMs"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A BIS and New York Fed survey of US households indicates men, better-educated individuals and those with higher incomes think they will benefit more from gen AI than women and those with lower educational attainment or incomes (Graph 7.B). (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "men, better-educated individuals or those with higher incomes think that they will benefit more from the use of gen AI"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: AI could have implications for economic inequality: displacement might eliminate jobs faster than new ones are created, potentially exacerbating income inequality; the digital divide could widen for those lacking technology access or digital literacy, and the elderly are particularly at risk of exclusion. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Displacement might eliminate jobs faster than the economy can create new ones"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A BIS study describes mechanisms for output and inflation: as a permanent productivity increase AI raises aggregate supply, higher consumption and investment raise aggregate demand, and output increases; in the short term, if households and firms fully anticipate future wealth they increase consumption at the expense of investment, slowing output growth. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they will increase consumption at the expense of investment, slowing down output growth"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Inflation response depends on anticipation: if households do not fully anticipate gains, AI acts as a disinflationary force in the short run as supply dominates; if they anticipate gains and consume more, the initial impact is inflationary. Because past general-purpose technologies were initially disinflationary, the former scenario appears more likely. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Since past general-purpose technologies have had an initial disinflationary impact, the former scenario appears more likely"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In either scenario, as capacity expands and wages rise, demand for capital and labour will steadily increase, and if demand effects dominate the initial output-capacity shock higher inflation would eventually materialise; speed depends on household expectations and the skill mismatch between obsolete and new tasks, where greater mismatch lowers employment growth, may leave some segments unemployable without retraining, and lengthens AI's disinflationary impact. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "some segments of the population will remain permanently unemployable without retraining"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Large online retailers use AI extensively in price-setting; algorithmic pricing has been shown to increase both uniformity of prices across locations and frequency of price changes, which could strengthen as smaller companies adopt AI, implying quicker pass-through of aggregate shocks to local prices and changed inflation dynamics; effects may depend on competition in the AI model and data market. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Algorithmic pricing by these retailers has been shown to increase both the uniformity of prices across locations"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The impact of AI on fiscal sustainability remains an open question: AI-induced growth would reduce debt burdens, but higher interest rates and possible fiscal programmes for labour relocation or higher unemployment could make the effect modest, and the AI growth dividend is unlikely to fully offset spending needs from the green transition or population ageing. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the AI growth dividend is unlikely to fully offset the spending needs"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Availability of data is a critical precondition for successful machine learning and AI applications, and data governance frameworks will be part and parcel of any successful AI application, so central banks' policy challenges encompass both models and data. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Data governance frameworks will be part and parcel of any successful application of AI"</sub>

## Toward an action plan for central banks

- **descriptive / context**: A trade-off arises between off-the-shelf models and in-house fine-tuned ones: external models may be more cost-effective in the short run and leverage private sector advantages, but bring reduced transparency, dependence on a few external providers, and concentration that could create significant operational risks for central banks' ability to fulfil mandates. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "reliance on external models comes with reduced transparency and exposes central banks to concerns"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Central banks are users, compilers and disseminators of data, a role that helps them meet obligations as key stakeholders in national statistical systems. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "central banks’ role as users, compilers and disseminators of data"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Central banks now need to use increasingly large and diverse structured and unstructured data often held by the private sector; LLMs can help process such data but hallucinations or prompt injection attacks can lead to biased or inaccurate analyses; commercial data costs have risen markedly and vendors have imposed tighter use conditions. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "hallucinations or prompt injection attacks can lead to biased or inaccurate analyses"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Setting up IT infrastructure is a key challenge, greater if central banks develop internal models and collect or produce their own data; adequate computing power, software, staff training or hiring, and creating a data lake (pooling curated data sets) involve high up-front costs, and reliable safe IT infrastructure is also a prerequisite to prevent cyber attacks. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a reliable and safe IT infrastructure is a prerequisite not only for big data analyses"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Hiring and retaining staff with the right mix of economic understanding and programming skills is challenging and a top concern for central banks (Graph 10); public institutions often cannot match private sector salaries for top AI talent. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "public institutions often cannot match private sector salaries for top AI talent"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: The chapter states AI is not a substitute for human judgment and requires supervision by experts with a solid understanding of macroeconomic and financial processes, since AI models for financial stability monitoring face limitations. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI is not a substitute for human judgment"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: The chapter says the answer to these challenges lies largely in cooperation paired with sound data governance; pooling resources and knowledge could ease constraints on collecting, storing and analysing big data, developing algorithms and training models. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The answer lies, in large part, in cooperation paired with sound data governance practices"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: Central banks could address rising commercial data costs, especially for smaller institutions, by sharing more granular data themselves or by joint procurement from vendors. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "by sharing more granular data themselves or by acquiring data from vendors through joint procurement"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: Cooperation could facilitate staff training through workshops and conference experience-sharing, benefiting central banks with fewer staff and resources, and re-using trained models could mitigate environmental (energy) costs of training algorithms and storing large data; collaboration could also help identify where AI adds most value and leverage synergies. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Cooperation, for example by re-using trained models, could also mitigate the environmental costs"</sub>

## Challenges in recruitment and retention

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Graph 10 is based on a May 2024 survey of 52 members of the Central Bank Governance Network (CBGN), asking whether recruitment or retention has become more difficult in the past five years and in which areas or skills (including cyber security, IT, fintech, data science, AI/ML, CBDC, climate, economics, financial analysis, law, operations/support); area shares are based on the subset of central banks experiencing more difficulty. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Based on a survey of 52 members of the Central Bank Governance Network conducted in May 2024"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Central banks could consider sharing domain-adapted or fine-tuned models within the central banking community, which could significantly lower the hurdles to adoption. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "central banks could consider sharing domain-adapted or fine-tuned models in the central banking community"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: Joint work on AI models can be done without sharing data, so the models can be applied even where confidentiality is a concern. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Joint work on AI models is possible without sharing data"</sub>

## Toward an action plan for central banks

- **descriptive / context**: The report says that as AI applications make the financial system more sophisticated over time, the premium on central banks having the right mix of skills (economic understanding and programming) will only grow. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the premium on having the right mix of skills will only grow"</sub>

## Challenges in recruitment and retention

- **descriptive / recommendation**: Common data standards could facilitate access to public data and automated collection from official sources, improving machine learning model training; dedicated repositories could share open source code of data tools (publicly or initially only among central banks), for example BIS Open Tech. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Common data standards could facilitate access to publicly available data"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: BIS jurisdiction-level statistics on international banking (started in the 1970s), debt securities and OTC derivatives exemplify collaboration in data collection, compiled from participating authorities under clear governance rules; confidential bank-level data sharing arrangements include the Basel Committee quantitative impact study data and the International Data Hub data on large global banks; sharing synthetic or anonymised data is another avenue to explore. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Other avenues to explore include sharing synthetic or anonymised data that protect confidential information"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Metadata (definitions, source, frequency, units and other information defining a data set) become more important as data expand and are crucial when privacy-preserving methods draw lessons from data sets held by different central banks; standardised metadata greatly enhance machine readability, and the FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable and Reusable) guide organising data and metadata. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "“Findable, Accessible, Interoperable and Reusable” (FAIR) principles provide guidance in organising data and metadata"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Metadata frameworks are crucial for understanding comparability and limits of data series; the SDMX standard provides a common language and structure for metadata, fostering data-sharing, lowering reporting burden and aiding interoperability, and the Generic Statistical Business Process Model gives a unified framework and terminology for official statistics business processes; sound data governance would also facilitate sharing confidential data. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Statistical Data and Metadata Exchange (SDMX) standard provides a common language and structure for metadata"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The report states that central banks must establish robust governance frameworks that include guidelines for selecting, implementing and monitoring both data and algorithms. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Central banks must establish robust governance frameworks that include guidelines for selecting, implementing and monitoring both data and algorithms"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Such data governance frameworks should include adequate quality control and should cover data management and auditing practices. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These frameworks should comprise adequate quality control and cover data management and auditing practices"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The chapter concludes that there is an urgent need for central banks to work together to develop a community of practice for sharing knowledge, data, best practices and AI tools; collaboration lowers costs, and such a community would encourage common standards. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is an urgent need for central banks to collaborate in fostering the development of a community of practice"</sub>

## Endnotes

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Endnote 18 states that distrust in big techs to safely handle data, relative to traditional financial institutions and government, also exists in other countries (Chen et al (2023)); a related risk is that alternative data are correlated with consumer characteristics (for example gender or minority status) that lenders are, for good reason, not allowed to use in credit assessment. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "alternative data are correlated with certain consumer characteristics that lenders are, for good reason, not allowed to use"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Endnote 18 adds that gen AI could exacerbate and perpetuate biases by creating biased data itself (because of biased training data or hallucination), which other models then use. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "gen AI could exacerbate and perpetuate biases by creating biased data itself"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Endnote 19 points to general policy discussion of AI financial stability risks (Hernandez de Cos (2024), Gensler and Bailey (2020), OECD (2023)); Calvano et al (2020) treat AI, algorithmic pricing and collusion; Assad et al (2024) find evidence of collusion by pricing algorithms in retail gasoline markets; Georges and Pereira (2021) find that even with close attention to model selection by traders, the risk of destabilising speculation cannot be entirely eliminated. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Assad et al (2024) find evidence of collusion by pricing algorithms in retail gasoline markets"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Endnotes 16 and 20 to 27 are citation pointers: Hitaj et al (2022); big data and AI in central banks (Doerr et al (2021), Araujo et al (2024)); Desai et al (2024) for machine learning anomaly detection in payment systems; Garratt et al (2024); the Finternet (Carstens and Nilekani (2024)) and tokenisation (Aldasoro et al (2023)); gen AI and cyber risk in central banks from a January 2024 Global Cyber Resilience Group survey (Aldasoro, Doerr, Gambacorta, Notra, Oliviero and Whyte (2024)); Du et al (2024); Barlas et al (2021) on transaction-level data for nowcasting consumption and investment, and the Beige Book summarising business-contact narratives for each of the 12 Federal Reserve districts (Soto (2023)). (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "See Desai et al (2024) for a recent application of machine learning to anomaly detection in payment systems"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Endnote 25 cites a report by Bernanke (2024) arguing that central banks need to improve forecasting by gathering more timely data and integrating advanced data analytics. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "argues that central banks need to improve their forecasting abilities by gathering more timely data"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Endnotes 28 to 30 cite Bricongne et al (2021), Dasgupta (2022) and Lehman and Mohrle (2022); Buckman et al (2023); and Aquilina et al (2024). (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "See Bricongne et al (2021), Dasgupta (2022) and Lehman and Möhrle (2022)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Endnote 17 gives an example of evidence from machine learning-based credit scoring models in the US mortgage market: Black and Hispanic borrowers benefited less often from lower interest rates than borrowers from other communities (Fuster et al (2019)). (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Black and Hispanic borrowers were less likely to benefit from lower interest rates than borrowers from other communities"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Endnote 31 states that synthetic data are unlikely to help; in most cases their generation relies on known data-generating processes, which do not apply to financial crises (Shumailov et al (2023)). (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "their generation relies on known data-generating processes, which do not apply to financial crises"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Endnote 31 adds that continued reliance on synthetic data by AI models diminishes information from the tails of the distribution (rare but highly consequential events), which are of particular concern for macroprudential policy. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "continued reliance on these data by AI models diminishes the information coming from the tails of the distribution"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Endnote 34 states that estimates range from 0.5 to 1.5 percentage points over the next decade (see for example Baily et al (2023) and Goldman Sachs (2023)), and that Acemoglu (2024) provides lower yet still positive estimates. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Estimates range from 0.5 to 1.5 percentage points over the next decade"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Endnote 32 reports Brynjolfsson et al (2023) found a gen AI conversational assistant improved customer support agents' productivity by 14%, and Noy and Zhang (2023) found ChatGPT support raised productivity on writing tasks for college-educated professionals, reducing time required by 40% and raising output quality by 18%. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "reducing the time required by 40% and raising output quality by 18%"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Endnotes 33, 35, 36 and 38 are citation pointers: employment, output, revenue growth, product innovation and labour productivity evidence (Yang (2022) for Taiwan, Czarnitzki et al (2023) for Germany, Alderucci et al (2020), Babina et al (2024), Damioli et al (2021)); Statista and Anwah and Rosenbaum (2023); Agrawal et al (2019, 2022) and Ahir et al (2022); Aldasoro et al (2024a, b) and Pizzinelli et al (2023). (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "On employment and output growth, see Yang (2022) and Czarnitzki et al (2023) for Taiwan and Germany, respectively"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Endnote 37 defines "AI agents" as systems built on advanced LLMs with planning capabilities, long-term memory and, typically, access to external tools (for example executing code, using the internet or performing market trades). (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "endowed with planning capabilities, long-term memory and, typically, access to external tools"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Endnote 39 states Cornelli, Frost and Mishra (2023) find investments in AI are associated with greater inequality and a shift from mid-skill jobs to high-skill and managerial positions; it also cites Doerr, Frost, Gambacorta and Qiu (2022) on the digital divide and Cazzaniga et al (2024) on income inequality. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "investments in AI are associated with greater inequality and a shift from mid-skill jobs to high-skill and managerial positions"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Endnotes 40 to 43 are citation pointers: Aldasoro, Doerr, Gambacorta and Rees (2024); Cavallo (2019); Gambacorta, Kwon, Park, Patelli and Zhu (2024); Wilkinson et al (2016). (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "See Gambacorta, Kwon, Park, Patelli and Zhu (2024)"</sub>

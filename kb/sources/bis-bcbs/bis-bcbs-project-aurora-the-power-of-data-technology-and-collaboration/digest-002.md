## 3.3 AML monitoring and analysis today

- **descriptive / requirement**: Project Aurora states financial institutions are required to implement AML controls and monitoring processes, including assigning customers a risk value, transaction monitoring systems, labelling and reviewing suspicious transactions, and reporting suspected money laundering to public authorities, typically national FIUs. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial institutions are required to implement a range of AML controls and monitoring processes"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Transaction monitoring for suspicious activity is typically based on one of two approaches, rule-based systems and behavioural monitoring systems; sanction risk, rising regulatory costs and compliance uncertainty from an evolving threat landscape can lead institutions to adopt a defensive approach to compliance. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "rule-based systems and behavioural monitoring systems"</sub>

## 3.3.1 Rule-based monitoring systems

- **descriptive / definition**: Rule-based monitoring systems use a set of pre-defined rules or algorithms to identify suspicious transactions; rules can be based on historical data and factors such as size, frequency and origin of transactions. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Rule-based monitoring systems use a set of pre-defined rules or algorithms to identify suspicious transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The rule-based approach is effective at detecting common money laundering typologies, for example a customer depositing funds in a manner designed to avoid reporting requirements. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "effective when detecting common money laundering typologies"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Rule-based systems can produce many false positives (legitimate transactions flagged as potentially suspicious) and false negatives (illicit transactions not flagged), often due to broad, generic rules triggering alerts for a wide range of transactions. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "false negatives – illicit transactions not flagged as suspicious"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: It is estimated that 90-95% of all alerts generated are false positives, and significant resources are spent reviewing them; footnote 32 cites the director of a SIFI telling a supervisor that if an ordinary customer is selected just once for manual review, the economic benefit of having that customer disappears (Finanstilsynet (2019)). (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is estimated that 90–95% of all alerts generated are false positives"</sub>

## 3.3.2 Behavioural monitoring systems

- **descriptive / definition**: Behavioural monitoring examines patterns of human behaviour associated with transactions, using machine learning models to understand typical behaviour and relationships between accounts and transactions, in contrast to rule-based reliance on pre-defined rules and triggers. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "behavioural monitoring examines patterns of human behaviour associated with transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Behavioural monitoring can identify deviations from anticipated normal behaviour, detect more sophisticated money laundering techniques and potentially reduce false positives, because it can analyse large transaction data sets and learn and adjust its model over time. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "detect more sophisticated money laundering techniques and potentially reduce the volume of false positives"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Behavioural monitoring is limited in detecting money laundering across institutions and borders due to siloed views of data and the inability to share information. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "due to siloed views of data and the inability to share information"</sub>

## 3.3.3 Defensive reporting and de-risking consequences

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Institutions can be sanctioned if deemed not to have necessary and effective AML compliance procedures and they process transactions that turn out to be illegal; this risk plus rising AML regulation costs and new threats can result in a defensive approach. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial institutions can be sanctioned if they are deemed not to have necessary and effective AML compliance procedures"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A defensive approach can lead to overreporting of transactions to authorities, draining public resources; where compliance costs and risks outweigh business benefits institutions can terminate relationships with particular customers (de-risking), which can have high social costs such as financial exclusion and could create distrust in risk assessment tools and regulatory frameworks. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "financial institutions can terminate relationships with particular customers to reduce their overall exposure to financial crime risk"</sub>

## 3.4 Challenges facing AML efforts

- **descriptive / context**: Three challenges are identified: launderers create complex cross-institution and cross-border transaction networks while institutions have limited, siloed views (Challenge 1); data are not standardised or readily consumable, hindering collaboration (Challenge 2); and data needed for AML/CFT must be balanced against privacy and personal data protection (Challenge 3). (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "data not being standardised or readily consumable, making collaboration across institutions more difficult"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Challenge 1: money launderers combine methods to hide the source and destination of funds, such as accounts at different financial institutions and PSPs, cryptoasset payments, correspondent banking relationships, domestic instant payment systems and cash purchases; such complex networks can remain undetected under siloed monitoring versus collaborative analysis using privacy enhancing technologies and advanced analytics. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "accounts at different financial institutions and payment service providers (PSPs), cryptoasset payments"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Challenge 2: data fields, formats and processes are not standardised, hindering data quality, efficient analysis, comparability and systems integration; data definitions describing financial crime typologies are also neither standardised nor available in standard machine-readable form. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "data fields, formats and processes are not standardised, hindering data quality, efficient analysis, comparability and systems integration"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Challenge 3: protecting individuals' privacy and personal data and complying with data protection regulations must be balanced against effective AML/CFT measures needing access to relevant data; balancing is complex, involving legal, ethical and technical considerations. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Balancing privacy protection and effective AML/CFT measures is complex"</sub>

## 3.5 Technology

- **descriptive / context**: The FATF has published several papers on digital transformation encouraging greater use of technology for AML/CFT, and identified technologies and approaches including data-sharing approaches, privacy-enhancing technologies, advanced analytics, data standardisation and data protection, which offer data-sharing and analytics benefits while preserving privacy. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "which encourage greater use of technology to improve AML/CFT efforts"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnotes 36 to 38: information sharing could also support customer due diligence (institutional risk assessment, onboarding, relationship risk management, beneficial owner identification) and help share patterns and typologies; PETs (homomorphic encryption, secure multi-party computation, differential privacy, zero-knowledge proofs) enable secure information sharing and analysis; advanced analytics (machine learning, federated learning, deep learning, network analysis, natural language processing) analyse large structured and unstructured data to identify patterns more effectively. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "homomorphic encryption, secure multi-party computation, differential privacy and zero-knowledge proofs"</sub>

## 3.5.1 Privacy-enhancing technologies (PETs)

- **descriptive / context**: PETs protect sensitive information even when data are distributed across organisations while allowing advanced analytics on protected data; each PET has specific strengths, limitations and use-case suitability; Table 1 overviews the PETs used in the project, with detail in Annex C. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "PETs could offer a solution, as they are designed to protect sensitive information"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Table 1: synthetic data (AML use: creating realistic datasets for AML testing) has the challenges of difficulty replicating real-world data accurately and a trade-off between information security and realistic replication; differential privacy (protecting privacy of sensitive data while allowing analysis) has risk of re-identification and the need to balance privacy with data quality and model accuracy. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Difficulty in replicating real-world data accuratelyTrade-off between information security and realisticreplication of the real data"</sub>

## 3.5.4 Network analysis

- **descriptive / method**: By examining connections between entities (eg individuals and businesses) and information in transaction data, network analysis can enable detection of money laundering networks and provide insights through data visualisation, statistical analysis and interpretation of results; this could allow AML experts to identify suspicious activities, understand the flow of illicit funds and pinpoint core laundering networks requiring investigation. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "provide insights through data visualization, statistical analysis, and interpretation of results"</sub>

## 3.5.1 Privacy-enhancing technologies (PETs)

- **descriptive / limitation**: Table 1: homomorphic encryption (processing data without revealing underlying data) has higher computational costs, slower processing times and the need to ensure information does not leak; federated learning (multiple parties train a shared model without sharing data) has risk of data leakage, higher computational costs and a potentially less accurate model. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Higher computational costs and slower processingtimesEnsuring that information does not leak"</sub>

## 3.5.2 Graph data structures

- **descriptive / method**: Graph data structures model networks such as transaction data and represent relationships between entities (individuals and businesses), helping identify key actors and relationship patterns; they can be used by machine learning models, clustering graph algorithms and network analysis, making them suitable for AML monitoring. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Graph data structures are useful in modelling various types of networks"</sub>

## 3.5.3 Machine learning

- **descriptive / method**: AI and machine learning may enhance AML suspicious transaction monitoring by identifying patterns and anomalies that traditional methods cannot; graph neural networks, which analyse graph-structured data such as transaction data, are one potential example. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "One potential example is the use of graph neural networks"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Machine learning models using network features could suffer from data bias and interpretability issues; data ethics, explainability of automated decisions and the role of human review would be important considerations. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Machine learning models using network features could suffer from data bias and interpretability issues"</sub>

## 3.5.4 Network analysis

- **descriptive / method**: Network analysis can uncover hidden patterns of suspicious laundering networks, complement machine learning by leveraging data features (eg ratios, accumulations; examples in Annex B), enable detection of laundering networks via connections between entities, and help AML experts understand flows of illicit funds and pinpoint core networks requiring investigation. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Network analysis can complement machine learning methods to improve AML monitoring"</sub>

## 3.6 Summary of trends and opportunities

- **descriptive / context**: Trends that could address AML challenges (details in Annex A): the G20 Roadmap for enhancing cross-border payments, with building blocks including financial crime prevention; harmonisation with ISO 20022, which gives a common language for financial messages and a richer set of structured data benefiting AML analysis. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The G20 Roadmap for enhancing cross-border payments"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Data standards such as the legal entity identifier (LEI) and beneficial ownership provide a common language and ability to link data sets about legal entities and beneficial owners (the LEI maps to other identifiers used in payments or securities); the Wolfsberg Group payments transparency standards consist of ten principles financial institutions should adhere to when processing payments. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "consist of ten principles financial institutions should adhere to when processing payments"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In certain jurisdictions transaction monitoring utilities (TMUs), for example TMNL in the Netherlands, have been established in private-private collaborative analysis arrangements; instant payment systems (and potentially CBDC systems) can provide a broad view of transaction data and could unveil suspicious networks across several institutions. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "transaction monitoring utilities (TMUs) (eg TMNL in the Netherlands)"</sub>

## 4.1 Objectives and scope

- **descriptive / method**: Project Aurora tests privacy-enhancing technologies coupled with machine learning and network analysis for money laundering detection in different monitoring scenarios and with different approaches to collaborative analysis and learning (CAL). (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the project tests privacy-enhancing technologies coupled with machine learning and network analysis"</sub>

## 4.1.1 Objectives

- **descriptive / method**: Objective 1: generate a synthetic data set with a minimum set of common data points representing real-world transactions between many financial institutions across several countries, with money laundering embedded. Objective 2: experiment with three simulated monitoring scenarios (siloed institution, national, cross-border) using machine learning and network analysis to compare performance in detecting laundering networks. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "at a siloed financial institution level, a national level and a cross-border level"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Objective 3: using the synthetic data and optimal models, experiment with centralised, decentralised or hybrid CAL approaches at national and cross-border levels with PETs applied, comparing performance in detecting laundering networks (CAL approaches are in Section 4.4). (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "different CAL approaches such as centralised, decentralised or hybrid at national and cross-border levels"</sub>

## 4.1.2 Scope

- **descriptive / method**: The PoC has three parts: Part A generates a synthetic data set of domestic and cross-border payments with laundering events embedded; Part B applies machine learning to detect networks and suspicious flows at single-institution, national and cross-border views; Part C applies PETs to test CAL arrangements (centralised, decentralised, hybrid) and model performance in each. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "application of PETs on the generated synthetic data to test CAL arrangements"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Out of scope for this phase: comparing all available machine learning models for AML; finding the best performing algorithm for any model class; a complete comparison of PETs; and a broader set of typologies and detection results on real-world data. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "finding the best performing algorithm for any class of machine learning models"</sub>

## 4.2 Part A: Synthetic data generation

- **descriptive / context**: Synthetic data generation creates realistic data to train, test and measure analytical tools and is particularly useful for simulating sensitive, hard-to-obtain data such as payments data; the project uses a step-by-step layered approach mimicking domestic and cross-border transactions and laundering across multiple institutions and countries. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is particularly useful for simulating data that would contain sensitive information"</sub>

## 4.2.1 Purpose of generating synthetic data

- **descriptive / method**: The synthetic data should enable: training models on a representation of real transaction data; experiments on siloed, national and cross-border views; understanding detection efficacy using a minimum set of common data points; showing such data points could be used by all CAL participants or at instant payment or CBDC system level; applying different PETs; and simulating CAL arrangements and instant payment (or CBDC) transactions. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Understanding the efficacy of detecting money laundering networks using a minimum set of common data points"</sub>

## 4.2.2 Generating the synthetic data

- **descriptive / data or feature**: The synthetic data represent domestic and cross-border transactions over two months among 155,250 entities (individuals and corporations), across six countries and between 29 financial institutions. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "over two months among 155,250 entities, including individuals and corporations, across six countries and between 29 financial institutions"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: The minimum essential data fields are: sender (payer) and recipient (payee) details; transaction amount; date and time; transaction method (eg card payment, bank transfer, cash deposit); country sent from; country received in; and details of the financial institutions involved (banks and PSPs). This follows the principle of data minimisation. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the transaction method (eg card payment, bank transfer or cash deposit)"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Footnote 41: the data set is intentionally smaller than real transaction networks for feasibility and simplicity, so may not reflect real scale but still serves evaluating technologies in a controlled environment; footnote 42: transaction form is included as a variable because criminals often use multiple methods to conceal the origin and flow of illicit funds. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "criminals often use multiple transaction methods to conceal the origin and flow of illicit funds"</sub>

## 4.2.3 Leveraging a three-step approach to generate synthetic data

- **descriptive / method**: Synthetic data are generated in three steps: relationship mapping (a network graph of relationships between individuals and businesses), transaction simulation based on the relationship map, and validation. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Relationship mapping generates a network graph that represents relationships between individuals and businesses"</sub>

## Relationship mapping

- **descriptive / method**: The relationship map is created using a statistical model tuned to behaviours and relationships observed in domestic payments data, so the synthetic data mimic real behaviour rather than directly replicate original data. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "instead of being a direct one-to-one replication of original data"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Relationship map steps: (1) simulate connections between individuals and businesses, including numbers of transactions received and sent and amount distributions; (2) establish relationships between individuals, businesses and FIs within each country, with the probability of being a customer of an institution influenced by factors including its relative size, and relationships based on behaviours (employment, shopping, remittance), transaction types (card, cash, transfer) and volumes approximating observed aggregated patterns; (3) establish cross-border relationships based on behaviours (cross-border purchases, remittances), payment methods and volumes. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The probability of an individual or business being a"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Businesses in the data set include VASPs, representing the growing use of cryptoassets and crypto exchanges to facilitate laundering (FATF (2020)); VASPs are characterised by substantial incoming transaction volume but limited visibility into outgoing transactions, mirroring opaque VASP outflows sometimes seen with VASPs in jurisdictions with minimal AML regulation. The output is interconnected relationships between individuals, businesses, FIs and countries organised into clusters. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "VASPs in the data set are characterised by a substantial volume of incoming transactions"</sub>

## The transaction simulation

- **descriptive / method**: The transaction simulator is a layer on top of the relationship map generating simulated transactions across institutions in various jurisdictions; each day it gathers information (transaction date, connections, usual frequency) and decides when, with whom, by which payment method and at what value the next transaction occurs, repeating continuously. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Using this information, the simulator decides when and with whom the individual or business should have its next transaction"</sub>

## Validation

- **descriptive / method**: Validity of the synthetic data is shown by combining actor behaviour (individual and business behaviour compared with real-world data, which were proprietary data of a third party) and aggregate patterns (national and cross-border patterns compared with statistics from central banks, intergovernmental bodies and others). (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the behaviour of individuals and businesses is compared with real-world data44 to ensure consistency"</sub>

## 4.2.4 Constructing money laundering activities in the synthetic data set

- **descriptive / method**: Laundering flows embedded in the data are based on known complex techniques: complex layering schemes (CLSs) and smurfing. Footnote 45 says CLS covers techniques such as money muling, abnormal cross-border transactions, transactions from dormant accounts and sudden account emptying; footnote 46 says smurfing's main purpose is avoiding reporting thresholds but it can also contain abnormal cross-border transactions and sudden account emptying. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The main purpose of smurfing is to avoid reporting thresholds"</sub>

## Complex layering scheme typology

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: A CLS event (covering several typologies, sometimes combined) is represented as a network with a clear source (starting point) and sink (endpoint) and layers of transactions and accounts in between. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "with a clear starting point (the source) and endpoint (the sink), and with layers of transactions and accounts in between"</sub>

## Smurfing typology

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Smurfing is similar to the complex layering scheme and both generate complex networks of laundering entities, but smurfing transactions are all designed to evade reporting requirements; smurfing events can take place over multiple days and involve small to large numbers of accounts, so complexity varies. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "transactions falling within this typology are all designed to evade reporting requirements"</sub>

## Complex layering scheme typology

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: In the CLS representation embedded in the synthetic data (Graph 8): (1) the CLS begins at a starting point (top node) and ends at an endpoint (bottom node), marking the beginning and end accounts of the laundering process; (2) a certain amount of illicit funds is assigned at the starting node as initial funds; (3) between start and end are several layers, each representing a step and consisting of different accounts at financial institutions. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These two mark the beginning and end accounts of the money laundering process."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: CLS layer size: the first half of the layers are each larger than the subsequent layer (branching-out), and the second half are smaller than the previous layers (branching-in), creating a complex layering structure throughout the laundering process. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The first half of the layers are larger than the subsequent layer (branching-out)"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: CLS connections and flow of funds: each layer is connected to the next, simulating the flow of funds; as funds move between layers they are split and passed on with a slight decrease in amount at each subsequent layer, which represents payments to people (eg mules) involved in the laundering. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "with a slight decrease in the amount of funds at each subsequent layer"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Footnote 47: the CLS description is simplified; the embedded CLS events include additional factors such as different account types, varying transaction sizes, intermediaries and transactions across multiple countries. The graphs are incorporated into the relationship mapping to generate the synthetic data. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "different types of accounts, varying transaction sizes, intermediaries involved and transactions taking place across multiple countries"</sub>

## Smurfing typology

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Smurfing simulation: (1) one or more starting points and endpoints, where a loop at the start represents cash deposits below the reporting threshold into one or more accounts controlled by a launderer (event 1 uses multiple accounts, events 2 and 3 a single account); (2) illicit funds allocated at starting points; (3) one or several layers between start and end. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the deposit of cash below the reporting threshold into one or more accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Smurfing simulation continued: (4) layer size, with the number of accounts in each layer increased or decreased; (5) funds transferred over a pre-defined period, with multiple layers passing funds on with a slight decrease at each layer; (6) the step repeats until money reaches the end accounts. The graphs are incorporated into the relationship mapping. (p. 37)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the above step is repeated until the money has reached the end accounts"</sub>

## Key assumptions for the synthetic data generation

- **descriptive / limitation**: Key assumptions: the maximum duration of a money laundering event was set at 14 days; data fields and structure are standardised across FIs and countries; there are no missing data fields. (p. 38)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the maximum duration of a money laundering event was set at 14 days"</sub>

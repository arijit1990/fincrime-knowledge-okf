## Box 1 Initiatives on correspondent banking

- **descriptive / context**: Box 1 describes World Bank survey work on the evolution and causes of bank account closures or restrictions in correspondent banking and in money and value transfer services (remittances): under the G20's Global Partnership for Financial Inclusion (GPFI) it gathered information on whether and why banks end or restrict relationships with remittance service providers, and with FSB and CPMI support it led a further survey, which also covered non-CPMI jurisdictions, on whether correspondent relationships are being ended or restricted, the net effect and the underlying causes; the correspondent banking results were published in November 2015 together with the GPFI-commissioned remittance provider survey. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "With support from the FSB and CPMI, the World Bank led another survey to obtain data"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In March 2016 the FSB created a Correspondent Banking Coordination Group (CBCG) to maintain impetus in delivering the action plan and provide high-level coordination, plus four workstreams of technical experts, one for each action point, which report periodically into and are steered by the CBCG. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FSB created a Correspondent Banking Coordination Group (CBCG)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The World Bank is conducting surveys to understand the evolution and drivers of bank account closures or restrictions in the context of correspondent banking and money or value transfer services (remittances); in November 2015 it published results of its survey on correspondent banking relationships, accompanied by a GPFI-commissioned survey on remittance service providers. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The World Bank is conducting surveys to better understand the evolution and drivers of bank account closures or restrictions"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FATF issued three public statements on de-risking (October 2014, June 2015, October 2015) to clarify its approach, which is based on the risk-based approach as a central element; the risk-based approach requires financial institutions to identify, assess and understand their ML/TF risks and implement AML/CFT measures commensurate with the risks identified. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The risk-based approach requires financial institutions to identify, assess and understand their money laundering and terrorist financing risks"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The report quotes the FATF June 2015 public statement: when establishing correspondent banking relationships, banks are required to perform normal customer due diligence on the respondent bank, and to gather sufficient information about the respondent bank to understand its business, reputation and quality of supervision (including whether it has been subject to an ML/TF investigation or regulatory action) and to assess its AML/CFT controls. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "banks are required to perform normal customer due diligence on the respondent bank"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In October 2015 the FATF confirmed de-risking will remain a priority, highlighted its work to clarify regulatory expectations so AML/CFT measures are implemented in line with its risk-based approach, and said it is developing guidance on correspondent banking and remittances whose main objective is to clarify applicable FATF requirements; the CPMI and FSB Secretariats were invited to FATF Policy Development Group meetings (February and June 2016) and private sector dialogue (April 2016). (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF is developing a guidance on correspondent banking and remittances whose main objective is to clarify"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FATF recently completed work of relevance to de-risking: guidance on the risk-based approach for effective supervision and enforcement by AML/CFT supervisors and law enforcement (October 2015), guidance for the risk-based approach for money or value transfer services (February 2016), and best practices on combating the abuse of non-profit organisations (June 2015). (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "guidance for risk-based approach for money or value transfer services (February 2016)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Basel Committee published in January 2014 Sound management of risks related to money laundering and financing of terrorism, containing an annex on correspondent banking (ML/FT risk assessments and CDD requirements); in February 2016 it released an expanded version with a new annex, the General guide to account opening. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "which contains an annex on correspondent banking"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FATF's June 2015 de-risking public statement, as quoted in Box 1, adds that, although high risk scenarios will be exceptions, the FATF Recommendations do not oblige banks to routinely carry out normal customer due diligence on the customers of their respondent banks when they set up and maintain correspondent banking relationships. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FATF Recommendations do not require banks to perform, as a matter of course, normal customer due diligence"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In 2009 the BCBS published Due diligence and transparency regarding cover payment messages related to cross-border wire transfers, relevant to payment messages used for correspondent business. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Due diligence and transparency regarding cover payment messages related to cross-border wire transfers"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Closer coordination between workstreams and authorities has been established since the October 2015 consultative report, including the FSB CBCG and participation of FSB and CPMI secretariats in FATF Policy Development Group meetings and the annual FATF Private Sector Consultative Forum; such liaison will continue to be essential to understand the issues and avoid duplication of work. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "such liaison will continue to be essential in order to understand the many issues involved"</sub>

## 2.1 Concept of correspondent banking

- **descriptive / context**: At the most basic level, correspondent banking requires respondent banks to open accounts in the correspondent banks' books and the exchange of messages to settle transactions by crediting and debiting those accounts. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "correspondent banking requires the opening of accounts by respondent banks in the correspondent banks’ books"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The main components of correspondent banking are a bilateral agreement between two banks by which one provides services to the other, the opening of accounts by the respondent in the correspondent's books, and the importance of payment services as a core function; relationships are frequently reciprocal, normally in different currencies. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a bilateral agreement between two banks by which one of them provides services to the other"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Correspondent banking is especially important for cross-border transactions, as its importance for domestic payments within a single jurisdiction has diminished greatly due to financial market infrastructures; the report analyses only cross-border correspondent banking activities with a focus on payment aspects. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "its importance for domestic payments within a single jurisdiction has diminished greatly"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Figure 1 shows settlement of a payment from bank A to bank C via bank B as intermediary where A and C do not hold accounts with each other; either bank B transfers the payment to C using correspondent banking only, or via a payment system in which both B and C participate. A, B and C would normally be in two or more jurisdictions and other banks could be intermediaries on the sending and receiving sides. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "As banks A and C do not hold accounts with each other, they use the services of bank B"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A footnote notes that some innovative payment service providers, including non-banks, offer services that could be an alternative to correspondent banking for specific types of retail payment, and that the CPMI Digital currencies report (2015) mentions that a decentralised distributed-ledger payment mechanism has the potential to facilitate certain cross-border transactions, possibly faster and cheaper, by in part eliminating intermediary banks. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Some innovative payment service providers, including non-banks, offer services that could be an alternative to correspondent banking"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The report gives a general definition of correspondent banking as one bank (the correspondent) holding deposits that belong to other banks (the respondents) and supplying payment and other services to them; the ECB's correspondent banking survey uses a comparable basic definition, speaking of agreements or contractual relationships under which banks provide payment services to each other. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an arrangement under which one bank (correspondent) holds deposits owned by other banks (respondents)"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The report cites the Wolfsberg Group's more detailed definition, under which correspondent banking is the supply of a current or other liability account and related services to another financial institution, affiliates included, for carrying out third-party payments and trade finance and for the institution's own cash clearing, liquidity management and short-term borrowing or investment needs in a given currency. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "provision of a current or other liability account, and related services, to another financial institution, including affiliates"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Figure 1 shows a payment settled from bank A to bank C through bank B because A and C hold no accounts with each other; in one variant B passes the payment to C using correspondent banking alone, in the other B uses a payment system in which both B and C take part. The steps common to both are: (1) debit of the payer's account at bank A; (2) credit of bank B's mirror account at bank A, kept for accounting purposes; (3) a payment message from A to B over a telecommunication network; and (4) debit of bank A's account at bank B (the loro account). (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Debiting of bank A’s account with bank B (loro account)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Correspondent banking may include services such as international funds transfers, cash management services, check clearing, loans and letters of credit, or foreign exchange services. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Correspondent banking may include various services, such as international funds transfers, cash management services"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: In traditional correspondent banking, a respondent bank agrees with the correspondent bank to execute payments on behalf of the respondent and its customers; the respondent's customers do not have direct access to the correspondent account but transact indirectly. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The respondent bank’s customers do not have direct access to the correspondent account, but they transact business indirectly"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Nested correspondent banking refers to use of a bank's correspondent relationship by a number of respondent banks that have no direct account relationship with the correspondent bank but conduct business through their relationships with its direct respondent bank (e.g. a local bank conducting correspondent business indirectly via its regional savings bank). (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Nested correspondent banking refers to the use of a bank’s correspondent relationship by a number of respondent banks"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Payable-through accounts, also known as "pass-through" or "pass-by" accounts, are similar to nested correspondent banking, but the respondent bank allows its customers to directly access the correspondent account to conduct business on their own behalf. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Payable-through accounts, also known as “pass-through” or “pass-by” accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In Figure 1, route A (correspondent bank only) continues with: (5) credit of bank C's account at bank B; (6) a payment message from B to C; (7) debit of bank B's mirror account at bank C, kept for accounting purposes; and (8) credit of the receiver's account at bank C. Route B (payment system involved) continues with: (5) a payment message from B to the payment system; (6) settlement through the payment system; (7) a payment message from the payment system to C; and (8) credit of the receiver's account at bank C. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Payment message from bank B to payment system"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report says that, since correspondent banking services are a key element of cross-border transactions, they could be expected to expand alongside growth in international trade and cross-border financial activity. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they might be expected to grow in parallel with the expansion of international trade"</sub>

## 2.2 Recent developments in correspondent banking – qualitative analysis

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Cutbacks in the number of relationships: relationships are being reduced especially for respondent banks that (i) do not generate sufficient volumes to recover compliance costs; (ii) are located in jurisdictions perceived too risky; (iii) provide payment services to customers about which information needed for an adequate risk assessment is not available; or (iv) offer products or services or have customers posing higher AML/CFT risk and are more difficult to manage. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "do not generate sufficient volumes to recover compliance costs"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Consultation comments argued that some decisions to withdraw services from certain respondent banks follow specific risk assessments of an individual respondent, which may include factors in addition to jurisdiction; this may suggest some de-risking may occur because banks are carrying out AML/CFT regime requirements and mitigating exposure to AML/CFT risks that cannot be managed effectively. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "some amount of de-risking may be occuring because banks are carrying out the requirements of the AML/CFT regime"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Changes in relationships: service types perceived to have higher risks (nested correspondent banking, payable-through accounts) are being scaled back so traditional correspondent banking predominates; remaining relationships are often retained only to support cross-selling of other products to respondent banks. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "nested correspondent banking, payable-through accounts) are being scaled back"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Concentration of relationships: cutbacks in number and changes in nature of relationships have resulted in significant concentration in a relatively small number of service-providing institutions that increasingly dominate the market, plus concentration within affiliated banks. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "significant concentration of relationships in a relatively small number of service-providing institutions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Increasing costs: establishing and maintaining a correspondent banking relationship is perceived to be increasingly costly for both correspondent and respondent banks. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "perceived to be increasingly costly both for correspondent and respondent banks"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: One trend identified is difficulty in setting up or keeping the correspondent relationships needed to take part in financial market infrastructures: members of multicurrency FMIs may use a correspondent bank for cash settlement, and such difficulties may make keeping a backup correspondent relationship more of a burden, which matters particularly for multicurrency FMIs whose participants may need several correspondent relationships; CLS, a multicurrency FMI, reports that some participants have struggled to establish alternative (backup) relationships. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "some participants have had difficulties in establishing alternative (backup) correspondent banking relationships"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Cutbacks in specific foreign currencies: some correspondent banks are increasingly reluctant to provide services in currencies where perceived sanctions risk, AML/CFT regulatory burden or uncertainty about implementation and reputational risk seem higher; there are indications that US dollar correspondent activity is increasingly concentrated in US banks, with non-US banks withdrawing except for some ancillary services while possibly still serving in their domestic currency. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "correspondent banking activities in US dollars are increasingly concentrated in US banks"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Geographical imbalances: not all jurisdictions and currencies are affected equally; respondent banks, in particular smaller banks in jurisdictions perceived too risky, are especially affected. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "in particular smaller banks located in jurisdictions perceived to be too risky, are especially affected"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: On the demand side, at least some respondent banks are actively reducing the number of correspondent relationships to reduce their own risk management work, simplify reporting of intraday liquidity, concentrate payment channels and cut costs, although significant demand for the services still seems to exist. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "at least some respondent banks are actively reducing the number of correspondent banking relationships"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Interviewed banks also cited high uncertainty about what constitutes compliance in order to avoid penalties and reputational damage; for example some believe it is necessary to "know your customers' customers (KYCC)" and there is uncertainty as to when this is necessary and how detailed it should be, which increases the difficulty of measuring risks and might be leading to abandonment of some relationships. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there seems to be a degree of uncertainty as to when this is necessary and how detailed"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Not all causes relate directly to rising regulatory costs: the general trend of financial institutions to downsize and deleverage after the financial crisis seems behind some banks' decisions to eliminate or scale back correspondent banking, particularly if not a core activity; also country risk (geopolitical and financial) may have increased, so rising costs may partly reflect applying existing policies to more high-risk countries, not just higher enforcement activity and penalties. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the general trend of financial institutions to downsize and deleverage in the wake of the financial crisis"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In summary, increasing costs, regulatory requirements and increased risk perception are reducing profit margins in some countries and/or with some customers; this is a business highly influenced by economies of scale, and the perception is that it has shifted from a low-risk/low-margin segment to a high-risk/low-margin one. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this line of business has shifted from being a low-risk/low-margin segment to a high-risk/low-margin one"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: A footnote states that from the regulatory side no significant AML/CFT changes were introduced recently and banks are expected to continue applying a risk-based approach to CDD, but the perception of ML/FT risks associated with activities such as correspondent banking seems to be changing in some instances; "de-risking" commonly refers to banks adopting increasingly stringent financial crime-related policies to reduce exposure to ML, TF, corruption and sanctions risk. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "increasingly stringent financial crimerelated policies to reduce their exposure to potential money laundering, terrorist financing, corruption and sanctions risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report says most drivers of the trends appear to lie on the supply side, and that one of the main ones appears to be banks' growing habit of assessing the profitability of business lines, customers and even jurisdictions, as correspondent banking costs have risen and capital and liquidity are scarcer and dearer; correspondent banking appears profitable in aggregate, but parts of it are not, so correspondent banks have dropped less profitable customers or jurisdictions, especially where returns do not justify the investment cost. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "One of the main drivers seems to be the growing tendency for banks to assess the profitability"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: According to the correspondent banks interviewed, the most frequent cause of the fall in profitability is rising regulatory compliance cost, especially for AML/CFT regulation, and anecdotal evidence suggests that for some financial institutions these costs have reached a point where there is no business case for staying in correspondent banking. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the most common cause for this reduction of profitability is the increasing cost of regulatory compliance"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Not all correspondent banks react the same way and not all respondent banks are affected equally; some correspondent banks specialise in correspondent services as a profit source, focusing on respondents with sufficient volume to justify increasing costs and located in jurisdictions perceived to carry acceptable risk, seeing increased complexity as an opportunity for competitive advantage. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Some correspondent banks are specialising in the provision of correspondent banking services as a source of profit"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The majority of institutions seem to maintain correspondent services only insofar as needed to serve corporate customers' cross-border payments and trade finance, to support cross-selling of other products, or to preserve reciprocity; respondent banks fitting these strategies are likely to maintain relationships, while others risk being cut off from international payment networks. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the majority of institutions seem to be maintaining existing correspondent banking services only insofar as these services are necessary"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Banks that might risk losing access to correspondent services tend to be smaller institutions that do not generate sufficient volumes, are located in jurisdictions perceived to be too risky, are not part of an international group, or provide payment services to customers about which information for an adequate risk assessment is not available; this implies a risk that cross-border payment systems will fragment, reducing available options. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Banks which might risk losing access to correspondent services tend to be smaller institutions"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A footnote notes some institutions report providing correspondent services only to affiliates within their banking group. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Some institutions report providing correspondent banking services only to affiliates within their banking group"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The division of banks into groups likely or unlikely to keep correspondent relationships could explain the apparent contradiction between cutbacks and declining margins: specialised banks are unable to pass increased compliance costs on to respondents, suggesting the market is still competitive or compliance risks are not adequately priced, so competition may remain vigorous in some segments (larger players, low-risk jurisdictions) while supply may be reduced or shut off for others (smaller institutions, high-risk jurisdictions), potentially isolating them. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "such banks are unable to pass increased compliance costs on to their respondent banks"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Many interviewed correspondent service providers in CPMI jurisdictions are adapting their business model to rising costs, regulatory requirements and risk management considerations, though some exited voluntarily for lack of a business case; most banks can still obtain cross-border payment services, but banks in some jurisdictions have lost the ability to make cross-border payments, and smaller banks and/or banks in jurisdictions considered too risky seem most severely affected. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Nevertheless, banks in some jurisdictions have lost their ability to make cross-border payments"</sub>

## 2.3.1 Data description

- **descriptive / method**: The SWIFT analysis uses monthly transaction data, provided by SWIFT on an exceptional basis, covering 2011 to 2015, including message types MT 103 and MT 202 and subtypes, with sent and received volumes and nominal values (converted to US dollars using daily exchange rates) for each country pair (corridor), plus the number of active correspondents per corridor per month and currency and message type. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "monthly transaction data, provided by SWIFT on an exceptional basis, are used to analyse developments in correspondent banking quantitatively"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: For confidentiality reasons, data for corridors with fewer than three transactions or three correspondents are not disclosed. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "data for corridors with fewer than three transactions or three correspondents are not disclosed"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: The data set comprises more than 200 countries and territories, grouped by continents and regions using United Nations Statistics Division data; the number of corridors showing payment activity is about one fourth of the theoretical number, declining slightly from roughly 13,000 in 2011 to 12,600 in 2015. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "slight decline from roughly 13,000 in 2011 to 12,600 corridors in 2015"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: As SWIFT is the most commonly used standard for cross-border payments, the data presumably capture a very large part of correspondent banking activity and give an accurate picture of actual payment traffic between jurisdictions, but they do not differentiate payments cleared via correspondent banking arrangements from those sent via transnational financial market infrastructures such as TARGET2. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "they do not differentiate payments cleared via correspondent banking arrangements from those sent via transnational financial market infrastructures"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: A footnote states that SWIFT statistics on financial flows do not represent complete market or industry statistics because financial institutions have multiple means to exchange information about their transactions, and SWIFT disclaims liability for decisions based on them. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Because financial institutions have multiple means to exchange information about their financial transactions"</sub>

## 2.3.2 Results

- **descriptive / finding or result**: In aggregated data the dominance of high-traffic corridors might mask developments in other corridors and entire regions with less activity; payment traffic is concentrated in the triangle linking Europe (without Eastern Europe) with Asia and North America, so overall developments can bias the picture as regional and national developments can differ substantially. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "payment traffic is concentrated in the triangle linking Europe (without Eastern Europe) with Asia and North America"</sub>

## 3.1 General considerations

- **descriptive / context**: The report identifies five potential measures that could facilitate compliance with regulatory requirements applicable to correspondent banking services: (i) KYC utilities; (ii) increased use of the LEI; (iii) information-sharing initiatives; (iv) payment messages; and (v) use of the LEI as additional information in payment messages. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These potential measures are: (i) Know-your-customer (KYC) utilities; (ii) increased use of the LEI;"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The report stresses that, in isolation, the measures will not resolve all the issues. Withdrawal from correspondent banking is very complex, AML/CFT compliance costs are only one element, and business considerations and economies of scope and scale also matter. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "in isolation, they will not resolve all the issues."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Limiting information challenges through enhanced technical tools addresses only part of AML/CFT compliance costs and will not resolve issues such as uncertainty about how far customer due diligence should go. The proposed measures will not immediately help banks without access to correspondent banking services to gain such access. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "will not immediately help banks without access to correspondent banking services to gain such access."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report notes it can be argued that industry should manage its own costs and revenues, but a bank's decision to withdraw from correspondent banking can disrupt the whole market and so may entail a negative externality for the correspondent banking network. Individual actors also face considerable uncertainty, high investment costs and a coordination problem, as there might be a first-mover disadvantage in implementing some measures. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may entail a negative externality for the correspondent banking network"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The Payments Market Practice Group (PMPG) is described as an independent body of payments experts from Asia-Pacific, Europe and North America acting as an independent advisory group, which aims to take stock of payments market practices across regions and recommend market practices and best practices. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Payments Market Practice Group (PMPG) is an independent body of payments experts"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The potential measures are assessed as ways to make procedures more efficient and lower compliance costs while leaving the applicable rules and the basic correspondent-to-respondent service channels unchanged; the current regulatory framework is treated as a given. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "without altering the applicable rules and the basic channels for correspondent banking services"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Public authorities and other relevant stakeholders (for example the Wolfsberg Group and the PMPG) may wish to encourage adoption of these solutions, to cut uncertainty and resolve the coordination problem and so improve the overall efficiency of correspondent banking and reduce negative externalities. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may wish to promote the implementation of these solutions to reduce the uncertainty and to solve the coordination problem"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: As a next step before any possible implementation, the report says all relevant authorities and stakeholders should analyse the measures further, to judge each measure's likely impact and avoid unintended consequences. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these measures should be further analysed by all relevant authorities and stakeholders in order to gauge the potential impact"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report notes that every bank offering a service in the correspondent banking chain applies customer due diligence to the institutions or customers it deals with directly. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Customer due diligence is applied by all banks providing a service in the correspondent banking chain"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The KYC discussion concentrates on what correspondent banks do to their respondent banks; KYC by respondents on their own customers is not specific to correspondent banking and falls outside that section. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This section focuses on the KYC activities performed by correspondent banks on their respondent banks"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 14: current expectations in correspondent banking include that correspondent banks extend their KYC on respondent banks to a deeper monitoring and understanding of the underlying transactions and possibly the identities of the originator and final beneficiary, informally referred to as "know your customer's customer" (KYCC); these expectations are covered in Section 3.4. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This approach is informally referred to as"</sub>

## 3.2 KYC utilities

- **descriptive / requirement**: Customer due diligence requires correspondent banks to identify and understand their respondents' banking activities and to know whether the respondents maintain additional correspondent banking relationships. This often leads to a massive exchange of documents. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Customer due diligence requires that correspondent banks identify and understand"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Footnote 15: the customer due diligence process should not be a "paper-gathering exercise" but a real assessment of ML risk (citing BCBS, Sound management of risks related to money laundering and financing of terrorism, 2014, Annex 2). (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should not be a “paper-gathering exercise” but a real assessment of ML risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: According to SWIFT, the 7,000 banks using the SWIFT network for correspondent banking have more than 1 million individual relationships, so the number of documents exchanged is presumably much higher. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the 7,000 banks that use the SWIFT network for correspondent banking have more than 1 million individual relationships"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The KYC document exchange creates problems: the same or very similar information must be sent to all correspondents; correspondents may have differing, non-standardised information requirements because the process is risk-based; and continuous updates are needed, not only at the outset. The KYC process is therefore complex, costly, time-consuming and labour-intensive. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the same or very similar information needs to be sent to all correspondents"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: KYC utilities store customer due diligence information in a single repository. Respondent banks provide initial information and updates in a standardised template, correspondent banks retrieve the information, and the information-providing respondents keep full control over their data and decide which banks have access. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "with the aim of storing customer due diligence information in a single repository"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report lists advantages of KYC utilities: fewer repeat sends of the same information; improved accuracy and consistency because banks maintain one updated set; a single template that might promote standardisation as a starting point for KYC obligations; possibly a faster process; and lower costs from less documentation exchanged. It says authorities may wish to promote their use. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "authorities may wish to promote the use of KYC utilities"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Banks' costs could be reduced further if they could rely more on KYC utilities and do fewer checks of data quality. One way might be an independent standard setting out the systems and controls utilities should have to ensure data accuracy, with some form of external accreditation to test compliance. The report says it is unlikely that central banks could do this, but other authorities, industry bodies or external auditors could have a role, for example through ISO or ISAE standards. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "establish some form of independent standard to set out what systems and controls such utilities should have"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In summary, the information in a KYC utility might be a good starting point for KYC due diligence by correspondent banks. Box 2 gives illustrative, non-exhaustive examples of KYC utilities. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the information in this type of utility might be a good starting point for KYC due diligence processes"</sub>

## Box 2 - Bankers Almanac

- **descriptive / case example**: Bankers Almanac is a KYC utility focused on KYC by financial institutions and designed for correspondent banking. To be included, financial institutions must demonstrate a legitimate physical address, appropriate licences and confirmation that they are regulated by a regulator of international repute; all data are quality-assured by a Bankers Almanac content team before publication. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "financial institutions must be able to demonstrate a legitimate physical address, appropriate licences"</sub>

## Box 2 - Clarient Entity Hub

- **descriptive / case example**: The Clarient Entity Hub went live in February 2015 and covers investment managers, hedge funds, corporates and banks. It allows secure upload, storage, categorisation and distribution of data; the data provider controls at a granular level who has access, and documents can be shared bilaterally beyond Clarient's standards. Clarient leverages DTCC customer reference data services and validates information to produce a "golden record" by linking each data element to evidentiary documentation, flagging inconsistencies to the customer. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Clarient Entity Hub went live in February 2015."</sub>

## Box 2 - KYC.com (Markit/Genpact)

- **descriptive / case example**: KYC.com (Markit/Genpact) was launched in May 2014 and covers financial institutions, investment advisors, asset managers, corporates and regulated and unregulated funds. It standardises and centralises KYC data collection to streamline onboarding; entities are identified once, globally, and reviewed against an industry-defined policy standard accepted by subscribing banks, including 10 of the G14 banks. Data is revalidated on regular schedules (annual refresh cycles). (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Markit/Genpact service, KYC.com, was launched in May 2014."</sub>

## Box 2 - SWIFT KYC Registry

- **descriptive / case example**: The SWIFT KYC Registry went live in December 2014 and focuses on banks active in correspondent banking, not on customers. Information is checked and validated by a dedicated SWIFT operational team; each bank keeps ownership of its data and others can access it only with the owner's permission. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The SWIFT KYC Registry went live in December 2014."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: SWIFT is also introducing the SWIFT Profile, a standardised portrait of a bank's traffic activity on SWIFT with sanctioned or high-risk countries (as per FATF/OFAC/EU lists), which banks can share with selected counterparties through the Registry. Additional services, by SWIFT or third parties, were to be added in the coming months. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "SWIFT is also introducing the so-called SWIFT Profile."</sub>

## Box 2 - Thomson Reuters Accelus

- **descriptive / case example**: Thomson Reuters Accelus Org ID went live in March 2014 with customer records for hedge funds, asset managers, corporations and banks active in correspondent banking. Customers submit documentation and authorise access; Accelus validates information, adds public data, scores customers according to risks, and standardises document requirements through a globally agreed KYC policy aligned with the Wolfsberg principles. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Accelus Org ID went live in March 2014."</sub>

## 3.2 KYC utilities

- **descriptive / limitation**: KYC utilities do not alter the correspondent bank's basic responsibility to perform due diligence on respondent banks. Correspondent banks cannot simply delegate it, because utilities cannot perform customer due diligence on behalf of third parties and ultimate responsibility always lies with the correspondent bank; resources are still needed to analyse and manage relationship risks. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Correspondent banks cannot simply delegate their responsibility as KYC utilities cannot perform customer due diligence"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: KYC utilities use agreed templates but the templates differ across utilities, and there is currently no standardised set of information that should be included. Requirements are usually based on the providers' analysis of legal requirements across jurisdictions and discussions with correspondent banks. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is no standardised set of information that should be included in KYC utilities"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: KYC utilities may not collect all the information a correspondent needs for its internal assessment, and risk-based processes cannot be easily standardised. Utility data would need to be complemented with bilaterally transmitted data, so utilities should be seen as a useful starting point rather than as eliminating the correspondent's need for customer due diligence. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "should be seen more as a useful starting point for customer due diligence obligations"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: KYC utilities need to be updated routinely by the respondent bank with fresh and accurate information to remain useful for ongoing monitoring or new relationships. Utility providers need to set adequate parameters regarding which events will trigger a requirement to update information. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "need to be updated routinely by the respondent bank with fresh and accurate information"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 18: FATF Recommendation 17 sets conditions for reliance on third parties for certain CDD measures and states that where reliance is permitted, ultimate responsibility for CDD remains with the financial institution relying on the third party. As a result correspondent banks are sometimes reluctant to use KYC utilities, although utilities may facilitate access to a basic set of information and in some cases provide an independent source of verification that could help the correspondent perform CDD on its own responsibility. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the ultimate responsibility for CDD measures remains with the financial institution relying on the third party."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: In some jurisdictions privacy law may bar the sharing, storage or mining of basic KYC utility information, such as a bank's other correspondent relationships and the geographic areas it serves. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The privacy laws of some jurisdictions may prohibit sharing, storing or mining of basic information"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Operators of KYC utilities need to check carefully, consistently with applicable laws, which information should and could be shared in the utility, particularly where it crosses borders. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Operators of KYC utilities need to check carefully and in line with applicable laws"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: To the extent some institutions do not participate in any utility, bilateral information exchanges must be maintained. To increase efficiency, both respondent and correspondent banks need to have access to a utility with broad coverage of relevant participants, and utilities may not address all information needs about where a respondent does business and with whom (see Section 3.4). (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to the extent that some institutions are not participating in any utility"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: KYC utilities are described as a promising tool for speeding up KYC compliance and cutting its costs, but the lack of standardisation in the type and format of gathered information limits their value. Complete standardisation seems unfeasible given the risk-based approach for AML/CFT. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This inconsistent gathering of information limits the value of KYC utilities."</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Standard setters such as ISO may wish to consider defining a standardised minimum set of information and data (including format) that any bank should be ready to provide via KYC utilities. This baseline could be augmented bilaterally for specific correspondent needs, since it seems unlikely any single utility will serve all segments and use cases. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "relevant standard setters such as ISO may wish to consider defining a standardised minimum set of information and data"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: For KYC utilities to be more effective, banks would need some assurances from relevant authorities (regulatory, supervisory or law-enforcement) about the appropriateness of, and reliance upon, any utility for AML/CFT compliance. If unclear how far banks can rely on the information, they might continue their own data-gathering, negating the value of utilities and much of the incentive to invest in them. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "banks have some assurances from the relevant authorities"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: More clarity about the extent of permissible reliance is described as essential if utilities are to reach their full potential, and this is within the purview of regulators, supervisors and other relevant authorities. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "more clarity about the extent of reliance that is permissible will be essential"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: The authorities responsible for AML/CFT (the FATF and AMLEG) are invited to consider developing a set of issues that financial institutions should consider when using KYC utilities, to support their appropriate use. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "are invited to consider developing a set of issues that financial institutions should consider when using KYC utilities"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Recommendation: where respondent and correspondent banks use KYC utilities that hold at least a minimum set of current, accurate information, such use could generally be backed as an effective way of lightening the customer due diligence compliance burden for banks in correspondent banking. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could be supported in general as an effective means of reducing the burden of compliance with customer due diligence requirements"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Recommendation: standard setters such as ISO may wish to consider defining a standardised minimum set of information and data, including format, that every utility should gather and that all banks must be ready to supply to other banks needing it. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may wish to consider defining a standardised minimum set of information and data (including the format) that all utilities should"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: The FATF and AMLEG, as the authorities responsible for AML/CFT, are invited to consider drawing up a set of issues that financial institutions should consider when using KYC utilities, in order to support appropriate use. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "are invited to consider developing a set of issues that financial institutions should consider when using KYC utilities"</sub>

## 3.3.1 General information on the LEI

- **descriptive / context**: After the financial crisis authorities worldwide could not clearly identify parties to transactions across markets, products and regions, making it difficult to identify trends, evaluate emerging risks including systemic risk, and take corrective action. Authorities and the private sector therefore developed a framework for unambiguous identification of entities through unique LEIs, which may also be used for reporting and other regulatory purposes. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Authorities worldwide were unable to clearly identify parties to transactions across markets, products and regions"</sub>

## Box 3 - Basic background information on the LEI

- **descriptive / data or feature**: LEI reference data include the official name of the legal entity as recorded in the business registry or constituting documents, in the local language and character set, plus any additional legal names and transliterated names in Roman characters. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The official name of the legal entity as recorded in the business registry"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: LEI reference data include two addresses: the address and country of legal formation, and the address of the headquarters (or of the fund manager). Addresses are structured into separate fields (street, city, region, country, postal code), the country uses the 2-character ISO 3166-1 code, and addresses can be repeated in several languages identified by an IETF language code. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Two addresses: (i) The address and the country of legal formation of the legal entity"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: LEI reference data also include the legal form of the entity, the name of the business registry in which it was formed and its identifier in that registry, when applicable, plus additional information for entities that have merged or ceased to exist. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Other information such as the legal form of the entity"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: LEI reference data include record status information: the level of validation the relevant Local Operating Unit of the Global LEI System could perform (in particular whether authoritative public sources corroborated the submitter's information) and the date of the last update. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Information on the status of the record, including the level of validation"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The LEI is envisaged for unambiguous identification of legal entities (and legal arrangements such as trusts) but is not applicable to natural persons, except individuals acting in a business capacity. The LEI ROC specified conditions for such individuals' eligibility in a statement of 30 September 2015. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "but is not applicable to natural persons, except for individuals acting in a business capacity."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The Legal Entity Identifier is a 20-character alphanumeric reference code under ISO standard 17442:2012, used to identify unambiguously the legal entities that take part in financial transactions; one LEI belongs to one legal entity and an entity can hold just one LEI. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The LEI (ISO standard 17442:2012) is a 20-digit alphanumeric reference code"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The LEI ROC issued its final report on collecting direct and ultimate parent data in the Global LEI System (Phase 1) on 10 March 2016; gathering that data, built on accounting definitions, was due to begin around end-2016. Records will show whether relationships were fully corroborated by consolidated financial statements, other supporting documents or regulatory filings. Entities may opt out for reasons given in the report, for example legal obstacles, and these reasons will appear in their LEI record. The LEI ROC will work on extending relationship data in later phases. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The collection of this information, based on accounting definitions, will start around end-2016."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Charter of the LEI ROC envisages that the private sector will use the Global LEI System to support improved risk management and increased operational efficiency, among other uses. The LEI is not used as a routing code for cross-border payments; the Business Identifier Code (BIC) is widely used for that purpose. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is not used as a routing code for cross-border payments"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: LEIs are issued in various jurisdictions through Local Operating Units (LOUs), which issue LEIs for a fee and validate reference data on issuance and following periodic certifications; LOUs make LEIs and reference data publicly available free of charge. An entity with an LEI cannot obtain another but can transfer maintenance of its code between LOUs. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "LOUs make the LEIs and the associated reference data publicly available free of charge."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The GLEIF (Global LEI Foundation), established in 2014 as a not-for-profit organisation, coordinates the LEI system globally and supports the centralised database of identifiers and reference data. It is overseen by the LEI ROC, a group of over 70 public authorities from more than 40 countries. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "established in 2014 as a not-for-profit organisation"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: As of end-April 2016, more than 430,000 entities from 195 countries had obtained LEIs from 29 operational LOUs endorsed to issue globally compatible codes. The LEI is used in rules and regulations covering 40 jurisdictions, mainly derivatives transaction reporting but also banking and insurance supervision and securities regulation; the EBA recommends the LEI as a unique identification code for supervisory purposes for every credit and financial institution in the EU. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "As of end-April 2016, more than 430,000 entities from 195 countries had obtained LEIs"</sub>

## 3.3.2 LEI and correspondent banking

- **descriptive / context**: Correspondent banking requires a robust mechanism for identifying parties involved in payment processing, for risk management, regulatory requirements and smooth processing (for example clear routing information for straight through processing). The BIC is the de facto standard for the latter; for the first two reasons, especially to facilitate AML/CFT screening, the LEI may be considered as a means of identifying parties to a transaction. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Whereas the BIC is the de facto standard for the latter"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The LEI system provides standardised identification and a centralised database from which information can be easily retrieved; it does not process or record financial transactions. It was not designed to facilitate AML/CFT compliance in correspondent banking, though its use may bring some benefits there. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It does not process or record financial transactions."</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: The LEI may be used to improve the effectiveness of measures in the report. KYC utilities and information-sharing mechanisms need unambiguous identification of the banks or customers in their databases, so rather than developing a specific standard, the LEI could be promoted as an efficient global standard for these utilities. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the LEI could be promoted as an efficient global standard for these utilities."</sub>
- **permissive / finding or result**: Widespread use of the LEI could help financial institutions identify specific entities unambiguously and increase the effectiveness of automatic screening packages, particularly for identifying sanctioned entities, for example by reducing "false positives" when screening names and addresses that only partially match an entity. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "increase the effectiveness of automatic screening packages"</sub>
- **permissive / finding or result**: The LEI could also facilitate consolidation of information received in financial intelligence units, by more easily identifying transactions of the same entity reported by different financial institutions. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "by identifying transactions of the same entity reported by different financial institutions more easily."</sub>
- **permissive / finding or result**: The LEI could become an option for supporting implementation of FATF Recommendation 16 on originator and beneficiary information in payment messages. That information could be communicated in different ways, but use of the LEI within payment messages when originator and/or beneficiary are legal entities might in future be an additional way of complying. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "might be an additional way of complying with the requirement in the future."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The report states the LEI is not a panacea for cross-border correspondent banking services. The LEI was not designed for AML/CFT purposes, so it needs to be investigated in more detail how far banks can rely on LEI information for AML/CFT screening, a point that becomes more vital as the LEI gains wider acceptance. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the LEI is not a panacea for cross-border correspondent banking services."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The LEI does not apply to natural persons (except individuals acting in a business capacity), so alternative means of clearly identifying natural persons in line with the FATF recommendations are needed in correspondent banking transactions, and these methods will also need to comply with data protection legislation. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "alternative means of ensuring a clear identification of natural persons in line with the FATF recommendations"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: It is envisaged that from 2017 the LEI will start providing information on ownership and relationships between legal entities. This will be limited to legal entities and thus will generally not cover identification of natural persons as beneficial owners of legal entities, which is one of the main aims of AML/CFT requirements. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "from 2017 the LEI will start providing information on ownership and relationships between legal entities."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The LEI helps unambiguously identify legal entities and might facilitate some CDD processes (for example determining more easily that an entity is already a customer and avoiding duplicated CDD effort and records), but the standard is not geared towards identifying counterparties from an AML perspective. At this point in time the LEI is not a substitute for customer due diligence, and banks remain responsible for adequate customer due diligence. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the use of the LEI is not a substitute for customer due diligence"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Up to now it has not been foreseen that payment messages used in correspondent banking (for example MT 103 or equivalents) would include a dedicated field/line or code for the LEI. Routing is based on BICs, and information providing adequate CDD is currently included in the message in line with FATF recommendations. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "would include either a dedicated field/line or a"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 27: the LEI was designed for use when legal entity identification is needed; the GLEIF statutes describe it as a reference code for uniquely legally distinct entities engaging in financial transactions. If it can identify legal entities behind entities engaging in payment transactions, the LEI could be used for AML/CFT purposes. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the LEI could be used for AML/CFT purposes."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report says that new data being added to the LEI system raises the possibility of using it to help with some AML/CFT-related customer due diligence, including the question of how far users may rely on checks done within the system; an example is implementing BCBS guidance that correspondent banks gather and regularly refresh details of the respondent bank's group and where group subsidiaries and branches are located. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "including the extent to which users can rely on the verifications performed in the system"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 25 reproduces FATF Recommendation 16, under which countries should make sure financial institutions put the required, accurate originator information and the required beneficiary information on wire transfers and related messages, and that this information stays attached throughout the payment chain. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Countries should ensure that financial institutions include required and accurate originator information"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Identifying the banks in a correspondent relationship by LEI may not seem an unsurmountable challenge, since the number of entities is limited and some banks are likely to obtain an LEI for other purposes (for example regulatory reporting and reporting of OTC derivatives to trade repositories). Additional benefits would be limited, as counterparties are usually well known to each other and other identifiers such as the BIC are needed for routing. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The additional benefits, however, would be limited, as the counterparties are usually well known to each other"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: BICs are the cornerstone of the global payments network and banks' IT applications are programmed around BICs. An entity can have multiple BICs but its LEI is unique and exclusive. BIC-to-LEI mapping utilities link legal entity information with routing information in payment messages, enabling unambiguous identification of banks in the payment chain; the SWIFTRef Entity Plus directory cross-references BIC to LEI for entities that have both. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "While an entity can have multiple BICs to serve various technical and organisational requirements, its LEI is unique and exclusive."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Using the LEI to identify a bank's customer seems much more challenging, since the number of legal persons/corporates is vastly higher than the number of correspondent banks, and information on the industrial sector code is not so far provided within the LEI. Benefits would be limited to legal entities. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The use of the LEI to identify a bank"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The report says that for effectiveness, and to deal with possible coordination problems, any requirement or recommendation to use the LEI in cross-border correspondent banking ought to be coordinated. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Any requirement or recommendation to use the LEI in cross-border correspondent banking should be coordinated"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The report stresses that the LEI serves to identify, not to route payments, and could replace the BIC only if banks' payment applications were changed very substantially. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "cannot substitute for the BIC without very significant changes to banks"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Mapping facilities that link payment-message routing information to LEIs would let banks look up the matching LEI whenever a message carries only the BIC. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "banks will always be in a position to retrieve the corresponding LEI when only the BIC is provided."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The current design of payment messages such as MT 103 (or equivalents) does not foresee the LEI in fields for the ordering customer/final beneficiary, but is geared to other information in line with FATF Recommendation 16. The current design therefore provides the information needed to identify each customer, corporates and natural persons, without specifying an LEI; the LEI would nevertheless offer a unique identifier with standardised reference data that could support automation. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "does not foresee the provision of the LEI in fields containing information on the ordering customer/final beneficiary"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Increased use of the LEI in correspondent banking is seen as a positive development that might be used in combination with the information-provision measures in Sections 3.2 and 3.4, and, per Section 3.5.3, may be used as additional information in payment messages on an optional basis. It is expected that, especially due to forthcoming regulatory requirements, future LEI use will increase significantly in various segments of financial markets. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the LEI may be used as additional information in payment messages on an optional basis"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Using the LEI to identify the ordering customer and final beneficiary, especially corporates, could be encouraged, as discussed in Section 3.5.3. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "using the LEI for the identification of the ordering customer and the final beneficiary could also be encouraged"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Because there is no LEI for individuals and sufficient CDD information can already be provided in the message without the LEI, it seems advisable to await wider use of the LEI by such entities before considering any mandatory changes to the information that should be included in a message, to avoid unintended consequences. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it seems advisable to await the wider use of the LEI by such entities"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Use of the LEI as a means of identification will not totally solve the challenges of correspondent banking, especially as it generally does not cover individuals, although it could improve the effectiveness of some measures (like KYC utilities or information-sharing initiatives) and reinforce other public policy objectives related to LEI use. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "will not totally solve the challenges associated with correspondent banking"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Recommendation: in addition to general promotion of LEIs for legal entities, relevant stakeholders may consider specifically promoting the LEI for all banks involved in correspondent banking as an identifier provided in KYC utilities and information-sharing arrangements. In a cross-border context this should ideally be coordinated and applied simultaneously in many jurisdictions. All authorities and stakeholders may wish to consider promoting BIC-to-LEI mapping facilities. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "relevant stakeholders may consider specifically promoting the use of the LEI for all banks involved in correspondent banking"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: The relevant authorities (for example the LEI ROC and AMLEG) are encouraged to elaborate further on the extent to which banks can rely on the LEI as a means of accessing reliable information to support customer due diligence in correspondent banking. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "are encouraged to elaborate further as to what extent banks can rely on the LEI"</sub>

## 3.4 Information-sharing

- **descriptive / context**: In some circumstances, such as jurisdictions or customers seen as higher risk for money laundering, a correspondent bank's due diligence obligations may go beyond KYC on the respondent bank. The correspondent may need to know with whom and where its respondent does business, possibly including the identity of the respondent's customers at account and payment level. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "due diligence obligations may go beyond KYC on the respondent bank"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: Authorities expect a correspondent bank to conduct sufficient due diligence to understand and mitigate risk, which at times may entail a better understanding of whom its customer does business with and where, including when acting as intermediary. A correspondent's efforts to obtain information on its customer's customer are informally called "KYCC". (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Authorities expect a correspondent bank to conduct sufficient due diligence to understand and mitigate risk"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Given that due diligence obligations may reach beyond KYC on the respondent bank in some circumstances, the report says correspondent banks should monitor the underlying transactions and understand them thoroughly. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "correspondent banks should monitor and thoroughly understand the underlying transactions"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The report states that correspondent banks need robust activity monitoring systems capable of detecting suspicious transactions. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Correspondent banks need strong activity monitoring systems to detect suspicious transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report says correspondent banks may need access to wider information about the originators and beneficiaries involved in such suspicious transactions. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may need access to extended information on the originators and beneficiaries related to such transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report observes that, exceptions for high-risk scenarios aside, the FATF Recommendations do not oblige banks to carry out customer due diligence routinely on their respondent banks' customers when they set up and maintain correspondent relationships. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FATF Recommendations do not require banks to perform, as a matter of course, customer due diligence"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: According to the report, banks remain unclear about what the high-risk exceptions to that position are. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "banks still lack clarity with regard to the exceptions in high risk scenarios"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: If respondent banks cannot provide additional information because of data privacy laws, correspondent banks may have no alternative but to block or reject suspicious transactions, which could eventually lead to termination of some correspondent relationships, particularly in jurisdictions with restrictive data privacy laws. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "correspondent banks may have no alternative but to block or reject suspicious transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: Data protection authorities play an important role and the FATF has already engaged with them. The report says further clarification on data privacy concerns in correspondent banking seems necessary, including a review of the potential impact of data privacy laws on correspondent banking and of the interaction and consistency between AML/CFT requirements and data privacy. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A further clarification on data privacy concerns in the area of correspondent banking seems necessary"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Even with additional information on specific transactions, an upstream bank often finds it very difficult to check identities and purpose, having no direct contact with customers who are normally located abroad. Except for a clear match with identities or jurisdictions on a few well-known international lists (footnote 33 gives the OFAC list, EU sanctions list, Consolidated UN Security Council Sanctions List and FATF list of high-risk and non-cooperative jurisdictions), it may be hard to obtain reasonable assurance of legitimacy. Some banks may therefore withdraw from correspondent banking or terminate relationships with respondents with low volumes or in jurisdictions perceived as high risk. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a correspondent bank may find it difficult to obtain reasonable assurance about a transaction"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Because many respondent banks have multiple correspondent relationships, there will be significant duplication of costs, as they will likely have to report bilaterally to several correspondents about specific customers. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there will be a significant duplication of costs"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Because many customers operate through several entities, a correspondent seeking information on a customer gets only a partial view of the customer's business profile, since payment information is limited to the customer's activities with the respondent bank providing it. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "will get only a partial view of the customer"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In October 2015 the FATF issued guidance for a risk-based approach on effective supervision and enforcement by AML/CFT supervisors of the financial sector and law enforcement. It reiterates the existing expectation that regulators and supervisors should use a risk-based approach when supervising financial institutions' AML/CFT compliance. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Guidance for a risk-based approach: effective supervision and enforcement by AML/CFT supervisors of the financial sector and law enforcement"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report describes as the most significant difficulty of additional due diligence the possibility that the expectation implicitly entails respondent banks being able to readily hand over extra transaction and customer information to correspondent banks. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this expectation may implicitly entail that respondent banks are in a position to easily provide additional transaction"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In many jurisdictions such requirements can clash with data privacy laws; if respondents cannot supply extra information on customers and transactions for that reason, correspondent banks may have no choice but to block or reject suspicious transactions, which could in time end some correspondent relationships, notably in jurisdictions with restrictive data privacy laws. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In many jurisdictions, however, these requirements can conflict with data privacy laws."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The report says it appears that the greatest problem is banks' uncertainty over what the authorities precisely expect in order to be fully compliant with the current national regulatory framework. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it seems that the biggest problem in this respect is the uncertainty of the banks involved in correspondent banking"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FATF, described as the international AML/CFT standard setter, has put out public statements on de-risking together with several pieces of guidance and best practices to inform risk-based decisions (see Box 1). (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "has issued public statements on derisking and has also issued a number of guidance and best practices"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Citing the FATF's October 2015 guidance, the report says financial institutions' implementation should be directed at managing risks rather than avoiding them. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Implementation by financial institutions should be aimed at managing (not avoiding) risks."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The report states that what departs from the FATF standards is blanket exclusion of whole countries and classes of customer without a serious, comprehensive look at their money laundering and terrorist financing risk and the applicable mitigation measures. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "wholesale cutting loose of entire countries and classes of customer"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Among possible measures, banks could write into contracts with direct customers for cross-border payment services an option to pass relevant information to correspondent or intermediary banks; with the customers' authorisation and where data privacy rules allow, this could ease information-sharing between banks and so speed up investigations and payment processing. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Banks could include in their contracts with direct customers for cross-border payment services"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The FATF guidance says the approach is not "zero failure" or "zero tolerance": when failures are detected, regulators and supervisors should apply actions that are appropriate and proportionate to the nature of the failure. Regulators and supervisors should also ensure that financial institutions take a risk-based approach to AML/CFT measures, without prejudice to rules-based measures such as targeted financial sanctions. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "regulators and supervisors should apply actions that are appropriate and proportionate"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The BCBS, through its AML/CFT Expert Group, has developed guidance on compliance with the FATF recommendations from a supervisory point of view. The report says it might be appropriate to encourage current FATF and BCBS work to increase clarity, because uncertainty about due diligence on respondent banks' customers is lessening the attractiveness of correspondent banking. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It might be appropriate to encourage current work by the FATF and the BCBS to increase clarity in this area"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Measures that might be considered in parallel to ongoing FATF work include more widespread use of the LEI to unambiguously identify corporate customers (Section 3.3). (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "More widespread use of the LEI to unambiguously identify corporate customers"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Centralised AML/CFT databases, in which banks provide information on identities, business and transactions of their customers in cross-border payment services, could reduce duplicated reporting (respondents report only to the database, accessible to correspondents and authorities with a legitimate interest) and give correspondents updated, better information by aggregating a customer's transactions reported by several respondents. Managing the confidential data might need public authority support, which could increase confidence in reliability. Such a mechanism might be an adequate solution for jurisdictions where banks have difficulty opening or maintaining correspondent services. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "development of centralised databases for AML/CFT purposes"</sub>

## Box 4 - Mexican initiative on information-sharing

- **descriptive / case example**: Mexico is building a centralised database into which Mexican banks will be required to report all cross-border transactions and may also report customer information. It has two components: a transactional component with aggregated information on customer-initiated cross-border transactions, and a customer due diligence component on each customer. The information required per customer depends on the aggregated number and value of its transactions. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Mexican banks will be required to report all crossborder transactions."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Domestic authorities could query the Mexican database to identify originators and obtain aggregated transaction data; correspondent banks would have access to an adequate subset of information about the respondent bank's customers for which they processed transactions. Originators would be required to agree to share information, and domestic authorities would regulate and oversee the database, including information verification requirements. (p. 34)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Originators would be required to agree to share information."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Section 314(b) of the USA Patriot Act is a voluntary programme allowing financial institutions to share information under a safe harbour from liability to better identify and report potential money laundering or terrorist activities. It is limited to domestic entities and does not contemplate cross-border sharing. In Turkey data privacy laws seem an unsurmountable obstacle to similar initiatives, and in the EU the 1995 Data Protection Directive implementation has led to different data protection levels. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Section 314(b) of the USA Patriot Act is a voluntary programme"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The EU Fourth Anti-Money Laundering Directive (AMLD4) will for the first time oblige EU member states to keep central registers of information on ultimate beneficial owners of corporate and other legal entities and trusts. Registers will be accessible without restriction to authorities and their FIUs, to "obliged entities" such as banks conducting CDD, and to the public in case of "legitimate interest". Member states will have two years to transpose it. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "oblige EU member states to keep central registers of information on the ultimate beneficial owners"</sub>

## 3.4 Information-sharing

- **descriptive / limitation**: Information-sharing mechanisms could increase procedural efficiency, may increase correspondents' confidence in the availability of information and reduce the cost of KYCC due diligence. Correspondent banks always remain responsible for performing adequate due diligence, and these mechanisms do not alter that basic responsibility. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "correspondent banks always remain responsible for performing adequate due diligence"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The most important obstacle to information-sharing mechanisms is compliance with data protection and privacy laws; communicating transaction or customer information to a sharing database might not be allowed in many jurisdictions. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The most important obstacle is compliance with data protection and data privacy laws and regulations."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Such databases may or may not include information on suspicious transactions, intended to help banks differentiate between customers. However, false positives (for example due to identical names) available globally on a mass scale may wrongly link innocent customers to illicit activity, which may lead to customers or institutions being broadly denied services and to restrictions on payment services even for entirely innocent customers and respondent banks, potentially a complete loss of correspondent relationships. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "false positives (eg due to identical names)"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: There is less clarity about the type of data information-sharing mechanisms could store and distribute; relevant authorities may wish to increase clarity, to the extent possible, so the mechanisms can be a useful source of information. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Relevant authorities may wish to increase clarity in this area"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Concentrating confidential information in a single repository requires operational risks to be adequately managed against hacking threats that could cause leakage (with serious reputational and legal problems), and establishing such databases might be quite costly. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "requires that operational risks be adequately managed to address hacking threats"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In conclusion, complying with KYCC due diligence expectations is complex; obvious technical improvements are difficult to identify, and the activities cannot easily be outsourced because responsibility for due diligence always remains with the banks. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these activities cannot be easily outsourced"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: Further FATF work with other authorities may help reduce correspondent banks' uncertainties. A continuing dialogue between national AML/CFT and data protection authorities is important to ensure consistent application of requirements. The FATF held a one-day seminar hosted by the European Commission in Brussels on 25 March 2014 with data protection authorities and financial institution representatives on the interplay between data protection laws and AML/CFT requirements. (p. 35)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A continuing dialogue between the national AML/CFT and data protection authorities is important"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FATF discussed information-sharing in depth with private sector participants at its Private Sector Consultative Forum in April 2016, covering challenges such as inconsistent data protection and privacy legal frameworks across jurisdictions and possible solutions. The FATF will continue to explore ways to tackle barriers to information-sharing; technical improvements such as information-sharing mechanisms could support more efficient exchange, provided that data privacy laws permit this. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Private Sector Consultative Forum held by the FATF in April 2016"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: To facilitate compliance with FATF CDD recommendations, information-sharing mechanisms (if they exist in a jurisdiction and data privacy laws allow) for knowing your customers' customers could be promoted as the first source of information by default, complemented bilaterally with enhanced information should there be a need. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could be promoted as the first source of information by default"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: To support information-sharing generally, the respondent bank may include provisions in its contractual framework with customers (for example in terms and conditions or a supplementary agreement) allowing it to provide general information on request to other banks for AML/CFT compliance purposes. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the respondent bank may include provisions in its contractual framework with its customers"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Recommendation (i): the report recommends inviting the FATF and AMLEG to give further clarity on due diligence recommendations for upstream banks, especially the extent to which banks must know their customers' customers (KYCC). (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is recommended that the FATF and AMLEG be invited to"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: Recommendation (ii): the FATF and AMLEG are also to be invited to examine further how to overcome obstacles to information-sharing, with a view to identifying potential best practices within an enterprise, among institutions outside the same financial group, and between the public and private sectors. (p. 36)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "further explore ways to tackle obstacles to information-sharing, with the aim of identifying potential best practices"</sub>

## Transparency and explainability

- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 48 states that as AI systems become more complex they often achieve higher performance at the expense of explainability, so striking the right balance between performance and explainability is one of the main challenges for institutions implementing AI, especially in credit and insurance underwriting. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "as AI systems become more complex, they often achieve higher performance at the expense of explainability"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The paper notes that because of explainability challenges some industry players advocate regulation focused on risk control around AI use rather than on explainability or transparency metrics, and others propose focusing on AI outputs (whether decisions are fair, ethical and compliant) regardless of how the AI arrives at them. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "some industry players advocate that regulations should focus on the risk control surrounding the use of AI"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 49 states lack of transparency in how AI makes credit and insurance decisions raises significant concerns about compliance with consumer protection and model risk management (MRM) requirements; consumer protection regulations generally require institutions to inform clients of the primary reasons for credit or insurance denials under 'adverse action' requirements. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Consumer protection regulations generally require financial institutions to inform clients of the primary reasons"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The paper states financial institutions are expected to address opacity and explainability risks as part of their evaluation of model complexity, which often requires enhancing oversight of AI models with particular attention to validation processes and risk control measures. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "address these risks as part of their evaluation of model complexity. This often requires enhancing oversight of AI models"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 50 states that for insurance underwriting policyholder protection is a key objective of insurance regulators and unfair treatment of customers from AI in underwriting attracts great regulatory scrutiny; New York Department of Financial Services (2024a) gives detailed guidelines on governance and risk management, fairness and transparency for AI in underwriting and pricing, and fair treatment rules apply to the whole process from ensuring unbiased data inputs and respect for data privacy laws to proper customer disclosure before concluding a transaction. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The overarching fair treatment rules apply to the entire insurance underwriting process"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Footnote 74 states the New York guidelines prohibit insurers from using AI in underwriting or pricing unless they can demonstrate that they do not unfairly or unlawfully discriminate against consumers, and give detailed steps for the assessment including quantitative metrics that should be considered. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The guidelines prohibit insurers from using AI in underwriting or pricing unless they can demonstrate that they do not"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The paper notes some life insurance products with savings or investment elements require extensive customer due diligence, so using AI to underwrite them may be challenging as the system will need to understand the applicant's context before recommending the product. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Some life insurance products with savings or investment elements require extensive customer due diligence process."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 51 states AI systems need to be properly documented (design, processes and data used), documentation of data being particularly important to explain outcomes to customers and assess which supplementary customer data is relevant; institutions need to assess whether data inputs are biased and put in place policies and measures to ensure lawful, ethical and secure collection, storage, processing/use and sharing of data, pointing to the need for robust data governance frameworks and data management tools and procedures. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "financial institutions need to assess whether data inputs are biased and put in place policies and measures"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 52 states gen AI use in credit and insurance underwriting further exacerbates explainability challenges: systems rely on billions or hundreds of billions of parameters that make tracing inputs to outputs difficult, can give different results from the same input because of designed variability (less deterministic), create novel content that is harder to explain, and analysing how biased or ethically questionable content arises needs highly technical approaches and may involve disclosing sensitive information. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "gen AI systems can give different results from the same input because they are designed to introduce"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Paragraph 53 states some US institutions address credit explainability by imposing upfront constraints on model complexity, applying post hoc techniques, or both; post hoc techniques give insight into how a model works or why it made a decision after training, for example surrogate models (SMs), simplified models approximating how complex AI models decide across the data set or for individual consumers (an example of local SMs is LIME), and feature-importance techniques (FTs) that quantify each input's contribution to a prediction (for example SHAP). (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Post hoc techniques aim to provide insights into how a model works or why it made a specific decision"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: Paragraph 50 notes that IAIS (2024a) asked insurance supervisors to make sure that insurers can give a meaningful explanation of what their AI systems produce, including how decisions or predictions are reached, particularly for use cases that could materially affect solvency or consumers. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "called for insurance supervisors to ascertain that insurers are able to meaningfully explain the outcomes of AI systems"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 54 states empirical analysis of ML credit underwriting models indicates not all explainability techniques reliably capture key aspects of model behaviour, outputs must be interpreted with a clear understanding of the underlying data, and there is no 'one size fits all' explainability solution. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "not all explainability techniques reliably capture key aspects of model behaviour"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: A recent EBA survey of European financial institutions found explainability measures used: Shapley values (40% of respondents), graphical tools (20%), enhanced reporting and documentation of model methodology (28%) and sensitivity analysis (8%). (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Shapley values (40% of respondents), graphical tools (20%), enhanced reporting and documentation of the model methodology (28%)"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Paragraph 55 states financial authorities can promote consistent application of sound explainability techniques in AI-driven underwriting; as a first step they could define basic concepts and provide guidance on key qualities when selecting explainability techniques and assessing their effectiveness, incorporate these into MRM frameworks, and consumer protection regulations may need refreshing to articulate disclosures required when individuals are denied credit or insurance based on AI decisions. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "authorities could define basic concepts and provide guidance on the key qualities to consider when selecting explainability techniques"</sub>

## Use of third-party AI services, data security and operational resilience

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 56 states use of third-party AI services appears prevalent and increasing; a 2023 survey of 1,240 respondents from business organisations in 87 jurisdictions found 78% used third-party AI models and 53% used exclusively such models; most financial institutions expected third-party AI model use to rise by 10-25% in the next 12 months; and a survey of small to mid-sized US institutions found 20% had no in-house credit modelling staff and outsourced that function. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "78% of the respondents were using third-party AI models, with 53% using exclusively such models"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 81 gives broad examples of third-party AI services: (i) providing the AI model itself that the institution customises; (ii) processing the institution's data using AI models, the output becoming input to the institution's own models; and (iii) providing AI model output that the institution uses as input to its own models. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "providing the AI model itself that financial institutions then customise to their use"</sub>

## Transparency and explainability

- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 78 says professional actuarial bodies have issued model risk management guidelines that cover AI models: Financial Reporting Council (2024) gives guidance on model governance and on identifying material biases and limitations of models, including a case study on communicating AI model performance to a non-technical audience, and Actuarial Association of Europe (2024) describes approaches to AI explainability including LIME and SHAP. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "professional actuarial bodies have issued model risk management guidelines which cover AI models"</sub>

## Use of third-party AI services, data security and operational resilience

- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 57 states the principle that the board and senior management are ultimately accountable for activities, functions, products or services provided by third parties also applies to AI; institutions are expected to have appropriate processes for selecting third-party AI models and validating them to the same standards as internally developed models. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "financial institutions are expected to have appropriate processes in place for selecting third-party AI models"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Contracts between institutions and third parties are expected to include clauses requiring third parties to provide evidence the model is appropriate for the intended use, testing results showing it works as expected, and information on the model's limitations and assumptions; third parties are typically expected to conduct ongoing performance monitoring and outcomes analysis and make modifications, and in some jurisdictions contracts give supervisory authorities a right to audit third parties. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "contracts or agreements between financial institutions and third parties are expected to include clauses requiring third parties to provide evidence"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 58 states the principle is hard in practice for AI: third-party models may not give full visibility of proprietary information such as code, requiring disclosure could expose intellectual property and confidential information and disincentivise innovation, so regulations recognise institutions may need to modify their approach, for example relying more on sensitivity analysis and benchmarking when validating third-party models. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "financial institutions may need to rely more on sensitivity analysis and benchmarking"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 59 describes a proposal, advocated by technology firms and borrowed from the cloud shared responsibility model, to delineate responsibilities by what each party controls: providers of AI models control the base/foundation model and should be responsible for documentation about it, while institutions control how the model is deployed and retrained, so regulators can look to them to ensure those processes are sound. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "clearly delineate the responsibilities of users of AI services (ie financial institutions) and their providers (ie third parties)"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 60 states that for underwriting, institutions' ongoing monitoring of third-party model performance using their own outcomes could be sufficient for reliability, but procedural fairness may still be challenging because it is unclear whether institutions can explain decisions largely driven by foundation models, institutions would face heightened reputational risk (blamed by customers whether built or bought), and requiring third parties to disclose to customers implies they must be identified and subject to oversight by financial authorities. (p. 29)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Achieving procedural fairness (ie external transparency and accountability), however, may still pose a challenge."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Paragraph 61 states third-party AI systems handling sensitive personal customer data (as in underwriting) are attractive targets for cyber attacks, data breaches and abuse and could be subject to data poisoning attacks that corrupt training data to compromise performance. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "attractive targets for cyber attacks, data breaches and abuse"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: The paper says risks of sharing data with third parties could be managed through master service agreements setting out requirements on data maintenance, access, rights, ownership and intellectual property and security, and institutions could conduct due diligence on third parties' data controls and ethical reviews of how the third party will use the data. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "master service agreements that set out requirements relating to data maintenance, access, rights, ownership and intellectual property"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 62 states third-party AI services (possibly via APIs), institutions' move of core workloads including underwriting to the cloud, and large AI providers also being major cloud service providers create interconnectivity that makes institutions more vulnerable to cyber threats and operational disruptions at AI service providers. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "makes financial institutions more vulnerable to cyber threats and operational disruptions at AI service providers"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 63 states authorities typically take an indirect approach relying on institutions to manage third-party risks (for example institutions are required to verify third parties have at least equivalent operational resilience to that expected by authorities), but institutions may lack full visibility and this may not address system-wide impact of a third-party disruption, so a few jurisdictions have or plan direct oversight of third parties critical to the financial system and more may find a case as institutions share the same cloud and AI providers. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "financial institutions are required to verify that third parties have at least an equivalent level of operational resilience"</sub>

## New players and new business arrangements

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 64 states meeting AI regulatory expectations is a challenge for non-bank lenders, especially fintech and big tech entrants using digital channels and alternative data, who may be subject to different regulations; it may be prudent to examine whether regulations for these players need adjusting for cross-sectoral AI expectations to avoid gaps, and big tech lenders that also provide cloud and AI services to banks pose risks across the banking value chain. (p. 30)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it may be prudent to examine regulations relevant to these players to determine if they require adjustments"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Paragraph 65 states novel arrangements such as Banking-as-a-Service (banks providing credit through non-bank intermediaries that serve as client interface while banks typically make credit decisions), and big techs acting as insurance intermediaries (embedded insurance, marketplaces) or technology and data providers, make it unclear who should ensure external transparency and accountability expectations are met, complicated further where credit models are supplied by third parties. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It is therefore unclear who should be responsible for ensuring that regulatory expectations regarding external transparency and accountability are met."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Paragraph 66 gives examples of authorities working with industry: the MAS co-created the Veritas Initiative, which lets institutions evaluate AI solutions against the MAS FEAT Principles and developed the FEAT assessment methodology; and the HKMA launched a GenA.I. Sandbox for banks to pilot gen AI use cases within a risk-managed framework with technical assistance and targeted supervisory feedback. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the MAS has co-created the Veritas Initiative"</sub>

## Section 5 - Conclusion

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 67 concludes AI offers institutions substantial productivity, time and cost efficiencies and unprecedented automation and accuracy in regulatory compliance, including fraud detection and AML/CFT, and the promise of better customer experiences and a more inclusive financial system. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI also offers unprecedented levels of automation and accuracy in regulatory compliance, including fraud detection and AML/CFT."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 68 concludes AI use by financial institutions, while potentially exacerbating existing risks, currently does not appear to present new ones, the risks being the usual familiar list, with AI possibly heightening model risk and data-related risks (privacy, security, bias); institutions are enhancing controls and tools and authorities are building capacity to oversee them. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "currently does not appear to present new ones"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: Paragraph 68 says AI use may have negative consequences for equality, privacy and the environment, among other factors, and that, given these societal implications, governments worldwide are producing legislation or regulations to make sure AI is used safely and responsibly. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Use of AI may have negative consequences for equality, privacy and the environment"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 69 states the need for separate comprehensive AI financial regulation is arguable because the common themes are already broadly covered, industry may await regulatory clarity before investing billions, overly wide AI definitions may capture non-high-risk systems used for decades, and the pragmatic way forward is to ensure desired regulatory outcomes are achieved regardless of the technologies used. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "ensure that the desired regulatory outcomes are achieved regardless of what technologies financial institutions use"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Area (i) Governance framework: the board and senior management are ultimately accountable for their activities including AI use cases; AI use in core business underscores a clear allocation of roles and responsibilities across the AI life cycle (design, delivery and deployment), and governance frameworks might need to specify the role of human intervention to minimise harmful outcomes. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "clear allocation of roles and responsibilities across the entire AI life cycle (ie design, delivery and deployment of AI)"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Area (ii) AI expertise and skills: the expertise needed, which may not be widely available in institutions including at board and senior management level, partly depends on the supervisory approach and proportionality (footnote 100: institutions are not expected to employ data scientists to fully understand LLMs for low-risk use cases); authorities may consider clarifying expectations for institutions planning to expand AI in core business. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial institutions are not expected to employ data scientists in order to fully understand LLMs for low-risk use cases."</sub>
- **permissive / context**: Paragraph 70 says AI raises some unique challenges in applying existing financial regulations, so AI-specific regulatory or supervisory guidance may be needed in certain areas. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI presents some unique challenges in implementing existing financial regulations"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 70 says this points to the need to examine existing regulations and, if necessary, to consider issuing clarifications, revisions or even new regulations, especially for use cases with higher risks or a significant potential impact on customers; for credit and insurance underwriting it lists six areas as important. (p. 32)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This points to the need to examine existing regulations and, if necessary, consider issuing clarifications"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Area (iii) Model risk management: authorities may need to pay close attention to institutions' model risk management given heightened model risk from lack of explainability; where MRM regulation exists it might help to define basic concepts and give guidance on key qualities for selecting explainability techniques, and where MRM is limited to regulatory-purpose models or covered in general risk regulation authorities might consider issuing MRM regulations capturing all models including AI. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it might be worthwhile for financial authorities to consider issuing model risk management regulations that capture all types of models"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Area (iv) Data governance and management: authorities may need to pay close attention to institutions' data governance and the tools and procedures enforcing it, may assess whether existing regulations (for example on model risk, consumer privacy and information security) are enough, and can support effective practice by taking stock of practices across institutions and promoting better ones. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial authorities can also support effective data governance and management by taking stock of the range of practices"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 71(i) suggests further research on institutions' risk management: little attention is paid to risk management spending to address AI risks, some budget increase can be expected though not linearly with AI spending, and actual risk management enhancements (outlined for gen AI by BCBS (2024) and IAIS (2024a)) could be mapped to heightened risks. The excerpt ends within this list of further research areas. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is not much focus on the risk management spending of financial institutions to address heightened risks from AI use"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Area (v) of paragraph 70 says that, to avoid potential regulatory gaps, regulations relevant to new or non-traditional providers of financial services would need to be assessed to determine whether they need adjusting for the cross-sectoral expectations on AI use. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "To avoid potential regulatory gaps, regulations relevant to new/non-traditional players providing financial services would need to be assessed"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Area (v) of paragraph 70 adds that a similar regulatory assessment might be needed for multi-layer arrangements for providing financial services that involve AI (for example BaaS), which may make it challenging for financial authorities to attribute accountability to the various players in the ecosystem. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A similar regulatory assessment might be needed with respect to multi-layer arrangements in providing financial services"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Area (vi) of paragraph 70 (regulatory perimeter - third parties) says the concentration of cloud and AI service providers in a few large global technology firms strengthens the argument for direct oversight frameworks for these providers; some jurisdictions have moved in that direction, while others have reinforced financial institutions' responsibility to manage third-party risks, an indirect approach that is prevalent in the financial sector. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This indirect approach is prevalent in the financial sector."</sub>

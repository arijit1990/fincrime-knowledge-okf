## Transparency and explainability

- **descriptive / definition**: Paragraph 27 states internal transparency refers to explainability, interpretability and auditability of AI models; an explainable AI model makes transparent how it arrived at an outcome, and an auditable model requires proper documentation of its design, processes and the data used. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An explainable AI model makes transparent how it arrived at a certain outcome."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The paper states explainability is especially emphasised, even more than reliability, when a model's use may have significant potential impact on customers or the public, and notes Stanford (2024b) found most foundation models remain opaque (footnote 58: 10 major developers scored on 100 transparency indicators, average score 37, top score 54). (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "found that most foundation models remain opaque"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: NIST (2023) distinguishes explainability from interpretability: explainability answers 'how' and interpretability answers 'why' a decision was made by the AI system; together with auditability they involve internal disclosure particularly to the board and senior management so they can understand risks and implications of AI use. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the former answers the question of “how” and the latter answers the question of “why” a decision"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 28 states external transparency towards customers, driven by the fairness objective, includes disclosing when customers are interacting with AI (for example their data are being used by AI), the use of AI-driven decisions affecting them and the consequences of those decisions; more recent guidance mentions explaining the decision including its logic or rationale and the AI models' contribution, and often states such disclosures should be in plain and easy-to-understand form. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "disclosing to customers when they are interacting with AI"</sub>

## Box 2 - Emerging high-level expectations on explainability in AI systems

- **descriptive / context**: Box 2 states AI guidance and model risk management (MRM) frameworks are currently the primary tools to manage AI-related risks including opacity and lack of explainability, and cites NIST's four non-binding principles of explainable AI (NIST (2021)). (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI guidance and model risk management (MRM) frameworks are currently the primary tool to manage and mitigate AI-related risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Box 2 finds the expectation of supported decision-making explanations is included in AI guidance but not always explicit in MRM frameworks, though inferable in contexts such as model validation or credit decisions. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The expectation to provide supported decision-making explanations is included in AI guidance but not always explicitly stated in MRM frameworks."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Under the four non-binding NIST (2021) principles of explainable AI, an explainable AI system should: (i) give supporting evidence or reasons for its outputs and processes (supported decision-making); (ii) give explanations that its intended users can understand (understandable explanation); (iii) accurately reflect the reasoning behind its output and faithfully represent its processes (explanation accuracy); and (iv) operate only within the conditions it was designed for and only once it has sufficient confidence in its output (capability limits). (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "four non-binding principles of explainable AI (NIST (2021))"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The paper states that FRB-OCC (2011) specifies that reports produced from model outputs should be reviewed during model validation, to check that they are accurate, complete and informative and include appropriate indicators of model performance and limitations. The paper presents this as an inference of the supported decision-making expectation in specific contexts such as model validation. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "reports generated from model outputs should be reviewed as part of the model validation process"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: For machine learning models used for regulatory capital purposes, EBA (2021) recommends that banking institutions document the results of statistical analyses involving risk drivers and output variables. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "EBA (2021) recommends that banking institutions document the outcomes of statistical analyses involving risk drivers and output variables"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) (2022) mandates creditors to provide applicants with specific reasons when an adverse action is taken against them; in some cases the obligation to explain arises only if the customer requests it. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "mandates creditors to provide applicants with specific reasons when an adverse action is taken against them"</sub>
- **descriptive / requirement**: The paper says EU consumer credit law contains the consumer's right to be informed immediately and free of charge, and the EU AI Act grants individuals the right to obtain clear and meaningful explanations from deployers about the AI system's role in the decision-making process and the key factors influencing the final decision. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the EU AI Act grants individuals the right to obtain clear and meaningful explanations from deployers"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: On explanation accuracy, the paper says AI guidance and MRM frameworks generally expect accurate and adequate explanations and there is growing consensus on disclosing material information about AI-driven decisions, information being material if its omission could influence stakeholders' decisions. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Information is considered material if its omission could influence stakeholders’ decisions."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: HKMA (2024a) suggests that financial institutions should disclose the factors influencing AI-driven decisions. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "HKMA (2024a) suggests that financial institutions should disclose the factors influencing AI-driven decisions."</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: On capability limits, the paper says AI guidance generally requires firms to communicate capabilities, limitations and risks to relevant stakeholders; EIOPA (2021) stresses highlighting system limitations, and the Central Bank of Brazil underscores that the board and senior management should clearly understand limitations and uncertainties in risk assessments, particularly where models are developed by third-party vendors. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the board and senior management should have a clear understanding of the limitations and uncertainties involved in risk assessments"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The UK Prudential Regulation Authority requires vendors to provide appropriate testing results showing their systems operate as expected and to clearly indicate the circumstances in which use may be problematic. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the UK Prudential Regulation Authority requires vendors to provide appropriate testing results"</sub>
- **recommended / recommendation**: FRB-OCC (2011) recommends mitigating model uncertainty by incorporating human judgment, reducing reliance on the model's output, or ensuring the model is supplemented by other models or approaches. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "recommend mitigating model uncertainty by incorporating human judgment, reducing reliance on the model’s output"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Qatar Central Bank's AI Guideline (2024) specifies that an entity must ensure the human overseer is given tools and authority to intervene in the operation of a high-risk AI system or interrupt it through a 'stop' button or similar procedure. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "an entity must ensure that the human overseer is given tools and authority to intervene"</sub>

## Governance and accountability

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 29 states transparency leads to greater accountability, which relates to clear roles and responsibilities and assigning ultimate responsibility to the board and senior management; documentation of how the AI model works and its control processes makes assessing fulfilment of those responsibilities easier. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "29. Transparency leads to greater accountability. Accountability relates to having clear roles"</sub>

## Box 2 - Emerging high-level expectations on explainability in AI systems

- **expected / expectation**: The paper says European Commission (2019) underlines that where an AI system has a significant effect on people's lives, stakeholders should be able to ask for an appropriate explanation of its decisions, and that such an explanation should come in good time and fit the expertise of the stakeholder concerned (consumers, regulators or internal auditors). (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "stakeholders should be able to request a suitable explanation of its decisions"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The paper says FRB-OCC (2011) stresses that reports should take into account that decision-makers and model developers often have different backgrounds and may read the same information differently. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "reports should account for the fact that decision-makers and model developers often come from different backgrounds"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The paper says EIOPA (2021) notes that simplified explanations are essential for non-technical stakeholders such as consumers, whereas technical stakeholders such as auditors need more detailed and comprehensive information to carry out their responsibilities effectively. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "simplified explanations are essential for non-technical stakeholders, such as consumers"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: The paper says the Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) (2018) and the Hong Kong Monetary Authority (HKMA) (2024a) have specified that financial institutions must tell data subjects that AI is being used, what risks are associated, and how customer data is used; this is described as reducing subjectivity in judging materiality or adequacy of disclosure. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "must inform data subjects about their use of AI, associated risks, and how customer data is being used"</sub>

## Governance and accountability

- **descriptive / expectation**: Paragraph 30 says AI guidance puts emphasis on human intervention, in order to reduce the risk of harmful outcomes from AI-based decisions, especially where AI outputs may significantly affect customers. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI guidance emphasises the role of human intervention."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Paragraph 30 names three concepts for human involvement: 'human-in-the-loop' (a human steps into the AI's decision cycle), 'human-on-the-loop' (a human steps in at the design stage and in later reviews) and, more recently, 'human-in-control' (humans have primacy in critical decisions). (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "human intervention during the design cycle and subsequent reviews"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: AI policies typically emphasise traceability by maintaining documents before and after deployment with an appropriate retention period; key elements to document are model changes and audit logs (who did what, and when), preliminary assessments, usage details (such as databases accessed and whether data matched verified identities), trails supporting outcomes, project documentation, versions of the model code, and the original data set used to develop, retrain and recalibrate the model. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Key elements to be documented include model changes and audit logs (who did what, and when)"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The paper notes development of new AI applications is becoming quicker, but assessing and validating those models typically takes longer because of firms' internal accountability processes. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Development of new AI applications is becoming quicker, but the time needed to assess and validate those models"</sub>

## Reliability/soundness

- **descriptive / expectation**: Paragraph 31 states reliability/soundness expectations for AI are closest to those for traditional models: regular independent testing or monitoring to confirm intended performance, including monitoring metrics on validity, accuracy, robustness and reliability; what differs is the focus on avoiding customer harm from inaccurate decisions or inappropriate advice, with AI risk management expected to prioritise minimising negative impact and emphasise human intervention where models cannot detect or correct errors. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "regular independent testing or monitoring to confirm that a model is performing as intended"</sub>

## Fairness, ethics and safety

- **descriptive / definition**: Paragraph 32 states AI guidance mentions two dimensions of fairness: distributive fairness (fairness of outcomes of AI-driven decisions, ie AI should be non-discriminatory; most often cited but most challenging to measure and achieve) and procedural fairness (fairness of the decision-making process, operationalised by external transparency and external accountability). (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Distributive fairness relates to the fairness of outcomes resulting from AI-driven decisions"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The paper says that although procedural fairness is theoretically easier to achieve, disclosures to customers about how an AI model works and reached a decision could prove challenging, especially for gen AI. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "disclosures to customers about how an AI model works and how it came up with a decision could prove challenging"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The paper identifies three major categories of AI bias - systemic; computational and statistical; and human-cognitive - each of which can occur even without prejudice, partiality or discriminatory intent; footnote 64 quotes NIST (2023) that systemic bias can be present in datasets, organisational norms and broader society, computational and statistical biases often stem from systematic errors due to non-representative samples, and human-cognitive biases relate to how individuals or groups perceive AI information. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "systemic; computational and statistical; and human-cognitive"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 33 states ethics is now somewhat folded into AI governance and accountability expectations, is broader than fairness (privacy and data protection, non-discrimination and equality, diversity, inclusion and social justice), and some guidance imposes measures such as an ethical code of conduct on AI use, policies for procurement and lawful processing of data, seeking diversity in input data, and careful review of training and validation data. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Ethics is now somewhat folded into AI governance and expectations on accountability."</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: Paragraph 34 states AI safety guidance emphasises that AI systems be used without causing harm or infringing human rights, with societal values including fairness and ethics integral to design, development and deployment, continuous monitoring and human oversight, and effective labelling and content provenance mechanisms to determine when content has used AI. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "avoids causing harm or infringing on human rights"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The paper notes some jurisdictions set up bodies to oversee AI safety compliance (the UK and US AI Safety Institutes), others handle it in broader oversight (the European AI Office with a safety unit) or online safety research (Singapore Centre for Advanced Technologies in Online Safety), and IAIS (2024a) highlights insurers taking steps to observe existing legal requirements, including anti-discriminatory requirements, when adopting AI. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "IAIS (2024a) highlights the importance of insurers taking steps to observe existing legal requirements"</sub>

## Data privacy and security

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 35 states data privacy/protection, safety and security have become more prominent in newer AI guidance, driven by the need for large quantities of data, fairness and ethical expectations, the potential for AI to mislead and manipulate through deepfakes and psychological profiling (increasingly convincing fraud and disinformation), and growing exposure to cyber attacks making secure AI systems that keep providing services despite disruptions indispensable. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI systems can be used to mislead and manipulate individuals through, for instance, deepfakes and psychological profiling"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Paragraph 36 says that, in line with applicable data-related laws and regulations, AI guidance requires individuals' consent to the collection, use and retention of their personal data. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI guidance requires individuals’ consent for the collection, use and retention of personal data"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 36 says such personal data should be safeguarded against privacy and confidentiality risks. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These data should be safeguarded from privacy and confidentiality risks."</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 36 says AI providers are also expected to respond effectively to individuals' requests for, among other things, correction, supplementation and deletion of their data. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI providers are also expected to effectively respond to individuals’ requests for"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Paragraph 36 says EU guidance goes further and requires a strict process for detecting and correcting biases that involve special categories of personal data, such as racial or ethnic origin, religious beliefs, health or biometric data, and sexual life or orientation. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "requires a strict process for detecting and correcting biases involving special categories of personal data"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 36 says that, with gen AI, draft guidance in China expects providers to comply with the relevant data privacy laws and regulations throughout the process of training the data used in AI systems. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the draft guidance in China expects providers to comply with relevant data privacy laws and regulations"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: Paragraph 37 states AI systems are expected to rely on sound security and resiliency standards, secured systems being those that maintain confidentiality, integrity and availability during disruption including serious cyber breaches; guidance outlines organisational and technical expectations including third-party risk management, typically risk-based, and a high-risk system is expected to operate under a strong control environment and cyber security framework preventing unauthorised employees and third parties exploiting vulnerabilities. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Secured AI systems are those that can maintain their confidentiality, integrity and availability in the event of a disruption"</sub>
- **descriptive / expectation**: The paper says guidance from Brazil (Section 38 of the Federal Senate text), China (Article 43 of the Draft AI Law) and the EU (Article 73 of the EU AI Act) increasingly envisages reporting or communication of serious cyber-related incidents to the competent authority and backup plans to promptly resume disrupted AI-related services. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "increasingly envisages reporting or communication to the competent authority and backup plans to promptly resume disrupted AI-related services"</sub>

## Consumer redress and AI literacy/awareness

## Other policy themes

- **descriptive / expectation**: Paragraph 40 states AI providers are expected to ensure gen AI systems comply with intellectual property laws (obtaining appropriate licences or permissions for training data, giving attribution to original creators of copyrighted material, and explaining transparently how copyrighted content is handled), to develop and operate gen AI using energy efficiency standards, and to keep records of development, testing and operation; climate-related disclosure commitments may oblige disclosure of the carbon footprint of AI-related services. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "obtaining appropriate licences or permissions for the use of training data"</sub>

## Consumer redress and AI literacy/awareness

- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 38 says financial institutions that use AI with a potentially significant impact on customers should give those customers ways to ask about, appeal and ask for review of AI-driven decisions that affect them. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "financial institutions using AI that may have a significant potential impact on customers should provide"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 38 gives as examples that the EU AI Act and MAS (2018) envisage deployers of AI systems having mechanisms to take into account verified and relevant supplementary data supplied by customers when reviewing AI-driven decisions. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "envisage deployers of AI systems having mechanisms in place to take into account verified and relevant supplementary data"</sub>

## Other policy themes

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 41 states many AI guidance documents highlight international interoperability (engaging internationally to support interoperability across regimes, minimise cross-border frictions and ease compliance for firms operating abroad) and encourage public-private partnerships among government, industry, academia and civil society. (p. 24)[^original]

## Section 4 - Practical issues in implementing cross-sectoral AI guidance: credit and insurance underwriting

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 42 states the common themes are not new to the financial sector and are addressed through general regulations on governance, risk management (including model risk, third-party risk, operational risk/resilience and cyber security) and consumer protection; authorities have long focused on appropriate risk management and controls, in contrast with the earlier compliance-based approach, and their role is not to manage institutions but to ensure they operate safely and soundly, including regarding AI. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "is not to manage financial institutions but to ensure that they operate in a safe and sound manner"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 69 notes some authorities have more recently issued cloud-specific regulations to address heightened security risks, though cloud use is generally still covered by general IT risk management, operational risk, operational resilience and third-party risk management regulations. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "some financial authorities have issued technology-related regulations (ie cloud-specific regulations)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 43 states few authorities have regulated AI use specifically: authorities have issued high-level principles (EBA, EIOPA, HKMA, MAS) or clarifications of how existing regulations apply (UK authorities), and among those examined only the Qatar Central Bank (QCB) and several US state insurance regulators have issued AI-specific regulations containing rules for developing, purchasing and deploying AI systems or outsourcing AI-reliant functions. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "only the Qatar Central Bank (QCB) and several US state insurance regulators"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The paper says the EBA and EIOPA may follow to clarify EU AI Act provisions that classify evaluating creditworthiness and risk assessment and pricing of health and life insurance as high-risk (credit and insurance underwriting); the IAIS concluded its Insurance Core Principles (ICPs) are sufficiently principles-based to capture AI risks, and IAIS (2024a), when finalised, will give a framework consistent with the ICPs; footnote 70 notes the NAIC (2023) model bulletin that US state regulators can use. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the IAIS has examined its Insurance Core Principles (ICPs) and concluded that they are sufficiently principles-based to capture AI risks"</sub>

## Box 3 - Risk-based policy approaches and high-risk AI systems

- **descriptive / context**: Box 3 states AI guidance increasingly follows a risk-based approach (EU AI Act, Brazilian Draft Bill 2338/2023, QCB AI Guideline) designed to address potential harm to fundamental human rights and democratic values, with more stringent restrictions, including prohibitions, the greater the potential harm. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The greater this potential harmful effect, the more stringent restrictions are imposed by policy frameworks"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Box 3 states the Brazilian Draft Bill classifies AI risks as excessive, high and other, while the EU AI Act classifies them as unacceptable, high, limited and minimal/no risk; activities generating excessive or unacceptable risk are prohibited, and the EU AI Act's prohibited examples include social scoring systems, subliminal behavioural manipulation and real-time biometric identification in public places for law enforcement. (p. 24)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "social scoring systems, subliminal behavioural manipulation and real-time biometric identification in public places for law enforcement"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Box 3 states that in finance the Brazilian Draft Bill treats AI as high-risk when used for assessing individuals' debt capacity, establishing credit ratings or biometric identification; the EU AI Act treats as high-risk AI evaluating creditworthiness or credit scores of natural persons and AI for risk assessment and pricing for natural persons in life and health insurance; and the QCB AI Guideline defines high-risk AI systems as those with potential to cause a significant negative impact on an entity's operations or the financial system. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "have the potential to cause a significant negative impact on an entity’s operations or the financial system"</sub>
- **descriptive / requirement**: Box 3 states the EU AI Act imposes more stringent requirements on providers of high-risk AI systems (risk management systems, data governance, technical documentation, record-keeping, transparency and information to deployers, human oversight, accuracy, robustness and cyber security). (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "risk management systems, data governance, technical documentation, record-keeping, transparency and provision of information to deployers"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Box 3 states that under the EU AI Act deployers of high-risk AI systems must ensure they use the system in accordance with instructions for use, apply suitable human oversight, monitor and keep logs of its operation, and inform workers' representatives when using the technology in the workplace. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "must ensure that they use the AI system in accordance with the instructions for use, apply suitable human oversight"</sub>

## Section 4 - Practical issues in implementing cross-sectoral AI guidance: credit and insurance underwriting

- **descriptive / definition**: Paragraph 44 describes underwriting as the process by which an institution determines whether an applicant qualifies for a loan or insurance and at what price; credit underwriting assesses probability of repayment by reviewing capacity and willingness to pay (credit history, income, employment stability, other liabilities), insurance underwriting assesses the applicant's risk to set premiums (for life insurance, medical history, lifestyle, age), and sound practices minimise losses from defaults or insufficient premiums. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In credit underwriting, the lender assesses the probability that an applicant can repay the loan."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The paper says discussions with authorities suggest four areas may be particularly relevant to AI in underwriting: governance and accountability; transparency and explainability; use of third-party AI services, data security and operational resilience; and new players and new business arrangements. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "use of third-party AI services, data security and operational resilience; and"</sub>

## Governance and accountability

- **descriptive / expectation**: Paragraph 45 states governance and accountability expectations in AI guidance are very similar to those already required of institutions, with specific accountabilities for underwriting: (i) model owner, with overall responsibility for development, implementation and use of underwriting models; (ii) model developers, who develop, test, evaluate and document the models; and (iii) model users, typically credit officers or insurance underwriters relying on model output; each is expected to align with the firm's AI risk management framework and risk appetite. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "model owner – this individual or team holds overall responsibility for the development, implementation and use of the underwriting models"</sub>

## Risk-based policy approaches and high-risk AI systems

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 44 says underwriting is a core process of lenders and insurers that is likely to become a focus of financial authorities' AI regulatory work, since it is a core financial and economic activity and AI use in it is likely to attract authorities' attention. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Underwriting is a core process of lenders and insurers that is likely to become a focus for AI regulatory work"</sub>

In credit underwriting the lender assesses the probability of repayment by looking at capacity and willingness to pay (credit history, income, employment stability, other liabilities); in insurance underwriting the insurer assesses the applicant's risk to set the premium (for life insurance, medical history, lifestyle, age). (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is a process by which a financial institution determines whether an applicant is qualified to be granted"</sub>

## Governance and accountability

- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 47 says that, in applying governance principles to AI, the board and senior management of financial institutions will need a sufficient level of AI expertise or familiarity to carry out their governance responsibilities, such as effectively challenging AI-driven decisions and assessing their broader effect on business strategy. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "board and senior management will need to have a sufficient level of AI expertise or familiarity"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 47 identifies the challenge for financial institutions of having the expertise to build or maintain AI systems that perform well and are also understandable to internal stakeholders (such as the board and senior management) and viewed as fair and reliable by external stakeholders (such as clients and regulators). (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "financial institutions face the challenge of ensuring that they have the necessary expertise to develop or maintain AI systems"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 47 adds that, as financial institutions use more AI, financial authorities will also need similar skills so that they can regulate and supervise effectively. (p. 25)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "financial authorities will also need similar skills to be able to effectively regulate and supervise"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 46 states new AI risk management frameworks reference general governance principles: ISO/IEC 23894 relies on ISO 31000:2018, while the NIST AI Risk Management Framework is based on four functions (govern, map, measure and manage) but also emphasises AI-specific considerations such as privacy risk, fairness and bias and human oversight; its gen AI companion resource draws out risks such as information integrity and intellectual property. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The NIST AI Risk Management Framework72 , on the other hand, is based on four all-too-familiar functions"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The paper notes a trade-off between human intervention requirements and the intended operational efficiency of AI, and that autonomous systems making their own decisions (for example automated acceptance of loan or insurance applications) could be seen as contradicting human intervention requirements. (p. 26)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is a trade-off between human intervention requirements versus the intended operational efficiency objectives"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 47 states applying governance principles to AI requires expertise and skills: the board and senior management will need sufficient AI expertise or familiarity to provide effective challenge to AI-driven decisions and assess broader impact on business strategy, staff need requisite skills to develop, deploy and manage AI risks and provide independent internal assurance, and financial authorities will also need similar skills to regulate and supervise. (p. 26)[^original]

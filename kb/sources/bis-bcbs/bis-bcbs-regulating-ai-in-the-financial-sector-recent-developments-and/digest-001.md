## Executive summary

- **descriptive / context**: The FSI Insights paper (No 63, December 2024) states that financial institutions have used AI for many years, and highlights three use cases: customer support chatbots; fraud detection, including for AML/CFT purposes; and credit and insurance underwriting. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Three AI use cases are worth highlighting: customer support chatbots; fraud detection, including for purposes of anti-money laundering"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The paper says AI use for chatbots and fraud detection is not new but the technology has improved significantly in recent years, and that for underwriting financial institutions increasingly use AI for credit scoring, valuation of collateral and assessing unstructured information from multiple sources to predict insurance risks and set premiums more accurately. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Use of AI for chatbots and fraud detection is not new, but the technology has significantly improved in recent years."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The paper finds that financial institutions are investing heavily in AI, largely because of expected wider adoption of generative AI, but seem cautious about gen AI: they experiment with it to boost operational efficiency and employee productivity, while gen AI use in customer-facing services and high-risk activities is relatively limited. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In comparison, gen AI use cases in customer-facing services and high-risk activities are relatively limited."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The paper attributes the cautious approach to gen AI to concerns about customer acceptance and impact, overreliance on third-party model providers, and regulatory uncertainty. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "concerns about customer acceptance and impact; overreliance on third-party model providers; and regulatory uncertainty"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: According to the paper, the risks AI poses when used by financial institutions are largely the same risks authorities already worry about: microprudential risks (credit, insurance, model, operational, reputational), conduct or consumer protection risks, and macroprudential or financial stability risks. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The risks AI poses when used by financial institutions are largely the same risks financial authorities are typically concerned about."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The paper notes that AI use may heighten some existing risks, such as model risk (lack of explainability makes it challenging to assess whether AI models are appropriate) and data-related risks (privacy, security, bias). (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI use may heighten some of the existing risks, such as model risk"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: International and national authorities have introduced (cross-) sectoral AI-specific guidance whose common themes are reliability/soundness, accountability, transparency, fairness and ethics; more recent guidance places more emphasis on data privacy/protection, safety and security, and the latest guidance on gen AI also covers sustainability and intellectual property. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "More recent guidance has placed increased emphasis on data privacy/protection, safety and security."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The paper states that the themes in AI guidance are interconnected, that there may be trade-offs between them when developing or upgrading guidance, and that the guidance generally allows a proportionate or risk-based approach to applying the policy expectations. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the guidance generally allows for a proportionate or risk-based approach to the application of the policy expectations"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The paper finds that the common themes in AI guidance are broadly contained in existing financial regulations on governance, risk management and consumer protection, which may explain why most financial authorities have not issued separate AI regulations; some issued only high-level principles, others clarified how existing regulations apply, and only a few authorities among those covered have issued regulations specifically addressing AI use by financial institutions. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "only a few authorities have issued regulations specifically addressing AI use by financial institutions"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: The paper says guidance on specific areas can be more important for AI used in core businesses or in higher-risk use cases with significant potential impact on customers, and that financial authorities may need to examine existing regulations and, if needed, issue clarifications, revisions or new regulations in several areas. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial authorities may need to examine existing regulations and, if needed, issue clarifications, revisions or even new regulations"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Governance framework: the paper states the board and senior management of financial institutions are ultimately accountable for their activities, including AI use cases. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The board and senior management of financial institutions are ultimately accountable for their activities, including AI use cases."</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: AI expertise and skills: the paper warns that wider AI adoption without corresponding expertise and skills could result in insufficient understanding and ineffective management of risks to financial institutions and the financial system. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could result in insufficient understanding and ineffective management of the risks to financial institutions and the financial system"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: The paper says financial authorities may consider clarifying their expectations on the expertise and skills envisaged for financial institutions planning to expand AI use in their core business activities. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Financial authorities may therefore consider clarifying their expectations regarding the expertise and skills envisaged"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Model risk management: the paper states heightened model risk can be caused by lack of explainability of AI models, and that where model risk management guidance exists, authorities might find it helpful to communicate explainability expectations and give guidance on key qualities to consider when selecting explainability techniques and assessing their effectiveness. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Heightened model risk can be caused by lack of explainability of AI models."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The paper says that the use of AI by financial institutions, 'particularly in their core business activities', would require a clear allocation of roles and responsibilities across the whole AI life cycle. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "particularly in their core business activities, would require clear allocation of roles and responsibilities across the entire AI life cycle"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: The paper says the governance framework of a financial institution might need to specify the role of human intervention, in order to minimise harmful outcomes from AI systems. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the governance framework might need to specify the role of human intervention to minimise harmful outcomes from AI systems"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: Data governance and management: the paper says many elements are captured in existing regulations (for example on model risk, consumer privacy and information security), and financial authorities may want to assess whether these are enough, need strengthening, or whether guidance addressing AI data governance and management issues is needed. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "financial authorities may want to assess whether these are enough or need strengthening"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Regulatory perimeter - third parties: the paper says concentration of cloud and AI service providers in a few large global technology firms strengthens the argument for direct oversight frameworks for these providers, depending on available legal authority; some jurisdictions have moved this way but the prevalent approach still relies on financial institutions to manage risks from third-party relationships. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The concentration of cloud and AI service providers to a few large global technology firms strengthens the argument"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The paper states the lack of a globally accepted AI definition prevents a better understanding of AI use cases in the global financial sector and the identification of areas where risks may be heightened, and suggests international public-private efforts agree a lexicon for AI and keep developing regulatory and supervisory frameworks that can adapt to rapid AI advances. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The lack of a globally accepted definition of AI prevents a better understanding of AI use cases"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: On new or non-traditional players and new business models, the paper says that, to avoid potential regulatory gaps, the regulations relevant to new/non-traditional providers of financial services would need to be assessed, to determine whether they need adjusting to reflect the cross-sectoral expectations on AI use. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "To avoid potential regulatory gaps, regulations relevant to new/non-traditional players providing financial services would need to be assessed"</sub>
- **permissive / recommendation**: The paper says a similar regulatory assessment might be needed for multi-layer arrangements for providing financial services that involve AI (for example Banking-as-a-Service), which may make it hard for financial authorities to attribute accountability to the various players in the ecosystem. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A similar regulatory assessment might be needed with respect to multi-layer arrangements in providing financial services"</sub>

## Section 1 - Introduction

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 1 states AI can be traced to the late 1950s, machine learning advanced significantly in the 1990s and deep learning took off in the 2010s, and that the late-2022 launch of ChatGPT, a gen AI application, made AI readily publicly accessible and reignited interest from the public, businesses including financial institutions, and authorities. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it was only when ChatGPT – a gen AI application – was launched in late 2022"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The paper notes IAIS (2024a) considers the OECD definition a useful reference, the EU AI Act definition converges with but falls short of fully adopting it, and definitions outside the EU are generally non-legal, non-prescriptive and non-mandated. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The definition under the European Union (EU) AI Act converges with the OECD definition but falls short"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The paper says the lack of consensus makes it challenging, particularly for global firms, to distinguish AI from non-AI and the types of AI, and that even nationally the intentionally broad AI definitions may fail to differentiate AI from non-AI systems or may inadvertently capture basic statistical models long used in the financial industry. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may inadvertently capture “basic” statistical models that have been used in the financial industry for many years"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 3 states financial institutions' use of AI preceded the explosion of gen AI applications; banks may use AI to increase operational efficiency and improve risk management, insurers use it for underwriting, risk assessment and claims management, and financial authorities are closely monitoring potential prudential, conduct and financial stability implications. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Use of AI by financial institutions preceded the explosion of gen AI applications."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 7 states that use of AI-enabled tools by financial authorities to support supervisory work (suptech tools) also precedes gen AI, that authorities face the same AI risks as financial institutions, and that this paper focuses only on financial institutions. The paper focuses mainly on banks and insurers. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to support supervisory work – so-called suptech tools – also precedes gen AI developments"</sub>

## Section 1 – Introduction

- **descriptive / definition**: Paragraph 2 says that no globally accepted definition of AI exists for financial regulatory purposes, though there is a convergence towards the OECD definition. Under it, an AI system is a machine-based system that, working towards explicit or implicit objectives, infers from its inputs how to produce outputs (predictions, content, recommendations or decisions) able to influence physical or virtual environments; AI systems differ in how autonomous and adaptive they are once deployed. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there is alignment towards the OECD definition. This states that"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 4 says that a large number of jurisdictions now have different types of policies covering AI, either specifically or within data protection, cyber security and consumer protection requirements, among others, whereas a few years earlier few jurisdictions had cross-sectoral AI-specific policies. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a large number of jurisdictions now have different types of policies that cover AI either specifically"</sub>

## Section 1 - Introduction

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 4 states many jurisdictions have introduced cross-sectoral AI-specific policies but only a small number of jurisdictions have financial authorities that issued AI-specific regulations; many cross-sectoral policies are binding legislation or non-binding guidance. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Only in a small number of jurisdictions have financial authorities issued AI-specific regulations."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The paper reports that the majority of respondents to an OECD survey (first quarter of 2024, 49 OECD and non-OECD jurisdictions) do not plan new regulations on AI use in finance in the near future, which could be explained by AI risks not being new, being addressed in existing financial regulation, and authorities taking a technology-neutral approach. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the majority of respondents to an OECD survey do not plan to introduce new regulations on AI use in finance"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Footnote 11 reports Stanford University (2024) analysis of legislation in 128 countries over 2016-23: those countries passed 148 AI-related bills in total and 32 enacted at least one AI-related bill. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these countries have passed 148 AI-related bills and 32 have enacted at least one AI-related bill"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 14 defines a technology-neutral regulatory or supervisory approach as one that does not differentiate between the different technologies, whether AI or not, that a firm may use. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "does not differentiate between the different technologies, whether AI or not, that a firm may use"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 5 states that under a risk-based approach supervisors require assurance that financial institutions understand the risks they take and have proper governance, risk management and controls to identify, monitor, manage and mitigate them; authorities may need to clarify how existing regulations apply, and may need to strengthen them or issue new ones for specific areas given AI's characteristics. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "supervisors require assurance that financial institutions understand the risks that they are taking"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 6 states the paper aims to identify practical challenges in enforcing regulatory expectations on AI by updating Prenio and Yong (2021) and looking at newer guidance from Brazil, China, the EU, Qatar, Singapore, the United Kingdom and the United States; it anchors discussion on credit and insurance underwriting, identified as high-risk under the EU AI Act. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "particularly that issued in Brazil, China, the EU, Qatar, Singapore, the United Kingdom and the United States"</sub>

## Section 1 – Introduction

- **permissive / context**: Paragraph 5 says that although financial authorities generally take a technology-neutral approach, they may need to enforce relevant provisions of cross-sectoral AI-specific policies. (p. 7)[^original]

## Section 2 – Overview of AI use cases in the financial sector

- **expected / expectation**: Paragraph 7 says financial institutions are investing heavily in adopting AI, and that the resulting transformative changes could profoundly alter how they conduct business; the paper states that this alone should warrant closer supervisory scrutiny. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this alone should warrant closer supervisory scrutiny"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Paragraph 7 reports a Statista estimate that financial-sector spending on AI will grow from USD 35 billion in 2023 to USD 97 billion in 2027. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Statista estimates that spending by the financial sector on AI will increase from USD 35 billion in 2023"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: McKinsey (2024) is cited as estimating that gen AI could generate USD 200 to 340 billion of added value each year, equal to 2.7% to 4.7% of industry revenues, chiefly through higher productivity. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "between USD 200 billion and USD 340 billion in value annually, or 2.7% to 4.7% of total industry revenues"</sub>

## Section 2 - Overview of AI use cases in the financial sector

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The paper reports banking sector spending on gen AI alone is expected to rise from USD 3.86 billion in 2023 to almost USD 85 billion in 2030, much of it on headcount and IT infrastructure, and that major banks are increasing AI talent headcount while cutting headcount elsewhere, suggesting expected productivity gains that can replace human resources. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "from USD 3.86 billion in 2023 to almost USD 85 billion in 2030"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Footnote 18 gives examples: JPMorgan Chase estimates the value of its AI deployment at around USD 1 to 1.5 billion in productivity and cost reduction, citing know-your-client file processing (from around 155,000 files in 2022 to 230,000 in 2025 with 20% less staff); DBS Singapore has deployed over 800 AI models across 350 use cases with estimated economic impact exceeding SGD 1 billion in 2025. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "from around 155,000 in 2022 to 230,000 in 2025 but with 20% less staff needed to do so"</sub>

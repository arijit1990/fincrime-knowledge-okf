## Section 2 - Overview of AI use cases in the financial sector

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The paper says chatbots might be an area of focus for financial regulators because of their wide and growing reach: CFPB (2023) estimates 37% of the US population interacted with a bank's chatbot in 2022, and use is expected to increase as human-interfacing AI improves (for example verbal conversation in different languages). (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "CFPB (2023) estimates that 37% of the US population interacted with a bank’s chatbot in 2022."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Footnotes 28-30 give chatbot figures: Forrester (2023) estimates a chatbot in a stylised financial institution cut human interaction handle time by up to 30%; DBS digibot can execute loan applications with instant fund transfers; Bank of America's Erica had 1.5 billion interactions with more than 37 million clients between June 2018 and June 2023; Bradesco's chatbot reportedly answers 283,000 questions a month with 95% accuracy. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "reduced human interaction handle time by up to 30%"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 14 states AI use to detect money laundering, terrorism financing and fraud is not new; what is new is the more widespread use of AI tools by financial institutions and their improving accuracy. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "What is new is the more widespread use of AI tools by financial institutions, and their improving accuracy."</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The paper says AI tools assist in flagging rapid movement of money into different accounts and transactions that significantly deviate from anticipated patterns, and are becoming more effective in identifying suspicious individuals, mule accounts and organised groups that exploit vulnerabilities in rules-based systems. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Such AI tools assist in flagging the rapid movement of money into different accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The paper states AI AML/CFT tools are reducing the number of alerts or false positives, freeing time for institutions to investigate legitimate cases comprehensively. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The tools are reducing the number of alerts or false positive cases"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The paper describes an AI fraud management system for payments fraud from digital financial services: it can prevent or detect suspicious payments and promptly alert institutions to unusual transactions so they can decide whether to approve or reject them, and it can adjust to unique customer behaviours and evolve with the business. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An AI fraud management system can be used to prevent or detect suspicious payments"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Footnote 31 reports HSBC estimates its AI AML tool identifies two to four times more suspicious activities than its previous system while reducing alerts by 60%, giving human investigators more time on genuine cases, and that the tool also allows identification of criminal networks. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identifies two to four times more suspicious activities than its previous system, while reducing the number of alerts by 60%"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 15 states underwriting is an area of increasing AI use, with some insurers appearing more advanced than banks; insurance underwriting may use simple questionnaires (for example life insurance) or complex assessments needing physical examination and written assessments (for example commercial property insurance), and AI, in particular gen AI, can assess unstructured information from multiple sources to predict risks and set premiums more accurately. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "with some insurers appearing to be more advanced than banks"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The paper states machine learning has long been used in bank credit underwriting for credit scoring, valuation of collateral, calculating the interest rate to charge and personalisation of loan offers, sometimes with synthetic data (data artificially generated, for instance by algorithms); footnote 33 notes synthetic data can create hypothetical data sets covering different credit behaviours to train AI without biases present in actual data sets. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In banking, machine learning has been used for many years in credit underwriting."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 16 states AI underwriting can help address challenges including, for credit underwriting, high operational cost from time-consuming manual processes, fraud risk and subjectivity; AI could greatly enhance credit scoring using unstructured (non-traditional financial) data. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI could greatly enhance credit scoring by making use of unstructured data"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The paper states AI can automate underwriting, analyse large volumes and varied forms of data, improve identity verification and enhance customer experience, reduce underwriting cost, support financial inclusion and improve efficiency (for example faster approvals); gen AI offers new ability to process large volumes of text in insurance. Footnote 35 cites a reduction of approval time by more than two days and 94% accuracy in credit analysis calculations. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI can reduce underwriting cost, support financial inclusion"</sub>

## Risks arising from banks' and insurers' AI use cases

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 17 states AI adoption offers significant benefits but exposes banks and insurers to a range of risks requiring careful management, and Tables 2 to 5 give a non-exhaustive list of such risks. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Tables 2 to 5 provide a non-exhaustive list of such risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Table 2 (microprudential risks) - credit risk: underestimation of probability of default or risk of loss due to inaccurate data inputs. Insurance risk: underpricing of insurance policies due to AI models trained on historical data not capturing latest developments (for example new disease outbreaks). (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Underestimation of probability of default or risk of loss due to inaccurate data inputs"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Table 2 - model risk includes: inaccurate output because the model does not capture changes in the nature of the data input (model drift); lack of explainability hindering assessment of conceptual/technical soundness; inaccurate output from overfitting or underfitting; hallucination, inconsistent responses and dependency on data quality; overestimating AI capabilities leading to misuse beyond them; and unreliable predictions if models are not trained with the most recent information. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Lack of model explainability hinders the ability to assess its conceptual/technicalsoundness"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Table 2 - cyber risk includes: greater vulnerability to cyber attacks from more contact points with external providers and IT interconnectivity; inadequate access control allowing unauthorised access to training data and the AI model; susceptibility to data poisoning attacks that alter training data for malicious purposes; and threat actors 'stealing' a model by querying it iteratively to build a functionally equivalent model. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI models may be susceptible to data poisoning attacks that alter the training data setsfor malicious purposes"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Table 2 - other operational risk: firms with legacy IT systems may add complexity to their IT architecture, increasing potential operational risks from IT failures. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Firms with legacy IT systems may add complexity to their IT architecture"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Table 2 (continued, operational risk) - increased use of third-party services (data and AI model providers) could lead to dependency, disruption of critical services and lack of control, exacerbated by vendor lock-in and market concentration; and risk controls may quickly become obsolete due to rapid updates by AI systems. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Quick obsolescence of risk controls due to rapid updates by AI systems"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Table 2 - reputational risk: operational failures, potentially from overdependency on third-party providers, can damage public trust; adverse publicity from unfair customer treatment or regulatory penalties can erode firms' reputation. Strategic risk: institutions partnering with other firms may lose control over critical functions such as business origination and customer relationships, potentially causing significant liquidity issues and financial instability if partners redirect business or alter key processes. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Operational failures, potentially due to overdependency on third-party providers, candamage public trust and confidence"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Table 2 - legal risk: firms may be liable for copyright infringement from unauthorised use of copyrighted data to train AI models, and may face legal liability from inaccurate or inappropriate responses given by customer-facing AI tools. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Firms may be liable for copyright infringement due to unauthorised use of copyrighteddata in training AI models"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Table 3 (conduct/consumer protection risks) - unfair treatment of customers: exploiting consumer vulnerability characteristics to charge unfair prices; discriminatory decisions based on biased data or personal information in alternative data that perpetuate bias; financial exclusion of perceived high-risk customers. Price collusion: collusive pricing implemented by automating price adjustments based on competitors' price changes. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Exploiting characteristics of vulnerability of consumers to charge unfair prices"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Table 4 (macroprudential/financial stability risks) - herding behaviour: procyclical behaviour amplified by multiple institutions using similar data sets and AI models, and AI outputs contributing to systemically biased conclusions, distorted asset prices or increased price correlations. Interconnectedness and concentration: concentrated AI third-party providers could cause systemic risk if hit by cyber attacks or operational failures. Opacity and complexity: limits to explainability of complex models create risk management challenges and give institutions and supervisors less insight into the build-up of systemic risks. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Amplification of procyclical behaviour due to the use of similar data sets and AI models bymultiple financial institutions"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Table 5 (other risks) - market competition risk: high cost of developing and maintaining AI may limit adoption to larger institutions, raising their market power and systemic importance and making it hard for smaller firms to compete. Data privacy risk: AI models may be manipulated to leak personal or sensitive information used in training and using the models. Environmental risk: heightened AI use will increase energy demand, which may contribute to climate change. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI models may be manipulated to leak personal or sensitive information used in trainingand using the models"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Table 5 footnote reports UK Government (2024) estimates that in 2026 computing power for AI will consume roughly the same electricity as smaller European countries such as Austria or Finland. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "computing power for AI will consume roughly the same amount of electricity as smaller European countries"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 18 describes AI as a double-edged sword for cyber resilience: it can strengthen cyber security by proactively detecting threats (including AI deepfakes) and identifying vulnerabilities and unusual patterns, and gen AI could enable realistic simulation attacks and real-time adaptive cyber security posture. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AI can significantly strengthen cyber security by proactively detecting threats"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The paper says cyber criminals can use similar AI tools for more sophisticated attacks, for instance targeting vulnerabilities in underlying models or data or generating realistic fake profiles for social engineering, which can be adapted in real time and automated at great scale and are harder to detect. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "generating realistic fake profiles to be used in social engineering attacks"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The paper reports a 2024 global cyber security survey in which the majority believed gen AI would give attackers an overall cyber advantage in the next two years, while a third expected a balance between attackers and defenders; regulators are increasingly attentive to AI used to exploit firms' cyber vulnerabilities, and UK Government (2024) finds no substantial evidence yet that general purpose AI can automate sophisticated cyber security tasks. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the majority of respondents believed that in the next two years gen AI would provide overall cyber advantage to attackers"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Footnote 40 reports a BOE and FCA (2024) industry survey found that the highest perceived benefits of AI include its use for cyber security; footnote 44 notes New York Department of Financial Services (2024b) gives guidance on managing cyber security risks arising from AI. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "found from their industry survey that the highest perceived benefits of AI include its use for cyber security"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 19 states consolidation of AI service providers within big techs is a particular concern for industry and regulators because it may expose financial institutions to heightened concentration risks; big techs' access to data, computational power and expertise gives a first-mover advantage and attracts closer supervisory scrutiny for microprudential and financial stability risks. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Consolidation of AI service providers within big techs is a particular concern for both the industry and regulators"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The paper notes that in 2023 the FSB published a toolkit for institutions and authorities to manage and oversee third-party risks, and in 2024 the Federal Trade Commission launched an investigation into gen AI investments and partnerships between AI companies and major cloud service providers. (p. 14)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FSB published a toolkit for financial institutions and financial authorities to manage and oversee third-party risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The paper reports BCBS (2024) noted banks' increasing reliance on third-party technology services introduces cyber risks and potential systemic vulnerabilities; IAIS (2024a) highlighted the importance of insurers regularly assessing reliance on AI service providers that may pose a risk to their business; ECB (2024) highlighted that technological penetration and supplier concentration can give rise to systemic risk. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "IAIS (2024a) highlighted the importance of insurers regularly assessing their reliance on AI service providers"</sub>

## Box 1 - Gen AI in financial services

- **descriptive / definition**: Box 1 defines gen AI as AI applications that can generate new content, including text, images or music, from a natural language prompt, relying on machine learning models, mainly deep learning, that identify and encode patterns in enormous amounts of data. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Gen AI refers to AI applications that can generate new content, including text, images or music"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Box 1 states gen AI is becoming more accessible as cloud providers expand offerings, big techs dominate the market owning the majority of foundation models (models trained on broad data sets for a wide range of tasks), and very high training costs are a barrier to entry for smaller firms; a footnote cites Stanford University (2024a): 97 of 163 foundation models released in 2019-2023 are owned by Google, OpenAI, Meta and Microsoft, and training GPT-4 and Gemini Ultra cost roughly USD 78 million and USD 191 million respectively. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "big techs continue to dominate the gen AI market, owning the majority of foundation models"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Box 1 states foundation models need adjustments to be fit for purpose in finance, and a technique to make gen AI outputs more relevant is retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), through which firms can control the context of a foundation model using their own information or data. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Through RAG, firms can control the context of a foundation model using its own information or data."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Box 1 states there are not yet widespread gen AI use cases by banks and insurers for revenue generation; insurers seem to have more gen AI use cases than banks, probably because insurance products involve more unstructured data, heterogeneous contract terms and large amounts of data from different sources, for example helping human underwriters identify policies and terms faster. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Insurers seem to have more gen AI use cases than banks."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Box 1 lists reasons firms are particularly cautious about customer-facing gen AI: heightened risk exposures such as mis-selling or wrong advice; the high bar for regulatory requirements such as validating model results; firms' own internal risk management policies such as customer information disclosure requirements before concluding a transaction; lack of clarity on who is ultimately accountable if results are wrong; lack of consumer trust in interacting with gen AI; and overreliance on third-party model providers. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "lack of clarity on the party ultimately accountable if the model results are wrong"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Box 1 states gen AI risks are mainly an extension or amplification of existing model risks, but gen AI also gives rise to unique risks related to anthropomorphism (treating AI models as having human-like qualities), with users possibly wrongly believing models can think, reason or display emotions. (p. 15)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "gen AI gives rise to unique risks related to anthropomorphism"</sub>

  <sub>anchor: "The technical performance of AI models is rapidly improving, surpassing human capabilities according to a study"</sub>

## Box 1 - Gen AI in financial services

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Box 1 reports BCBS (2024) highlights gen AI can hallucinate, generating inaccurate or inappropriate responses and giving different responses over time to similar prompts because outputs are characterised by randomness; Perez-Cruz and Shin (2024) explain gen AI models are susceptible to reasoning errors and cognitive limit; a footnote cites a study estimating LLM hallucination rates between 1.4% and 4.2%. These risks contribute to the cautious rollout of customer-facing gen AI. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "by producing different responses over time, even when given similar questions or prompts"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Box 1 states the democratisation of gen AI has accelerated institutions' strengthening of internal AI governance and risk management; some firms banned gen AI while working out guardrails; new structures include senior management committees screening gen AI use cases under a risk-based approach; use cases with complex models, autonomous decision-making and customer-facing impact attract greater scrutiny and controls; and firms are establishing use case and risk registries and 'AI factories' with dedicated staff and infrastructure. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "formation of senior management committees to screen gen AI use cases under a risk-based approach"</sub>

## Risks arising from banks' and insurers' AI use cases

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 20 states anticipated widespread AI use without adequate supervisory oversight and sound risk management in firms could threaten the safety and soundness of the financial sector, that even late adopters may be pushed by fear of missing out, and that regulators may need to anticipate AI becoming integral across the value chain and understand the risks so they can ascertain whether their existing toolkit remains fit for purpose. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could pose threats to the safety and soundness of the financial sector"</sub>

## Section 3 - Overview of cross-sectoral AI-specific guidance

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 21 states the G20 emphasised human-centric and trustworthy AI in AI Guidelines adopted in 2019 that built on the OECD AI Principles, and that the G7 leaders endorsed 'the Hiroshima AI Process Comprehensive Policy Framework' in December 2023 (the process launched in May 2023), providing guiding principles and a code of conduct for safe, secure and trustworthy advanced AI systems. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The G20 has emphasised the need for human-centric and trustworthy AI."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The paper notes UNESCO's recommendations on the ethics of AI were adopted by all 193 UN member states in November 2021, the UN adopted its first resolution on AI in March 2024 (emphasising sustainable development), and published its final report on global AI governance in September 2024. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "adopted by all 193 UN member states in November 2021"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 22 describes the OECD AI Principles as a key, non-binding reference for national AI policy, adopted in 2019 and updated in 2024, guiding trustworthy AI based on inclusiveness, sustainability and well-being; human rights and democratic values including fairness and privacy; transparency and explainability; robustness, security and safety; and accountability. The 2024 update reflects technological developments, particularly the growing importance of gen AI. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These non-binding principles were initially adopted in 2019 and updated in 2024."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 54 lists OECD recommendations for policymakers: investing in AI research and development; fostering an inclusive AI-enabling ecosystem; shaping an interoperable governance and policy environment; building human capacity and preparing for labour market transition; and international cooperation on trustworthy AI. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "investing in AI research and development; fostering an inclusive AI-enabling ecosystem"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 23 states jurisdictional AI policy approaches are broadly principles-based (non-binding principles and voluntary commitments backed by technical standards or cross-sectoral regulations, for example Singapore, United Kingdom, United States, which consider it too early to regulate forcefully) or rules-based (issued or issuing AI legislation, for example Brazil, China, European Union and Qatar, aiming at regulatory clarity and legal powers of enforcement against unlawful AI deployment). (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Jurisdictions opting for a rules-based approach have issued or are in the process of issuing AI legislation"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 24 states AI guidance generally allows proportionate or risk-based application: proportionality avoids unnecessary or disproportionate costs and burdens, policy stringency varies with outcomes an AI system is likely to generate, and the risk-based rationale is to foster innovation without compromising trustworthy AI, including excluding non-consequential uses (for example summarising internal meeting minutes). (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The policy measures vary in stringency based on the outcomes that an AI system is likely to generate"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Footnote 55 notes regulatory treatment may vary by client type (wholesale versus retail); for example reinsurers' AI use with respect to primary insurers may not attract the same scrutiny as AI use affecting retail policyholders. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "may not attract the same level of regulatory scrutiny as AI use cases that impact retail policyholders"</sub>

## Section 3 – Overview of cross-sectoral AI-specific guidance

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 23 says that some of the jurisdictions taking a rules-based approach to AI (those that have issued or are preparing AI legislation) consider it imperative to protect consumers' rights from potential harms. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Some of these jurisdictions consider it imperative to protect consumers"</sub>

## Section 3 - Overview of cross-sectoral AI-specific guidance

- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 25 states Prenio and Yong (2021) identified five common themes (reliability/soundness, accountability, transparency, fairness and ethics); more recent guidance continues to cover these except that explicit coverage of ethics is less evident, and consistently highlights security, safety, explainability and data privacy, plus sustainability and intellectual property with gen AI, and consumer redress, awareness and training, international interoperability and public-private partnerships. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "More recent AI-related guidance continues to cover these themes except for ethics"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Paragraph 26 states the common themes are interconnected with possible trade-offs; transparency is considered as enabling assessment of the other themes, since without it assessing reliability and enforcing accountability, fairness and ethics is challenging, while there may be a trade-off between reliability and transparency (including explainability) because precision may need more data inputs or parameters, as with gen AI, making models more complex and in turn affecting fairness decisions. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Transparency, for example, is considered as enabling the assessment of the other themes"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The paper cites Aldasoro et al (2024) as providing a framework for regulating gen AI and AI agents in finance built on core activities (govern, map, measure and manage) and main stages of the AI value chain (design and training; deployment and usage; longer-term diffusion); footnote 57 defines AI agents as systems built on advanced LLMs such as GPT-4 or Claude 3, with planning capabilities, long-term memory and typically access to external tools such as executing code, using the internet or performing market trades. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "core activities in dealing with AI (ie govern, map, measure and manage)"</sub>

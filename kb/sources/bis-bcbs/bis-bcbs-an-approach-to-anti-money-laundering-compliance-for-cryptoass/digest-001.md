## Key takeaways

- **descriptive / finding or result**: BIS Bulletin No 111 (13 August 2025) states that existing AML approaches relying on trusted intermediaries have limited effectiveness with decentralised record-keeping in permissionless public blockchains. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Existing anti-money laundering (AML) approaches relying on trusted intermediaries have limited effectiveness"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The estimated volume of cryptocurrencies used in illicit activities reached $51.3 billion in 2024; the figures are probably a lower bound as they exclude cryptoassets used for money laundering in non-crypto-related crimes (e.g. drug trafficking) and only count known illicit addresses. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The estimated volume of cryptocurrencies used in illicit activities reached $51.3 billion in 2024"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Current AML compliance rules rely on regulated financial intermediaries, especially banks: in an intermediary-based system a payment debits the sender's account and credits the receiver's account, customer checks can be conducted at the time of account updates and the duty to perform them falls on the intermediary, domestically and through the correspondent banking network. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Customer checks can be conducted at the time of account updates, and the duty to perform them"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Existing international standards attempt to apply intermediary-based AML principles to crypto, but there are clear limits: permissionless blockchains rely on decentralised consensus among dispersed self-interested validators, so no individual intermediary may be held accountable for the account update. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "No individual intermediary may be held accountable for the account update"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A footnote states that FATF standards require virtual asset service providers and financial institutions to obtain, hold and transmit specific originator and beneficiary information when transferring virtual assets (the "travel rule"). (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to obtain, hold and transmit specific originator and beneficiary information when transferring virtual assets"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Bulletin says the open transaction history of blockchains can support AML and other compliance work (for example FX regulations), because the provenance and history of any given unit or balance of a cryptoasset, stablecoins included, can be drawn on. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The public transaction history on blockchains can enable AML and other compliance efforts, such as FX regulations"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: A score reflecting how likely a given cryptoasset unit or balance is to be tied to illicit activity could be consulted where crypto meets the banking system (the off-ramps); the Bulletin says this would stop proceeds of illicit activity flowing in and would foster a culture of duty of care among crypto market participants. (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AML compliance score based on the likelihood that a particular cryptoasset unit or balance is linked with illicit activity"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Bulletin reports that from 2022 stablecoins displaced bitcoin as criminals' preferred crypto asset, and that in 2024 they made up roughly 63% of all illicit crypto transactions (sources cited: Chainalysis (2025); TRM Labs (2025)). (p. 3)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Since 2022, stablecoins have overtaken bitcoin as the asset of choice among criminals using crypto"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Customer verification can be performed at points of contact with the conventional monetary system (e.g. crypto exchanges) when funds move into and out of the exchange, but once claims move to unhosted wallets on the permissionless blockchain, transactions are out of reach of conventional forms of intervention; unhosted wallets, unlike hosted wallets offered by exchanges that do KYC, allow users to transact without identification. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "once claims move to unhosted wallets on the permissionless blockchain itself, the transactions are out of the reach"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: While stablecoin issuers have frozen balances at authorities' request in high-profile financial crime cases, such an approach is unrealistic to cover billions of day-to-day transactions. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "such an approach is unrealistic to cover billions of day-to-day transactions"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: The Bulletin explores an alternative approach using the features that make permissionless blockchains impervious to traditional approaches: because the full transaction history is publicly available, it could inform an assessment of how closely a unit of a cryptoasset is associated with past or current illicit activity, and a diagnostic "AML compliance score" could be referenced in interventions by authorities when cryptoassets (including stablecoins) are presented for conversion to fiat at off-ramps, notably the point of contact with the banking system. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A diagnostic “AML compliance score” could be referenced in any further interventions by authorities"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: The specific assessment criteria could vary across jurisdictions; for example, where foreign exchange regulations are maintained, the definition of illicit activity could encompass transactions violating them, and such safeguards could help slow erosion of monetary sovereignty given widespread cross-border use of stablecoins. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the definition of illicit activity could encompass transactions that violate those regulations"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Bulletin presents a range of implementation options and the possible broader impact on the crypto ecosystem, including endogenous changes in the behaviour of illicit actors and shifts in the allocation of the "duty of care" between crypto users and authorities. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "endogenous changes in the behaviour of illicit actors and associated shifts in the allocation of the “duty of care”"</sub>

## Record-keeping and cryptoasset transfers on permissionless blockchains

- **descriptive / context**: Cryptoassets on permissionless public blockchains repudiate trusted intermediaries such as central banks and commercial banks; a dispersed network of self-interested record-keepers ("validators" or "miners") maintains the record in a decentralised way, with each validator incentivised through rewards and fees to follow the consensus protocol provided others do so, so that the system is self-sustaining. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "rely on a dispersed network of self-interested record-keepers"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: In a permissionless public blockchain, the entire history of transfers serves as a "master ledger" and as a substitute for intermediaries that maintain account balances. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the entire history of transfers on the blockchain serves as a “master ledger”"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Bitcoin blockchain follows the unspent transaction output (UTXO) model, in which holding and transferring bitcoin are defined by the history of UTXOs and no account balances are recorded as banks do; the bitcoin in a wallet is a collection of unspent records from previous transactions, each transaction references these records to generate a new one, and users can trace records back to the original minting of the bitcoins. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The holding and transferring of bitcoin are defined entirely by the history of UTXOs"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: Footnote 4 explains that on blockchains such as Bitcoin, a UTXO (unspent transaction output) stands for an amount of cryptocurrency that a sender has authorised and that the recipient has not yet spent. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a UTXO represents a given amount of cryptocurrency that has been authorised by a sender"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Stablecoins follow an "account-based" token standard on programmable blockchains (e.g. Ethereum or Tron), which record balances as account updates, so once a unit enters a wallet it is indistinguishable from other units there; this differs from Bitcoin where UTXOs in a wallet remain unmixed and individually traceable. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "once a unit of a stablecoin enters a wallet, it is indistinguishable from other units in that wallet"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Stablecoins circulate on account-based blockchains because they allow greater programmability features, a higher number of transactions per second and lower fees. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "greater programmability features, a higher number of transactions per second and lower fees"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: In account-based blockchains, tracking the individual token or UTXO is not possible, but stablecoins allow some degree of transaction traceability because wallet addresses that have previously transacted on the public blockchain can be mapped. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "tracking the individual token or UTXO is not possible"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The stablecoin market is dominated by centralised fiat-backed stablecoins, where only the issuer can mint new coins on receiving fiat and burn them on payout; the tree of wallets for any stablecoin can be traced back to its minting origins until it is burned, and this centralisation allows the issuer to freeze stablecoins in a wallet or deny their conversion to fiat. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The tree of wallets for any stablecoin can be traced back to its minting origins until it is burned"</sub>

## AML compliance scores for cryptoassets

- **permissive / method**: The Bulletin proposes that a system of summary scores could be the basis of diagnostic tools and AML compliance scoring at points of contact with the conventional monetary system; a score referencing UTXOs for bitcoin or wallets for stablecoins could use blockchain information, including the full transaction history and the wallets passed through. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a system of summary scores could be the basis of diagnostic tools and AML compliance scoring mechanisms"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: In the illustrative score design, a higher value (e.g. maximum 100) denotes relatively clean funds coming mostly from "allow-listed" wallets, while a lower value (e.g. minimum zero) denotes funds tainted by association with one or more wallets known to be on a "deny list". (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A higher value (eg maximum 100) would denote relatively clean funds, coming mostly from “allow-listed” wallets"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: The score for a wallet can be assessed against a threshold value chosen by authorities following jurisdictional considerations to determine whether off-ramp transactions with that wallet are allowed or denied; crypto exchanges, stablecoin issuers and banks could apply safeguards by considering minimum score requirements for cashing out crypto coins. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "assessed against a threshold value chosen by authorities following jurisdictional considerations"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: AML scoring can be designed to reflect compliance with various existing rules, such as adherence to foreign exchange regulations in some economies; by incorporating jurisdiction-specific requirements, the scoring can reference local rules, and leveraging traceability the scores could further address criteria such as taxation and consumer protection, so scores could vary with the type of rules applied. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AML scoring can be designed to reflect compliance with various existing rules"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: At its most stringent, AML compliance would require off-ramps to accept tokens for conversion only if they have passed through addresses that met KYC compliance checks (an "allow list"), implying all users including those with unhosted wallets would undergo KYC, as bank clients do when opening an account; using smart contract functionalities, exchanges and other wallet providers could be required to block transactions from or to addresses not on the allow list. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the strongest form of AML compliance would require off-ramps to accept tokens for conversion only if"</sub>

## AML compliance scores for cryptoassets

- **permissive / method**: At the permissive end of the stringency range, AML compliance would test whether a coin has passed through an address known to be linked to illicit activity, that is, an address on a deny list. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a permissive form of AML compliance is to check whether a coin has passed through an address known to be associated with illicit activity"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: For bitcoin, the deny-list test can establish whether a UTXO, or part of it, has ever passed through a deny-listed address. For stablecoins the full provenance of specific coins cannot be tracked, but transactions going directly to or from illicit addresses, or to addresses that directly dealt with deny-listed addresses, could be flagged so authorities can intervene at the off-ramp. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "For bitcoin, it is possible to assess whether a UTXO (or part of it) has"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: A footnote states that an endogenous response whereby illicit activities move to alternative wallets is to be expected, including mixers or unregulated exchanges performing off-chain transactions between clients; systematic efforts to circumvent rules could also be observed and taken into account in AML scoring at off-ramps. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "An endogenous response whereby illicit activities move to alternative wallets is to be expected"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: Between the two extremes lie intermediate levels of stringency; given potential for regulatory circumvention and laundering through mule or dormant wallets, multiple criteria and fund-transfer patterns could be considered, such as requiring that the most recent recipients of a UTXO or wallet addresses are on an allow list, that an address has not transacted with deny list addresses, that funds have remained on an allow list for a certain period, or differentiating allowable transfer amounts by score range. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "multiple criteria and patterns of fund transfers could be considered"</sub>
- **permissive / typology or red flag**: A history of transactions with protocols known to be used to facilitate money laundering could raise red flags for further scrutiny. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A history of transactions with protocols known to be used to facilitate money laundering could raise red flags"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: The approaches require defining which actor is responsible for preventing illicit flows, which could be the individual user, the crypto intermediary (crypto exchange or VASP), or other parties such as a system of clearing houses converting stablecoins into fiat. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "these approaches would require defining which actor is responsible for preventing illicit flows"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A footnote notes that if these AML approaches were widely adopted, seizure risk for token recipients could be mitigated; an alternative is to treat tokens like banknotes and explicitly protect recipients who receive tokens for value, in good faith and without notice of a claim (Jeng (2025)), but this could undermine the duty of care and incentivise (known) illicit activity. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This approach could, however, undermine the duty of care and incentivise (known) illicit activity"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Graph 2 lays out the stringency spectrum with three labelled positions: strong (allow list), intermediate (multiple criteria) and weak (deny list). The graph's test questions are 'KYC check?', 'Block off-ramp unless all tokens are clean', 'Tokens held over 6 months?', 'No interaction with suspicious addresses' and 'No links to deny list?'; the extracted text does not show which question belongs to which position. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Strong Intermediate WeakAllow list Multiple criteria Deny list"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The graph notes define an indirectly tainted token as one indirectly associated with deny-list addresses via intermediary addresses or multi-step transactions, and a directly tainted token as one directly linked to deny-list addresses. The graphs show four token categories: clean, indirectly tainted, directly tainted and fully tainted. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Indirectly tainted token = indirectly associated with addresses on the deny list through intermediary addresses or multi-step transactions"</sub>
- **descriptive / recommendation**: The Bulletin calls it a pragmatic approach to place reliance on the points at which cryptoassets and stablecoins are converted to fiat currency, and says a duty of care on those entities would give them reason not to take in or pay out tainted coins, since non-compliance could bring fines or other penalties; individual holders could also face specific requirements. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A pragmatic approach is to rely on the points where cryptoassets and stablecoins are converted into fiat currency"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Because blockchain transactions are public, the Bulletin expects more third-party providers of compliance services to appear; a user's claim to have received a tainted token in good faith would carry less weight once such services are widespread and affordable. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "such an argument would be less persuasive if there were widespread and affordable compliance service providers"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In a setting with widespread compliance services, the Bulletin says users could reasonably be expected to exercise a duty of care, checking before transacting whether a crypto coin is known to be compromised. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "users could reasonably be expected to exercise a duty of care in transacting with crypto tokens"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: According to the Bulletin, approaches that make responsibility clear give well-intentioned actors an incentive to look for illicit activity and report it to the authorities. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Approaches that clearly define responsibility create incentives for good actors to seek out illicit activity"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: A footnote notes proposals for a "bounty hunter" approach to compliance, whereby licensed firms could receive compensation for reporting suspicious activity to regulators (Kellerman (2025)), comparable with "bug bounties" paid for finding cyber vulnerabilities. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "There are even proposals for a “bounty hunter” approach to compliance"</sub>

## Broader policy considerations

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Crypto and stablecoins pose challenges to existing regulatory frameworks; their risks differ from those of traditional financial instruments, meaning the principle of "same risk, same regulation" does not apply (Aldasoro et al (2025)). An approach using the unique features of crypto and stablecoins to enhance regulation emerges as a promising avenue to close regulatory gaps. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "meaning the principle of “same risk, same regulation” does not apply"</sub>
- **permissive / context**: Incorporating the provenance of crypto coins into regulations could support AML/CFT efforts and strengthen financial system integrity; differentiating stablecoins by provenance would make histories an essential attribute, so stablecoins that passed through wallets with a chequered history could trade at a higher discount than others. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "could trade at a higher discount relative to others with no such history"</sub>
- **permissive / method**: Compliance scores could be used at off-ramps and could also accompany the token as it moves within the permissionless blockchain, by embedding the score into the UTXO or wallet itself, establishing a duty of care among users that could influence behaviour even among those using only unhosted wallets and create an incentive to transact with clean allow-listed wallets, generating a positive feedback loop in terms of compliance. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "embedding the score into the UTXO or wallet itself"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: International cooperation across jurisdictions would significantly improve regulatory outcomes because cryptoassets straddle borders and pose policy challenges requiring effective cooperation; jurisdiction-level rules could allow greater scope for international cooperation at subsequent stages of rule development. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "International cooperation across jurisdictions would significantly improve regulatory outcomes"</sub>

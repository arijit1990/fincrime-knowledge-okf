## Recitals

- **descriptive / context**: Block, Inc. is a publicly-traded financial services and technologies company; it reported 2023 revenue of $21.91 billion and total assets that grew from $15.02 billion (2021) to $34.06 billion (2023). (p. 1)[^original]

  <sub>anchor: "has been licensed by the Department to operate a money transmission business in New York State pursuant to New York"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: In June 2018 the Department issued Block a BitLicense permitting Virtual Currency Business Activity (VCBA) as defined in 23 NYCRR § 200.02(q). (p. 1)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In June 2018, the Department issued Block a BitLicense,"</sub>

  <sub>anchor: "Block owns and operates Cash App, a peer-to-peer money transmission"</sub>

  <sub>anchor: "the Department conducted two full-scope examinations of Block covering both its money transmitter"</sub>

## Money Transmitter Regulations

- **mandatory / requirement**: Under Section 417.2 of Part 417, money transmitter licensees must establish and maintain an AML program complying with all applicable federal AML laws. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "requires money transmitter licensees to establish and maintain an AML program that complies with all applicable Federal anti-money laundering laws"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Licensees must demonstrate risk-based policies, procedures and practices to ensure, to the maximum extent practicable, that transactions comply with OFAC requirements, and must comply with federal KYC requirements. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "demonstrate that it has in place riskbased policies, procedures and practices to ensure, to the maximum extent practicable"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Money transmitter licensees are subject to the consumer protection requirements in 3 NYCRR § 406. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Money transmitter licensees are further subject to the consumer protection requirements contained in 3 NYCRR § 406."</sub>

## The Virtual Currency Regulation

- **mandatory / requirement**: Companies conducting VCBA in New York must be licensed by the Department, either through a BitLicense or a Limited Purpose Trust Company Charter, and are subject to ongoing supervision. (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Companies that conduct VCBA in the State of New York must be licensed to do so by the Department"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: BitLicensees must develop and implement compliance policies and programs including a robust AML program (23 NYCRR § 200.15), a cybersecurity program (§ 200.16), and a comprehensive BCDR policy (§ 200.17). (p. 4)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "including a robust AML program, _see_ 23 NYCRR § 200.15, a cybersecurity program"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Licensees must ensure consumers are fully informed as to all aspects of the transactions they enter into (23 NYCRR § 200.19). (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "ensure that consumers are fully informed as to all aspects of the transactions they enter into"</sub>

## Events at Issue - Background

- **descriptive / context**: Block is a Delaware corporation offering financial services to businesses and consumers and is licensed by the Department for money transmission and VCBA in New York. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Block is a Delaware corporation that, through its various lines of business"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Department determined that Block failed to maintain a compliant and effective AML program and failed to comply with other critical requirements of the Money Transmitter and Virtual Currency Regulations. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "failed to maintain a compliant and effective AML program, as well as failed to comply"</sub>

## Deficiencies in Block’s Anti-Money Laundering Program

- **mandatory / requirement**: Section 417.2 requires each licensee to establish and maintain an AML program complying with applicable federal laws and regulations. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "requires that each licensee establish and maintain an AML program that complies with applicable federal laws and regulation"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Federal regulation requires money services businesses to develop an effective AML program reasonably designed to prevent use of the business to facilitate money laundering and terrorist financing (31 CFR § 1022.210(a)). (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "that is reasonably designed to prevent the money services business from being used to facilitate money laundering"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: BitLicensee AML programs must provide a system of internal controls, policies and procedures designed to ensure ongoing compliance with AML laws (23 NYCRR § 200.15(c)(1)). (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "provide for a system of internal controls, policies, and procedures designed to ensure ongoing compliance"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Virtual Currency Regulation requires licensees to monitor for transactions that might signify money laundering, tax evasion or other illegal activity (23 NYCRR § 200.15(e)(3)). (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "monitor for transactions that might signify money laundering, tax evasion, or other illegal activity"</sub>

## Events at Issue - Background

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Block's policies, procedures and processes did not keep pace with the significant growth it experienced, resulting in Block being unable to fully comply with its obligation to effectively monitor and report suspicious transactions on its platforms. (p. 5)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "did not keep pace with the significant growth the Company experienced"</sub>

## Deficiencies in Block’s Anti-Money Laundering Program

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Block's AML program, covering fiat and Bitcoin on Cash App, failed to adequately consider the substantial risks of an entity of its new size and complexity, shown by its alert backlog, delayed SAR filing, insufficient OFAC screenings and KYC/CDD shortcomings. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "failed to adequately consider the substantial risks posed to an entity of its new size and complexity"</sub>

## Backlog in SAR Filings and Transaction Monitoring Alerts

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Between 2018 and 2021 Block experienced a significant transaction monitoring alert backlog of about 18,000 alerts in 2018, growing to over 169,000 by 2020. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In 2018, Block had accumulated a transaction monitoring backlog of approximately 18,000 alerts, which grew to over 169,000 by 2020."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The backlog was caused partly by Block's inability to predict the impact of Cash App's customer growth on alert volumes and staffing, and by new transaction monitoring tools that increased alerts. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "This extensive backlog was caused, in part, by Block’s inability to predict the impact"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The alert backlog caused an unacceptable number of days between the last transaction date and SAR filing dates. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "caused an unacceptable number of days to elapse between the filed SARs’ last transaction date"</sub>
- **descriptive / requirement**: Block's procedures require analysts to review an alert and document a recommendation to file or not file a SAR within sixty days after the alert is generated. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "within sixty days after an alert is generated"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Between February 2021 and September 2022, Bitcoin and fiat SARs were at times filed over a year after alerts were generated; the average was 129 days from alert to SAR filing and 70 days from alert to case investigation start. (p. 6)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The average number of days between the date of the transaction monitoring alert and the SAR filing was 129 days."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Alert backlog and processing delays let potentially suspicious and illicit activity continue unaddressed on Block's platforms for an extended period. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "allowed potential suspicious, and illicit activity to continue on Block’s platforms unaddressed"</sub>

## Transaction Monitoring Failures

- **mandatory / requirement**: Under Part 417 and Part 200, licensees are required to implement risk-based policies and procedures to ensure compliance with OFAC requirements, which aim to ensure licensees do not transact with sanctioned persons. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "licensees are required to implement riskbased policies and procedures to ensure compliance with the requirements"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Section 417.2(c) and Section 200.15(i) require each licensee to implement risk-based policies, procedures and practices to ensure OFAC compliance. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "require that each licensee implement risk-based policies, procedures, and practices to ensure compliance with OFAC"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Block used two vendors to block and alert transactions with exposure to terrorism-associated wallets; for one vendor, alerts were generated only above 1% exposure and automatic blocking only above 10% exposure. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "did not generate alerts on Bitcoin transactions until the recipient’s wallet had more than 1% exposure"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Department stated that any funds sent to terrorism-connected wallets are illegal, and that alert thresholds above 0% without a supporting risk-based analysis fall short of the regulatory requirement. (p. 7)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "setting threshold alerts above 0% without a risk-based analysis supporting that decision"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Existing service providers and employees were not subject to subsequent screening against updated OFAC sanctions listings. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "existing service providers and employees were not subject to subsequent screening"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In 2021 Block reported fifteen rejected Bitcoin transactions late to OFAC during the examination period, in violation of 31 CFR § 501.604(c). (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In 2021, Block also reported fifteen rejected Bitcoin transactions late with OFAC"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Before 2023 Block did not conduct OFAC screening for restricted accounts, which transact in fiat and are subject to certain dollar-amount thresholds. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "prior to 2023, Block did not conduct OFAC screening for restricted accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: Block's monitoring and risk rating of transactions using anonymizing services ('mixers', which obscure source, destination or amount by combining users' assets) were deficient, contributing to failure to monitor Bitcoin transactions as required by Section 200.15(e)(3). (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "was Block’s deficient monitoring and risk rating of transactions that used anonymizing services"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Department's April 28, 2022 guidance identified mixers as a typology virtual currency licensees should be considering when evaluating transaction monitoring risk. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "identified mixers as a typology virtual currency licensees should be considering"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: The Department's guidance states that VC Entities should evidence appropriately tailored transaction monitoring coverage against applicable typologies and red flags, identify deviations from customers' intended purposes, and address other risk considerations as applicable. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is important that VC Entities evidence appropriately tailored transaction monitoring coverage"</sub>

## Deficiencies in Block's Anti-Money Laundering Program

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The Department states that the untraceable and anonymous features of mixers make them highly susceptible to abuse by criminal and sanctioned actors, who pose a threat to National Security. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "that pose a threat to National Security"</sub>

## Transaction Monitoring Failures

- **descriptive / typology or red flag**: The Department's April 28, 2022 guidance lists typologies for virtual currency business, including exposure to high-risk or sanctioned jurisdictions, processing through mixers or tumblers, darknet market transfers, scams or ransomware, and other illicit activity relevant to the business model. (p. 8)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Relevant typologies related to virtual currency business activity include but are not limited to"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Mixers allow criminal actors to buy illicit items on the darkweb, including drugs, contraband and child sex-abuse material, with little risk of detection, and can obscure the ultimate destination of funds, which may reach terrorist organizations or sanctioned parties. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "It allows a sender to obscure the ultimate destination of funds"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Block risk-rated transactions with exposure to mixers as 'medium' risk, rather than the 'high' risk rating the Department considered appropriate. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "risk rated transactions identified as having exposure to mixers as “medium” risk"</sub>

## KYC/CDD Deficiencies

- **expected / expectation**: A core tenet of an effective AML program is a risk-based and robust KYC/CDD policies and procedures. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "A core tenet of an effective AML program is the adoption of a risk based and robust KYC/CDD"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Money transmitter and Virtual Currency regulations require licensees to maintain effective processes and controls to identify and understand the nature and purpose of customer relationships to mitigate money laundering risks (3 NYCRR § 417.2(a)(1)(i)(A) and 23 NYCRR § 200.15(h)). (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "require licensees to maintain effective processes and controls to identify and understand the nature and purpose"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Block had no formal KYC refresh process to identify changes to customers' initial KYC information and apply them to update customer risk ratings. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Block did not have a formal KYC refresh process to identify"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Customers opened multiple accounts using different email addresses and phone numbers, bypassing transaction limits on certain accounts or individuals. (p. 9)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "customers opened multiple accounts using different email addresses and phone numbers"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Cash App restricted accounts may transact only in fiat up to a limit and do not require full identity verification; Block did not prohibit opening restricted accounts sharing attributes with SAR-denylisted customers, allowing bad actors to re-enter. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Block did not prohibit opening of restricted Cash App accounts that shared attributes"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Block imposed a $1,000 transaction limit per rolling 30-day period on each fiat-only restricted account sharing the same linked financial instrument, but without limiting account numbers this was not an effective control. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Block imposed a transaction limit of $1,000 in a rolling 30-day period"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: One SAR concerned $1.6 million with 91 subjects holding 16,811 accounts and 19,518 transactions. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a SAR was filed for $1.6 million with 91 subjects that were holders of 16,811 accounts"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In a 2022 internal investigation Block self-identified over 8,000 accounts linked to a Russian criminal network, and took action including filing SARs, closing and denylisting accounts and implementing new controls. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Block self-identified over 8,000 accounts linked to a Russian criminal network"</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: Approximately 25-30 subjects in the Russian criminal network opened 8,359 Cash App accounts using falsified information and autogenerated email addresses and phone numbers before detection. (p. 10)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "were able to open 8,359 Cash App accounts using falsified information"</sub>

## Cybersecurity Deficiencies

- **descriptive / context**: Cash App operates in an entirely virtual environment and collects non-public information for about 54 million monthly transacting active accounts (estimate as of June 2023). (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "collects non-public information (“NPI”) for each of its approximately 54 million"</sub>
- **expected / requirement**: A robust cybersecurity program is critical to protect Block's information systems and consumer NPI; management oversight and sufficient, robust cybersecurity policies are critical components of the requirements in the Virtual Currency Regulation and Part 500. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "it is critical that Block maintain a robust cybersecurity program"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Block's Information Security Policy and other cybersecurity policies were not subject to annual board review and approval as required by 23 NYCRR § 200.16(b) until October 26, 2023; review was delegated to the CISO. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "were not subject to annual board review and approval, as required by 23 NYCRR § 200.16(b) until October 26, 2023"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Section 200.16(b) requires the cybersecurity policy be reviewed and approved by the licensee's board or equivalent governing body at least annually. (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "be reviewed and approved by the Licensee’s board of directors or equivalent governing body at least annually"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Block's cybersecurity policy failed to address capacity and performance planning as required by 23 NYCRR § 200.16(b)(5). (p. 11)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "failed to address capacity and performance planning as required by 23 NYCRR § 200.16(b)(5)"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Block's BCDR plan was narrow, addressing only its pandemic response plan and one additional scenario, and was not a companywide plan complying with the regulation. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Block’s BCDR plan was narrow, addressing only the Company’s pandemic response plan"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Section 200.17(a) requires a BCDR plan to address documents, data, facilities, infrastructure, personnel and competencies essential to continued operation, backup of essential documents and data with offsite storage, and identification of necessary third parties. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "requires that a licensee’s BCDR plan address the documents, data, facilities, infrastructure, personnel, and competencies"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Block tested its BCDR plan annually, but the Department found no evidence that tests were observed by qualified internal personnel or qualified third parties, in violation of 23 NYCRR § 200.17(e). (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Department found no evidence that the tests were observed by qualified internal personnel"</sub>

## Consumer Protection Deficiencies

- **descriptive / finding or result**: The Department found that Block failed to present virtual currency disclosures required under 23 NYCRR § 200.19(a) in clear, conspicuous and legible writing, although the disclosures were present; they were spread across pages of the Cash App Terms of Service. (p. 12)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Block failed to present those disclosures to the consumer in “clear, conspicuous, and legible writing.”"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: For fiat transactions from stored balances, receipts must contain the company's refund policy and a statement of liability for non-delivery or delayed delivery (3 NYCRR § 406.3(f)); Block did not provide them. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Block failed to provide receipts containing the Company’s refund policy"</sub>

## Violations of Law and Regulations

- **descriptive / finding or result**: Block failed to maintain an effective and compliant AML program, in violation of 23 NYCRR § 200.15 and 3 NYCRR § 417.2. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Block failed to maintain an effective and compliant anti-money laundering"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Block failed to obtain annual board approval of its cybersecurity policy, in violation of 23 NYCRR § 200.16(b). (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Block failed to obtain annual approval of its cybersecurity policy"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Block failed to maintain an adequate BCDR policy and ensure independent testing, in violation of 23 NYCRR § 200.17. (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Block failed to maintain an adequate business continuity and disaster recovery"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Block failed to provide required risk disclosures for virtual currency transactions in a clear and conspicuous manner, in violation of 23 NYCRR § 200.19(a). (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Block failed to provide required disclosures of the risks of virtual currency"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Block failed to provide required disclosures on receipts for money transmission transactions, in violation of 3 NYCRR § 406.3(f). (p. 13)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Block failed to provide required disclosures on receipts for money transmission transactions"</sub>

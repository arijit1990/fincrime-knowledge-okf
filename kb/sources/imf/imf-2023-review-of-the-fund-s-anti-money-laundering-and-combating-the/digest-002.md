  <sub>anchor: "TF includes the raising and processing of funds to supply terrorists with resources"</sub>

  <sub>anchor: "PF measures are related to United Nations Security Council Resolutions (UNSCRs) applying targeted financial sanctions (TFS)"</sub>

## FINANCIAL INTEGRITY RISKS AND COVERAGE OF AML/CFT IN FUND WORKSTREAMS

- **descriptive / method**: Staff's understanding of the nature and severity of financial integrity risks supports the extent of AML/CFT coverage in surveillance, guides design of financial integrity conditionality in Fund-supported programs, guides scope and depth in FSAPs, informs scoping of assessment exercises, and focuses CD on areas of greater risk. (p. 16)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "guides CD engagements to focus on areas of greater financial integrity risks"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Staff's risk understanding draws on quantitative and qualitative data: staff knowledge from workstreams; published MERs and follow-up reports, NRAs and thematic risk assessments including predicate crime data; and data on cross-border flows, financial stability and interconnectedness from the Fund, World Bank, UN and OECD. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "published AML/CFT mutual evaluation reports (MERs) and follow-up reports produced by the FATF Network"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: Since 2018 staff analyzes unusual cross-border financial flow patterns, using financial flows, trade and investment data and ML/TF risk indicators, to assess ML/TF risks tied to wire transfers and non-resident activity and to focus FSAPs and AIV consultations on countries needing attention. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Staff analyzes unusual cross-border financial flows patterns to identify and assess ML/TF risks"</sub>

## Box 1. The Macroeconomic Impact of ML/TF and IFF

- **descriptive / definition**: ML involves cleaning illegal assets (proceeds of crime), whereas TF can involve raising and processing both legitimate and illegal funds for terrorist activity. (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "TF, on the other hand, can involve the raising and processing of both legitimate and illegal funds"</sub>
- **expected / method**: ML and TF should be assessed separately; ML or TF risk is assessed as the product of threats, vulnerabilities and consequences, together with their macroeconomic impact (fiscal, financial, structural channels). (p. 17)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "ML and TF should therefore be assessed separately."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: ML threats stem from proceeds of all domestic crime and cross-border illicit funds laundered in or through the jurisdiction; TF threats are the scale of funds raised for terrorists needing processing, overlapping with ML threats only when those funds arise from criminal activity. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "TF threats thus only overlap with ML threats when the funds/assets raised or processed"</sub>
- **descriptive / data or feature**: Vulnerability indicators differ: a large politically exposed person client base in financial institutions is an ML vulnerability indicator (laundering of corruption proceeds), while exposure to customers from high-terrorism-risk jurisdictions is a TF vulnerability indicator. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "exposure of financial institutions to customers from jurisdictions with high-risk of terrorism is a vulnerability indicator for TF"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: ML can threaten financial stability, growth and prosperity via destabilizing flows and obstructed access to global markets and FDI; in origin countries it causes illicit outflows, in transit countries hot money may cause bank liquidity and solvency issues, and in international financial centers it can cause economic capture, asset price bubbles and reputational risks. (p. 18)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "destabilizing effects of hot money resulting from IFF, which may cause macro-critical bank liquidity and solvency issues"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: TF supports terrorist attacks and causes human, social, economic and political costs, and is often macro-relevant for FCS or states where terrorist groups derive income from control over natural resources. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "TF is often macro-relevant for FCS or states where terrorist groups derive income"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: LICs and FCS typically experience illicit outflows of proceeds of crimes such as corruption and organized crime (resource-rich ones most heavily), while international financial centers attract illicit inflows. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Low-income countries (LIC) and FCS typically experience illicit outflows of proceeds from crimes"</sub>
- **descriptive / limitation**: Identifying and mitigating TF-related IFF is less developed than for ML-related IFF because TF flows often use informal channels with lower values, whereas ML-related IFF often use formal channels with higher values, enabling data analytics for detection. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The ability to identify and mitigate TF-related IFF is less well developed than it is for ML-related IFF"</sub>

- **descriptive / context**: Building robust AML/CFT systems requires sustained efforts and resources customized to a country's risk profile; staff leverages synergies across surveillance, lending, FSAPs, assessments and CD to identify risks and tailor reform priorities and capacity building. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Building robust and effective AML/CFT systems is a complex task that requires sustained efforts"</sub>

## Box 2. Safeguarding Financial Integrity of Digital Finance

- **descriptive / context**: Staff's financial integrity work on digital money has grown, contributed to the Fund paper on Elements of Effective Policies for Crypto Assets, and produced two Fintech Notes on VAs and AML/CFT to help countries implement FATF Standards on VAs and VASPs; staff also analyzed CBDC integrity implications. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "staff has published two Fintech Notes on VAs and AML/CFT."</sub>
- **descriptive / case example**: In surveillance staff advised on VA and CBDC risks, including against granting legal tender status to VAs (El Salvador) and on CBDCs (China, Bahamas); FSAPs analyzed VA/VASP frameworks (Singapore, France); and under the Central African Republic's ECF program the Fund advised repealing legal provisions giving VAs legal tender status. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "to repeal the legal provisions that provided VAs legal tender status"</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: The FATF defines a VA as a digital representation of value that can be digitally traded or transferred and used for payment or investment; it excludes digital representations of fiat currencies, securities and other financial assets already covered by the FATF Recommendations. (p. 19)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "a digital representation of value that can be digitally traded, or transferred"</sub>

## B. Fund Surveillance

- **mandatory / requirement**: Under the 2012 Integrated Surveillance Decision, financial integrity issues are covered on a mandatory basis in AIV consultations when they significantly influence present or prospective balance of payments and domestic stability, or spillovers that may significantly influence the international monetary system (macro-criticality standard); assessed case by case and evenhandedly, and voluntarily when the member requests. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "financial integrity issues are covered on a mandatory basis when considered macro-critical"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: When financial integrity issues are discussed voluntarily at the member's request and included in the AIV staff report, the advice constitutes technical assistance by staff despite its inclusion in the report. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "this advice constitutes technical assistance (TA) by staff notwithstanding its inclusion"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Fund has endeavored to cover these issues since 2001; in 2011 the Executive Board endorsed mandatory coverage of financial integrity issues in surveillance when deemed appropriate, and a 2012 Staff Guidance Note provided guiding questions and criteria for deciding when to cover them. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Executive Board endorsed the mandatory coverage of financial integrity issues in surveillance"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The Fund's policy advice on financial integrity issues given on a voluntary basis is technical assistance under Article V, Section 2(b) and not surveillance, although it can be included in the member's AIV consultation report. (p. 20)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The Fund’s policy advice on these issues would be TA under Article V, Section 2(b)"</sub>
- **descriptive / method**: The guidance criteria assess the impact of integrity issues on financial system or economy stability, threats to financial sector stability from predicate crimes, adverse effects on the broader economy, and spillovers and global stability impact. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the threats to financial sector stability arising from predicate crimes"</sub>
- **mandatory / requirement**: Under the 2018 Governance Framework, AML/CFT is one of six state functions to be assessed for corruption and governance vulnerabilities on a mandatory basis whenever macro-criticality can be established, prioritizing AML elements such as international cooperation, asset recovery and PEP preventive measures; members can also agree to voluntary assessment of AML efforts against transnational corruption. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "AML/CFT is one of the six state functions identified as most relevant for economic activity"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Facilitation issues are covered in volunteer countries as of September 2023: Austria, Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, Czech Republic, Saudi Arabia, Switzerland, the Netherlands, Norway, the United Kingdom and the United States. Corruption could also undermine AML/CFT effectiveness and staff will deepen coverage of those structural issues. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "corruption could also undermine the effectiveness of the AML/CFT framework"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Since 2010 FSAP stability assessments are a mandatory part of AIV surveillance for jurisdictions with systemically important financial sectors; FSAPs and AIV coverage are complementary, and staff AML/CFT input is a key FSAP information source. (p. 21)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the Executive Board made the FSAP’s financial sector stability assessments a mandatory part of surveillance"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Coverage of financial integrity issues in AIV staff reports rose from 60-70 percent before 2018 to over 90 percent since; the 2020 drop in numbers reflected COVID streamlining, though percentage coverage stayed high. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "from 60-70 percent of all AIV staff reports before 2018 to over 90 percent since that year"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: In 2022 coverage was full in all AFR, MCD and WHD AIV staff reports, 95 percent in the European department and 94 percent in APD; scope and depth vary with the nature and severity of issues. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "there was full coverage of financial integrity issues in all African (AFR)"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Since 2016 several countries face pressure on correspondent banking relationships (CBRs), with global institutions citing perceived ML/TF risks and AML weaknesses as reasons for restricting or terminating CBRs; more members have been publicly identified by the FATF grey list, and staff gave targeted policy advice to exit it where macro-critical. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "perceptions of ML/TF risks and perceived weaknesses in AML frameworks as one of the reasons"</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Voluntary coverage of "facilitation" of transnational corruption under the 2018 Governance Framework has led to discussion of AML to tackle foreign proceeds of corruption in many advanced economies. (p. 22)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "resulted in the discussion of AML to tackle foreign proceeds of corruption in many advanced economies"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Traditional issues in AIV consultations include risk understanding, risk-based AML/CFT supervision of financial institutions and non-financial sectors (lawyers, accountants, TCSPs), and strengthening legal and institutional frameworks; emerging issues since 2018 include beneficial ownership, crypto assets, and citizenship by investment (CBI). (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "These issues include beneficial ownership, crypto assets, and citizenship by investment (CBI)."</sub>
- **descriptive / finding or result**: Staff followed up on emergency-lending commitments to publish beneficial ownership information of companies awarded procurement contracts, advised countries to closely monitor ML/TF risks of crypto assets, and increased attention to AML tackling corruption proceeds and tax evasion; CBI programs have come under scrutiny. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "publish beneficial ownership information of companies awarded procurement contracts"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Post-pandemic hybrid engagement lets staff join financial integrity discussions with authorities directly and more regularly, saving travel costs and increasing work pressure but yielding more targeted, in-depth discussions, whereas before staff gave background notes and questionnaires. (p. 23)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the hybrid modality of engagement with country authorities has afforded flexibility"</sub>

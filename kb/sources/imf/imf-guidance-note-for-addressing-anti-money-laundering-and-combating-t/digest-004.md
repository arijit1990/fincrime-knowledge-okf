## Box 7. The Difference in Coverage of AML/CFT Policies in FSAPs and Comprehensive AML/CFT Assessments

- **descriptive / context**: The FSAP AML/CFT workstream and the FATF mutual evaluation process are complementary rather than duplicative; the FATF process is an in-depth peer review of the robustness and effectiveness of countries' measures to combat ML/TF/PF. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The FATF’s global network mutual evaluation process aims to safeguard financial integrity through in-depth peer-reviews"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The scope of FSAPs is determined by the Fund and focuses on a country's financial sector policies, with a targeted analysis of AML/CFT issues relevant to the financial sector embedded in the comprehensive analysis of financial sector resilience. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "In comparison, the scope of FSAPs is determined by the Fund1/ and focuses on a country’s financial sector policies."</sub>
- **descriptive / definition**: FSAP AML/CFT coverage is narrower than a comprehensive mutual evaluation and focuses on selected issues relevant to financial stability, such as threats from illegal proceeds in the banking sector or other high-risk sectors, vulnerabilities of banks in preventing laundering, and the potential impact on financial stability. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FSAP is focused on selected AML/CFT issues relevant to financial stability."</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The FSAP AML/CFT workstream aims to analyse three things: domestic and cross-border illegal proceeds transiting or integrated in the banking sector or other high-risk sectors; vulnerabilities of banks and covered institutions and gaps in AML/CFT supervision; and the potential impact of these threats and vulnerabilities on financial stability. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FSAP AML/CFT workstream aims to analyze (i) the threat of domestic and cross-border illegal proceeds"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: FSAPs look at FATF-standard issues such as preventive measures by financial institutions and risk-based AML/CFT supervision, and also at topics beyond the FATF standards, such as coordination between prudential and AML/CFT supervision, financial stability implications of AML/CFT failings, and cross-border risk understanding. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "the FSAP looks at issues covered under the FATF standards (for instance, the application of preventive measures"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: An FSAP may also cover designated non-financial sectors based on their linkages with the financial sector, such as illicit fund inflows into real estate contributing to asset bubbles; broader framework aspects such as beneficial ownership transparency may be covered where relevant to the financial sector. (p. 27)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "While the focus is on the financial sector, an FSAP may also cover designated non-financial sectors"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The depth of FSAP review is tailored to the country's ML/TF risk exposure; staff has significant flexibility to vary depth of AML/CFT coverage, and for higher-risk jurisdictions the FSAP can dive deeper into financial sector issues than FATF assessments, including cross-border risks and spillovers where warranted. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "For higher risks jurisdictions, the narrow scope of the FSAP’s AML/CFT workstream allows a deeper dive into financial sector issues"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: The AML/CFT coverage in an FSAP is a data-driven exercise feeding into the wider analysis of financial sector and macroeconomic stability, unlike FATF assessments that uniformly cover all effectiveness pillars. (p. 28)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "The AML/CFT coverage in an FSAP is a data-driven exercise that feeds into the wider analysis"</sub>

## Types of AML/CFT-Related Conditionality

- **descriptive / context**: AML/CFT-related conditionality supports program objectives such as facilitating members' access to the international financial system (for example via correspondent banking) and leveraging the AML system to tackle corruption; it is grouped into three categories: legal and regulatory framework, institutional reforms, and effectiveness of the framework. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Generally, the types of measures can be grouped into three main categories"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Staff should propose measures whose implementation timeframes generally correspond to the duration of the Fund-supported program, with area department and LEG coordinating on expected timeframes. (p. 31)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Staff should propose measures whose implementation timeframes generally correspond"</sub>

## Box 8. Illustrative Types of AML/CFT-Related Conditionality

- **permissive / context**: Legal and regulatory measures could include enacting new AML/CFT legislation in line with FATF standards, adopting by-laws to implement UNSCRs on terrorism financing, or implementing preventive measures such as CDD and suspicious transaction reporting in specific sectors.   <sub>anchor: "Enacting new AML/CFT legislation to bring the national legal framework in line with the FATF standards."</sub>

## Depth of Structural Conditionality

  <sub>anchor: "staff should also consider whether proposed conditionality has adequate depth"</sub>
- **expected / expectation**: Staff should analyse the structural and lasting impact an AML/CFT measure is intended to achieve and the extent of stakeholder buy-in required; the ultimate judgement of depth rests with staff and management. (p. 33)[^original]  
  <sub>anchor: "Staff should therefore analyze the structural and lasting impact a given AML/CFT measure is intended to achieve"</sub>
- **descriptive / context**: Depth is very important in designing effective conditionality for fragile and conflict-affected states and low-capacity members, where ML/TF risks with resource constraints and weak institutional capacity can affect reform implementation. (p. 33)[^original]
